**Findings**

Recent meta-analyses and systematic reviews (2024–2025) provide the most authoritative evidence on the effectiveness of generative AI (GenAI) tools for improving mathematics achievement among middle school students ([6] Ma & Zhong, 2025; [1] Walkington, 2025; [11] Issues in Information Systems, 2025; [10] Frontiers in Education, 2025). These reviews consistently evaluate comparative and experimental studies across a wide variety of GenAI tools—such as ChatGPT, adaptive intelligent tutors, AI-driven assessment generators, and platforms like ALEKS and Smart Sparrow.

Performance outcomes studied include standardized test scores, course grades, and task completion rates. Research predominantly utilizes quasi-experimental designs, with some randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and frequently reports performance gains ranging from 15% to 35% compared to traditional instruction, although the effect size varies by tool, educational setting, and study quality ([11], [6]).

Settings: 
- The strongest benefits are observed when GenAI tools are integrated into classroom instruction under teacher supervision and aligned with the curriculum ([3], [8], [11]).
- Tools used exclusively for homework or independent study yield more variable and sometimes weaker results ([10]).
- Teacher involvement in AI deployment is repeatedly emphasized as critical for maximizing effectiveness and addressing risks such as misinformation and over-reliance ([3], [7]).

Types of tools:
- ChatGPT and similar large language models are the most studied, widely used for generating explanations, practice questions, and study prompts ([10]).
- Adaptive platforms (e.g., ALEKS, Smart Sparrow) that personalize learning and assessment are reported to yield greater performance gains than generic question-generation tools ([11]).
- Automated AI-generated multiple-choice question (MCQ) tools are pervasive but carry significant validity risks: “Results showed that 80% of items violated at least one guideline, with 73.7% likely to cause major measurement error… only 20% acceptable as generated” ([7]). Review and revision by educators is necessary before use in high-stakes assessment ([7]).

Comparative effectiveness:
- Direct head-to-head comparisons between GenAI tools are rare ([10], [11]), but overall heterogeneity in impact appears more related to implementation quality and context than to the specific platform ([11]).
- Quasi-experimental protocols dominate, with limited, though increasing, use of RCTs for adaptive and tutoring platforms ([4], [6]).

Subgroup findings:
- There is limited evidence disaggregating effects by student subgroups (e.g., SES, race/ethnicity, ELL), with calls in most reviews for further research in this area ([10], [11]).

Cautions and limitations:
- Reliable gains in mathematics achievement depend on educator oversight, ongoing tool review, and curricular alignment ([3], [10], [7]).
- “Most GenAI tools developed for educational uses lack pedagogical expertise involvement.” ([7])
- Rapid AI adoption outpaces evidence of longitudinal, sustained achievement effects ([11]).

The abstract highlights an increase in studies on chatbots in education and a synthesis of experimental findings, but does not specify effects by content area, such as mathematics, or by grade level (e.g., high school specifically) [1].

This study examines the effect of ChatGPT—a highly salient GenAI tool—on student learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking. It represents the largest and most up-to-date quantitative synthesis available directly on generative AI (specifically ChatGPT) and covers a variety of educational levels and contexts. While broad in coverage, findings are not distinguished by mathematics versus other disciplines, or by high school versus other grade levels in the abstract [2].
