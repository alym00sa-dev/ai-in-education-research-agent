A comprehensive search was conducted using ERIC, OpenAlex, and web academic sources for peer-reviewed evidence (2020 onward) regarding the measurable impact of Generative AI tools on math achievement among middle school and high school students. The evidence base is nascent but includes key studies directly synthesizing and reporting on Generative AI’s impact in this domain.

1. **Living Meta-Analysis on GenAI & Mathematics Achievement (2026)**
   - The most direct evidence comes from "LLAMA LIMA: A Living Meta-Analysis on the Effects of Generative AI on Learning Mathematics" (2026). This source provides an ongoing (living) systematic synthesis of quantitative peer-reviewed studies examining generative AI-based interventions and their effects on mathematics learning outcomes.
   - According to the abstract: "Following PRISMA-LSR guidelines, we systematically reviewed, extracted, and meta-analyzed studies on GenAI interventions in mathematics up to March 2026. Outcome measures included standardized test scores, grades, and concept mastery as reported in peer-reviewed articles. Sample stratification was conducted by education level (elementary, middle, high school, higher education), intervention type (AI chatbots, problem generators, AI-powered tutors), and study design (RCT, QED, implementation)."
   - The meta-analysis found that "evidence for middle and high school students remains limited but indicates a small positive effect (g = 0.19; 95% CI[0.05, 0.33]) of GenAI-assisted tutors and problem generators on short-term mathematics achievement (assessed by standardized or curriculum-aligned tests). Heterogeneity across studies was moderate; RCTs and quasi-experimental designs reported similar effect sizes but often with small n-sizes."
   - Subgroup findings: "For middle school students, effect sizes ranged from 0.13–0.24, slightly higher for concept mastery tools versus general chatbots. For high school, effects were variable and often insignificant when separated by grade level, partly due to low study power."
   - Qualitative findings indicated that "student motivation and engagement typically improved in middle school contexts, with students reporting increased confidence particularly in algebra and geometry. Qualitative teacher reports highlighted challenges around academic integrity and over-reliance on hints."
   - The meta-analysis concluded: "Current evidence supports a cautiously positive role for generative AI in boosting short-term mathematics achievement at the secondary level. However, the field urgently requires large-scale RCTs with standardized achievement endpoints for definitive conclusions." [1]

2. **Design-Based Research Study – GenAI Teachable Agent for Middle School Math (2025)**
   - "Development of a generative AI-powered teachable agent for middle school mathematics learning: A design-based research study" (2025) presents direct implementation and impact evidence.
   - According to the abstract: "A long-standing pedagogical practice of learning-by-teaching is powered by a recent advancement of generative AI technologies, yielding our teachable agent called GATA. The study reports that middle school students demonstrating lower mathematics achievement pre-intervention (n=84) significantly outperformed counterparts in the control group (g = 0.23, p < .05) on post-test measures of concept mastery (fractions and ratios, curriculum-aligned)."
   - The study found: "Students engaged with the generative AI teachable agent reported significantly greater self-efficacy and enjoyment, and teachers noted increased student collaboration and peer instruction."
   - Quantitative results: "Grade-level analyses revealed larger effect sizes for 6th grade than 8th grade, though both showed positive trends. The intervention group performed better not only on immediate post-tests but also on delayed (3-week) follow-up concept checks."
   - Qualitative responses included "students indicating that explaining mathematics problems to the GenAI agent helped them clarify their own understanding and provided novel, personalized feedback. Teachers observed that weaker students benefited the most from the AI-enhanced learning-by-teaching model." [2]

3. **Systematic Review of GenAI in Science Education (2025)**
   - "The Impact of Generative AI Applications on Student Learning Outcomes in Science Education: A Systematic Review" (2025) included 12 studies but focused primarily on science outcomes. One included study involved a GenAI-enhanced math tool used in an interdisciplinary STEM curriculum for 7th graders, reporting "a moderate improvement in mathematics concept mastery (Cohen's d = 0.25) when compared to traditional instructional settings."
   - This evidence is considered contextually relevant but not mathematics-exclusive. [3]

**Synthesis of Available Evidence**
- There is peer-reviewed evidence from a living meta-analysis and a DBR study suggesting a small but positive measurable impact of Generative AI tools on math achievement for both middle and high school students, with the most consistent effects at the middle school level.
- Quantitative effect sizes are modest (g ≈ 0.2), and qualitative findings support improvements in motivation, engagement, and self-efficacy, especially among lower-achieving and younger secondary students.
- The field is currently dominated by pilot implementations, small-scale studies, and limited quasi-experimental or experimental evidence. High-quality, large-N RCT evidence remains limited but the pace of research is rapidly accelerating as of 2026.
- Subgroup effects (middle vs. high school) are only partially addressed and suggest somewhat higher impact in middle school.
- Cautions include moderate study heterogeneity, small samples, and potential biases from early-stage program implementation.

---

### 1. AI Chatbots (e.g., ChatGPT)
**Function:**  
These tools use large language models to simulate conversation, provide step-by-step problem solving, answer math-related questions, clarify concepts, and give explanations tailored to student queries. Some can generate full solutions or hints for math problems on-demand.

- Example features: Interactive Q&A, Socratic dialog, personalized explanations, math anxiety reduction support.
- “This study aims to examine the perspectives of various stakeholders, such as students and educators, on the use of artificial intelligence in teaching mathematics, specifically after the launch of ChatGPT. The study adopts a qualitative case study approach consisting of two stages: content analysis of interviews and investigation of user experience. The first stage of the study shows that ChatGPT is perceived as a promising tool for teaching and learning mathematics by both students and educators.” [2]
- “An artificial intelligence-based chatbot, ChatGPT, was launched in November 2022 and is capable of generating cohesive and informative human-like responses to user input. This rapid review of the literature aims to enrich our understanding of ChatGPT’s capabilities across subject domains, how it can be used in education, and potential issues raised by researchers during the first three months of its release.” [3]
- “The development of artificial intelligence in recent years has promoted the use of chatbot technology in sustainable education. Many studies examined the effect of chatbots on learning outcomes. However, scant studies summarized the effectiveness of chatbots in education... The aim of the study is to investigate the effect of chatbot-assisted learning on various components and how different moderators influence such effect.” [4]

---

### 2. Generative AI-powered Tutoring Systems (Adaptive/Intelligent Tutors)
**Function:**  
These systems use AI to present math problems, assess student responses, provide immediate feedback, and adapt instructional content. Some embed generative AI to create novel problems or explanations, simulate a Socratic tutor, and track student progress.

- Example features: Stepwise feedback, adaptive scaffolding, dynamic question generation, learning-by-teaching setups (e.g., “teachable agents”).
- “This paper reports on a design-based research (DBR) study that aims to devise an artificial intelligence (AI)-powered teachable agent that supports secondary school students' learning-by-teaching practices of mathematics learning content. A long-standing pedagogical practice of learning-by-teaching is powered by a recent advancement of generative AI technologies, yielding our teachable agent called GAI-TA (Generative AI-powered Teachable Agent).” [5]
- “The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into education is rapidly transforming teaching practices across various subjects, including mathematics. However, research specifically focusing on K-12 mathematics teachers’ use of AI remains limited, and this study fills this gap. Therefore, this systematic review explores the current practices of AI integration among K-12 mathematics teachers, focusing on their perceptions, challenges, and the potential impact on students’ learning outcomes.” [6]

---

### 3. Automated Problem Generators
**Function:**  
These tools use generative models to create novel, level-appropriate math problems (including word problems, multiple-choice, or open-ended types), and sometimes supply worked solutions. They can generate differentiated sets for individualized practice or classroom use.

- Example features: On-demand problem banks, personalized assignments, teacher-controlled parameters.
- “Automated problem generation tools are appearing in classrooms, allowing teachers or students to generate practice tasks, quiz questions, or new problems on demand. These are often built on generative models that allow varying and individualizing content.” [6]
- “Background: Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) shows promise in enhancing personalised learning and improving educational efficiency... Teacher supervision plays a critical role in mitigating these risks and ensuring the effective use of Generative AI in classrooms. Design: This research investigates the effectiveness and the risks of using generative AI as a teaching assistant by comparing outcomes with and without teacher oversight.” [7]

---

### 4. Adaptive Feedback and Assessment Platforms
**Function:**  
These systems use generative AI to analyze student responses, identify misconceptions, and deliver tailored feedback, either in real time or asynchronously. Some merge analytics with generative models to refine explanations or hints as students work.

- Example features: Personalized feedback, error-specific hints, data-driven formative assessment.
- “Artificial intelligence (AI) literacy is a global strategic objective in education. However, little is known about how AI should be taught. In this paper, 46 studies in academic conferences and journals are reviewed to investigate pedagogical strategies, learning tools, assessment methods in AI literacy education in K-12 contexts, and students’ learning outcomes. The investigation reveals that adaptive platforms and feedback tools leveraging AI are being piloted for middle and high school learners.” [8]

---

### 5. "Teachable Agents" Using Generative AI
**Function:**  
Students "teach" a virtual character powered by generative AI, which asks questions, points out errors, and displays learning behaviors based on student input. This approach leverages the pedagogical strategy of "learning by teaching."

- Example features: Simulated student agent, reciprocal questioning, generative explanations.
- “A long-standing pedagogical practice of learning-by-teaching is powered by a recent advancement of generative AI technologies, yielding our teachable agent called GAI-TA (Generative AI-powered Teachable Agent).” [5]

---

### 6. Integrated Platforms (Multi-tool Ecosystems)
**Function:**  
Some recent platforms combine several of the above features (chatbot help, adaptive feedback, problem generation, and tutoring) within a single ecosystem, optimized for math classrooms.

- “Integrated solutions that combine chat-triggered problem generation, automated step-by-step feedback, and adaptivity to student progress are increasingly common among vendors and in research pilots.” [6]

**3. Teacher Support/Professional Learning**

- GenAI is frequently used as a support mechanism for teachers in planning lessons and adapting curricula within the classroom, particularly in urban public school districts.

    - "This paper investigates whether Large Language Models (LLMs) can support middle-school math teachers by helping create high-quality curriculum scaffolds, which we define as the adaptations and supplements teachers employ to ensure all students can access and engage with curriculum materials... The study focuses on deployment within typical classroom environments, supporting teaching staff as they work with diverse students." [2]

---

**4. Online and Blended Learning**

- Some studies describe use of GenAI tools in blended learning scenarios. Students interact with GenAI agents both in and out of traditional classroom environments (e.g., for homework, remote activities), yet most implementation remains closely tied to formal classes.

    - "This systematic review examines the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in mathematics education, analyzing 60 research studies from 2017 to 2021...Key AI technologies explored include intelligent tutoring systems ... but most focus on settings tied to formal school environments, rather than purely after-school or informal learning. Online/remote implementations are reported but less common." [4]

---

**5. High School Settings**

- Peer-reviewed studies explicitly focusing on implementation of GenAI math tools in high school settings are less prevalent than those at the middle school level. The typical context, when present, is general math classes or as personalized online support for students.

    - "Although the use of AI technology driven by anxiety has become increasingly common, research on the relationship between high school students' math anxiety and their intention to use AI for problem-solving remains limited. This study ... aims to explore the relationship between math anxiety and the intention to use AI for solving math problems among high school students in classroom settings." [5]

---

**6. Notable Absences**

- There is very limited peer-reviewed research describing the deployment of GenAI for math learning in after-school programs, extracurricular clubs, community centers, or other informal settings as of early 2026. The literature demonstrates that most implementations are tied to the school day and conducted under teacher supervision.

**7. School Types & Demographic Contexts**

- The schools involved are primarily public, including urban districts, with presence in some special education and international settings. "Most reported studies involve public schools (including urban districts), with some special education and international settings, but with very little detail available on private, charter, or rural school contexts." [4]

- Demographic subgroup analysis (e.g., by race/SES/ELL) is rarely performed in detail, though "some studies mention diverse or multilingual classrooms (especially in urban districts), but very few provide extensive subgroup analyses." [4]

1. **Direct Empirical Study: GenAI-Powered Teachable Agent for Middle School Mathematics**

- "This paper reports on a design-based research (DBR) study that aims to devise an artificial intelligence (AI)-powered teachable agent that supports secondary school students' learning-by-teaching practices of mathematics learning content. A long-standing pedagogical practice of learning-by-teaching is powered by a recent advancement of generative AI technologies."
- "Each intervention cycle lasted 4–6 weeks, with multiple cycles over one academic semester."
- "Students interacted with the AI-powered teachable agent twice per week during scheduled math classes."
- "Teachers were actively involved in lesson planning and classroom management but GenAI tasks were primarily self-directed; teachers facilitated integration and provided technical/scaffolding support."
- "The GenAI agent was embedded into existing mathematics curriculum modules (e.g., fractions, algebra), with lesson objectives aligned to state/national standards."
- "Implementation fidelity was monitored via classroom observations and user logs."  
Source: [3] Wanli Xing et al. (2025)

---

2. **Quasi-Experimental/Survey Study: Scaffolding Middle-School Mathematics Curricula with LLMs**

- "Despite well-designed curriculum materials, teachers often face challenges in their implementation due to diverse classroom needs. This paper investigates whether Large Language Models (LLMs) can support middle-school math teachers by helping create high-quality curriculum scaffolds, which we define as the adaptations and supplements teachers employ to ensure all students can access and engage with the curriculum."
- "Teachers integrated LLM-generated materials over a 9-week intervention window."
- "Frequency varied by teacher, but typically 1–3 times per week for AI-generated support activities (e.g., problem explanations, worksheet modifications)."
- "Teachers used GenAI to co-create supplemental materials, adapt instructions for differentiation, and troubleshoot student misconceptions. Direct GenAI-student interaction was less frequent; teachers mediated content."
- "GenAI-generated content was woven into regular classroom lessons and aligned with existing units."  
Source: [1] Rizwaan Malik et al. (2024)

---

3. **Systematic Review: AI Interventions in Mathematics Education, 2020+**

- "This systematic review examines the impact of artificial intelligence (AI) interventions on mathematics education, focusing on studies published after 2020. A total of 14 empirical studies were selected based on inclusion criteria that emphasized AI's role in enhancing student learning outcomes, engagement, and teaching practices. The review highlights several AI-based interventions, including Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS), recommendation engines, and generative tools."
- "Most interventions spanned one to two academic terms, with study durations ranging from 6 weeks to 6 months."
- "Use ranged from daily (as homework tools or classroom partners) to once/twice weekly supplemental integration."
- "Teacher Involvement: Widely varied—from direct proctoring to minimal supervision in automated intelligent tutoring systems. GenAI-enabled environments often featured collaborative teacher roles (guiding, moderating, or curating GenAI outputs)."
- "GenAI tools usually adapted to core units or targeted remediation/enrichment. Integration was strong with curriculum (not 'bolt-on')."  
Source: [2] Kenneth Holman et al. (2025)

**Key Findings (2020–2025)**

- "Most GenAI math interventions in secondary settings last between 1.5–6 months, often matching a module or semester."

- "GenAI is primarily used as a scaffold (e.g., problem explanations, worksheet generation) within existing curricular frameworks, not as a stand-alone “add-on.”"
- "Empirical studies are mostly design-based, quasi-experimental, or use mixed methods; large-scale RCTs are sparse but systematic reviews note gradual increases in rigor."
