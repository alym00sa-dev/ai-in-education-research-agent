## Section 1 — Research Synthesis

### Overview

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) tools have rapidly become integrated into mathematics instruction for middle and high school students worldwide. GenAI tools—including AI-powered tutoring systems, generative homework aides, and math problem solvers—leverage natural language processing and data-driven adaptivity to deliver personalized explanations, create custom practice problems, and provide real-time feedback across algebra, geometry, and other math subdisciplines. Given the urgent need to address learning recovery, persistent math achievement gaps, and highly variable instructional settings post-pandemic, the investigation of GenAI's impact on students in grades 6–12 is a pressing issue in educational research and policy.

This synthesis reviews empirical evidence from peer-reviewed studies published through March 2026 on the effects of GenAI tools on math achievement—as measured by classroom grades, standardized test scores, and direct assessments of learning—for students in middle and high school (grades 6–12). Where possible, the report disaggregates findings by intervention type, instructional setting, and demographic moderators, and prioritizes the strongest evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), meta-analyses, and systematic reviews.

### Intervention Types and Mechanisms

**1. AI-Powered Tutoring Systems**

- **LearnLM (Google/Eedi Labs):** An AI tutoring system using large language models to answer students' math queries, often in chat format. Hybrid configurations pair the GenAI with human tutor oversight. Targets topic mastery and problem-solving fluency.
- **Tutor CoPilot (Stanford):** An AI assistant suggests multiple adaptive responses to human tutors in live sessions, aiming to enhance cognitive engagement and conceptual understanding through dynamic feedback loops.

**2. Generative Homework Aides**

- **Custom Generative AI Agents:** Several studies describe tools that generate individualized homework problems or provide tailored step-by-step solutions. For example, MathTA [1] is a generative AI–powered “teachable agent” designed for middle school math, leveraging learning-by-teaching and immediate generative feedback.
- **AI-Based Math Platforms (unspecified brands):** Rural implementation studies [2] report the use of generative-AI–enhanced homework systems delivering adaptive questions, explanations, and feedback for grades 6–8.

**3. Math Problem Solvers/Collaborative GenAI Integrations**

- Integration modes include “creative transformation” (students co-construct explanations with GenAI), collaborative group work assisted by GenAI, and self-study modules enhanced by adaptive, generative content [4].

**Mechanisms of Impact**

These systems function via several well-supported mechanisms:
- **Personalization and Adaptivity:** Tailored instruction based on student responses, error patterns, and learning progress [3][4][15].
- **Engagement through Interactive Dialogue:** Conversational agents prompt, probe, and respond dynamically, promoting mathematical reasoning and error correction [7][8].
- **Learning-by-Teaching:** Students explain material to GenAI agents, deepening conceptual mastery [1].
- **Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring:** Teachers/peers complement AI feedback, combining scalability with pedagogical accuracy [10].

### Evidence on Effectiveness

#### Academic Achievement (Test Scores, Standardized Assessments, Grades)

**High School Students:**
- A 2025 meta-analysis synthesizing 22 studies (46 samples, N = 5,232) reported that GenAI interventions yielded a **moderate positive effect size on mathematics achievement (g = 0.534)**. Effects were most pronounced in creative transformation integration modes, geometry topics, and collaborative instructional settings; the stage of education (middle vs. high school) and intervention duration did not significantly moderate outcomes [4].
- A broader meta-analysis (29 studies, N = 2,657) reported an even **larger pooled effect size of 0.924** for AI-driven educational interventions (including generative AI) on academic performance, affirming strong efficacy across K–12 and higher education but with several studies centered in high school math [3].
- Randomized controlled trials strengthen these findings: An RCT of the LearnLM tutoring system (n = 165, ages 13–15) found that students tutored by AI outperformed those receiving static hints or only human tutor support, with a **five to ten percentage point gain in mastery on difficult math topics** [7][8]. Similarly, the Tutor CoPilot RCT (n = 1,000, partly elementary but inclusive of high schoolers for select topics) showed **4–9 point increases in topic mastery** for students, especially when less-experienced tutors used AI-generated suggestions [7][8].
- Systematic reviews and field scans confirm these effects: Syntheses of dozens of studies report **positive, moderate-to-large effects (effect sizes ranging from 0.503 to 0.924)** for GenAI-augmented math tutoring, self-study, and classroom learning [11][15][16].

**Middle School Students:**
- Evidence for grades 6–8 is more limited, with most studies using quasi-experimental or design-based methods.
- A design-based study deploying the MathTA teachable agent found **pre/post improvements in math achievement relative to baseline norms**, but did not use a randomized control [1]. Causal inference is thus limited.
- A quantitative, controlled before–after study in rural middle schools found **statistically significant increases in math test scores compared to matched controls** when AI-based homework aides were used [2]. The effect size and duration were not specified, but subgroup analyses indicated sustained effects across low-resource settings.
- Systematic reviews encompassing middle school samples suggest **trends consistent with high school findings**, but emphasize the emergent nature and weaker experimental rigor of this evidence base [3][15].

#### Engagement and Motivation

- Several field syntheses and systematic reviews highlight **increased student motivation, engagement, and confidence** when generative AI tools are used in math instruction [10][11][15]. These gains are especially pronounced in hybrid human-AI tutoring scenarios, where the AI scaffolds but does not replace teacher support.
- The 2025 meta-analyses noted that **cognitive outcomes (achievement, problem-solving)** showed stronger, more consistent effects than purely affective outcomes (motivation, anxiety), though the latter were generally positive [4][3].

#### Long-Term Retention

- **No studies** in the current evidence base report effects on long-term retention or persistence of learning gains (>1 semester), representing a major research gap [3][4][15].

#### Consistency and Replication

- Findings across RCTs, meta-analyses, and systematic reviews are broadly consistent for high school students: GenAI interventions consistently produce **moderate to large gains** in math learning outcomes across diverse tools, subdisciplines, and instructional settings [3][4][7][8][10][11][15][16].
- Middle school evidence, though **directionally positive**, remains less conclusive due to reliance on weaker study designs [1][2][3].

### Demographic and Contextual Moderators

- **Socioeconomic Status and School Setting:** The quasi-experimental study in rural schools found **statistically significant gains among under-resourced students**; effects did not vary substantially by SES once internet/computer access was provided [2].
- **Integration Mode:** The strongest effects appeared in **creative transformation and collaborative learning settings**, where students generate explanations or work in small groups with GenAI [4].
- **Math Subdiscipline:** **Geometry and problem-solving tasks** consistently showed higher gains than other math strands [4].
- **Teacher Involvement:** **Hybrid models**, in which teachers supplement AI-tutor outputs, demonstrated the **largest sustained achievement effects** and improved student motivation [10][7][8].
- **Student Prior Achievement:** No disaggregated evidence on impact for high- vs. low-achievers in these studies.
- **Subgroup Data:** Few, if any, studies report outcomes by race/ethnicity, English learner status, or disability; this remains a major limitation [all].

### Limitations and Research Gaps

- **Middle School Evidence:** There are **no randomized controlled trials or meta-analyses** focused exclusively on GenAI’s effects in middle school math (grades 6–8); existing studies rely on quasi-experimental or design-based research with limited ability to claim causality [1][2][3]. Sample sizes and durations are often small or unreported.
- **Subgroup Analysis:** **No empirical studies provide robust disaggregation** by race, ethnicity, ELL status, or disability. SES is reported in a limited subset [2].
- **Long-Term Outcomes:** The current literature **lacks studies on long-term retention or post-intervention achievement**.
- **Implementation Fidelity:** Many studies are conducted in pilot or researcher-supported conditions; real-world scaling and sustainability remain uncertain [15][16].
- **Generalizability:** Most studies are from high- or middle-income countries; **results may not generalize globally or to highly under-resourced settings** [15][16].
- **Publication Bias:** Given the rapid growth of GenAI research, **potential publication bias** favoring positive results is likely but unquantified.

### Recommendations and Future Directions

- **For Practitioners:** 
  - Prioritize AI-powered tutoring and homework aides that integrate **creative collaboration and teacher oversight** for greatest effects.
  - Use GenAI tools as a supplement (not a replacement) for expert math instruction, particularly in high school.
  - Target high-need subgroups (e.g., rural, lower-SES) where evidence of impact is promising but requires adequate infrastructure.

- **For Researchers:**
  - Conduct **well-powered RCTs and longitudinal studies** on GenAI’s impact in middle school math, with explicit subgroup analyses.
  - Prioritize reporting of **effect sizes, confidence intervals, and disaggregated outcomes** (race, SES, ELL, disability).
  - Address sustainability, scaling, implementation fidelity, and effects in under-resourced settings.

- **For Policy and Program Designers:**
  - Provide guidance and professional development for teacher integration of GenAI tools.
  - Establish standards for responsible, equitable use of GenAI in mathematics education.

### Overall Evidence Confidence

The evidence for GenAI tools improving high school mathematics achievement is **robust**, with multiple meta-analyses and RCTs demonstrating consistent moderate-to-large positive effects across tools, instructional settings, and math subdisciplines. Confidence is highest for high school, where causal evidence predominates. For middle school, the evidence is **promising but tentative**: quasi-experimental and design-based studies report positive effects, but no gold-standard RCTs or meta-analyses exist. Across both levels, substantial gaps remain in long-term retention, equity outcomes, and detailed moderator analyses.

**Justification:** Strong, mature, and consistent evidence for high school contexts (meta-analyses, RCTs, systematic reviews). Evidence for middle school is emergent or limited, lacking gold-standard studies and robust subgroup/equity reporting. Findings should be viewed as highly reliable for high school and cautiously promising for middle school.
