**Findings**

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, GitHub Copilot, and Leonardo AI have seen significant uptake for skill formation among high school (secondary-level) students globally since 2022.

**United States:**  
Surveys and reports document rapid integration and experimentation with GenAI tools. Usage is widespread—between January and May 2025, self-reported use of GenAI for schoolwork among U.S. high school students increased from 79% to 84%, with ChatGPT as the dominant tool (69% usage in May 2025). GenAI is mainly used for brainstorming, editing essays, research, concept explanation, and information seeking (summarizing, writing improvement) [2][3][6].  
- "Teacher adoption of AI tools increased from 46% in 2023-24 to 60% in 2024-25, used for grading, personalized instruction, language learning, and administrative tasks" [1].  
- Students report learning benefits from GenAI, including personalized feedback, improved grades, and increased engagement, but also express concerns about over-reliance, 'AI guilt,' skill degradation, misinformation, and ethical uncertainties [2][1][3][6].
- "70% of teens have used generative AI, with 57% using it specifically for homework help" [1].  
- "ChatGPT is high school students’ GenAI tool of choice, with 69% of high school students reporting using ChatGPT to help with school assignments and homework in May of 2025" [2][3].
- Equity concerns are noted: usage and impacts vary by race and socioeconomic status [1][6].
- Policy response and access vary widely: only 14% of schools teach ethical AI use, 31% of schools have AI policies (but 60% of educators find them unclear), and 6% offer AI skills training. About 39–45% of districts or schools restrict GenAI use, with many decisions devolved to individual teachers or departments [2][3][1][6].
- AI detection tools are widely used despite inconsistency and bias—"AI detection tools often produce inconsistent results and disproportionally flag non-native English speakers as cheating" [1].

**Italy and European Union:**  
- The OECD (2025) reports "moderate AI use among teachers (25% reported use in past year) and higher student use mainly at home for homework support. AI offers opportunities to reduce teacher workload via automation, personalize learning, support multilingual families, and improve school administration" [5].
- AI chat-tutors and conversational agents are being used to provide academic support, especially for disadvantaged students who lack access to private tutoring [5].  
- AI-enabled learning is being introduced to "prevent dropout and promote learning, reduce the gender gap in mathematics, and support immigrant student integration." The digital divide and uneven infrastructure remain a challenge for adoption, particularly in southern regions and among lower-SES and immigrant populations [5].  
- A European Commission JRC report documents GenAI adoption across five EU Member States in secondary education, noting both opportunities (e.g., ethical use, AI literacy, digital competences) and persistent challenges [10]. The report addresses the importance of "embedding AI literacy in secondary education curricula and providing teacher training for effective integration" [10].

**Slovakia (Vocational Secondary Schools):**  
- A September 2025 study conducted among 200 IT-focused vocational secondary school students (ages 15-20) in southern Slovakia found "approximately 72% of students use AI tools for programming learning, with ChatGPT nearly universally used" [4].  
- AI is most used for debugging (36%), generating code (30%), and learning syntax (11%).  
- Students with higher prior programming proficiency use GenAI more frequently; most see it as a complement, not a replacement for teacher instruction.  
- Main concerns: dependency (67%), reduced creativity (54%), inaccuracies (41%), and low recognition of ethical issues (7%) [4].

**Indonesia (Greater Jakarta):**  
- A study in Greater Jakarta across four high schools (229 students in sociopreneurship, digital marketing, and coding tracks) found "high positive reception, particularly regarding the usefulness of GenAI for academic studies (mean rating 4.32/5), with approximately 86% affirming its academic value" [9].  
- GenAI tools used included ChatGPT, Gemini, Leonardo AI, and GitHub Copilot.  
- "Successful integration in secondary education necessitates a scaffolded pedagogical approach that prioritizes critical thinking, prompt engineering skills, and ethical reasoning. Students faced challenges regarding prompt formulation, data verification, and ethical concerns such as hallucinations. Preparing students to engage with this technology critically and ethically is no longer an option but an absolute necessity" [9].

**Types of Schools and Populations:**  
- Studies reflect GenAI use in public schools (US, Italy/EU, Indonesia), vocational secondary schools (Slovakia), and mixed tracks such as sociopreneurship and digital marketing (Indonesia).  
- US data note variation in use and access by race, socioeconomic status, and district policy [1][2][3][6].  
- Italian and EU sources highlight risks of digital divide, particularly among immigrant, low-SES, and rural students [5][10].  
- The global AI-in-education market is booming, but "only about 6% of institutions offer AI skills training" and only "13% of universities and 7% of schools globally have formal AI guidance or policies" [6].

**Tool-Specific Mentions:**  
- ChatGPT: Universally cited as most popular, both for general and specialized uses (essay writing, coding, language support, reasoning tasks) [1][2][3][4][6][9].  
- Gemini, Leonardo AI, GitHub Copilot: Used in Indonesia for creative and technical skill development [9].  
- Custom project platforms (e.g., Dreamina in Indonesia for elementary literacy, not main focus here) also appear in regional research [8].

**Policy and Training Context:**  
- Widespread experimentation but lack of clear, unified policies; educators report feeling unprepared [1][2][3][6][10].  
- "Successful AI integration demands closing the policy-implementation gap, providing equitable access and training, and instituting ethical frameworks to harness AI's transformative potential while safeguarding educational quality and equity" [1].  
- Teacher training and professional development for responsible GenAI integration consistently highlighted [9][10][1][5].
- There is a "growing need for AI literacy programs and comprehensive policies to harness AI’s benefits while mitigating drawbacks in education" [6][10][9][1].

**Summary of Geographies and School Types:**  
- US (urban, suburban, public, mixed SES/race): Large-scale use and documentation [1][2][3][6].
- Italy (public, regional and SES variation): Targeted for dropout prevention, math gender gaps, and immigrant integration [5].
- Slovakia (vocational, IT specialty): Skill formation in programming/coding with ChatGPT [4].
- Indonesia (urban, four high schools with creative/technical focus): Varied GenAI deployment in practical/project learning [9].
- European Union (five Member States, public secondary): Case study of early adopter schools and evolving policy context [10].

**Findings**

Recent research (2022–2026) shows that GenAI in secondary/high school contexts most commonly targets digital/AI literacy, problem-solving, subject-specific skills (especially programming and mathematics), creativity, and engagement. Evidence on regional, school-type, and demographic variations is emerging, with more systematic analyses still needed. Most evidence is Rung 2–3 (case and quasi-experimental studies); RCT-level data is not yet prevalent for this population.

**Programming and Computational Thinking Skills**

Introducing GenAI in project-based learning environments supported both technical (coding, debugging, software development) and higher-order cognitive skills (problem solving, planning): "The project-based learning model via generative artificial intelligence, or PjBL model via Gen-AI, is a research tool that was initiated based on the concept of project-based learning management focusing mainly on self-directed learning, in which learners are able to learn and practice through the projects they are interested in as to their aptitudes and competencies with the aid of generative artificial intelligence" [10]. Population focused on vocational secondary students, likely in Thailand. Targeted skills included programming skills, problem-solving, self-directed learning, and project management. Regional or group variation was not specifically disaggregated by SES, gender, or region; context is vocational/technical schools in Southeast Asia [10].

**Digital Literacy and AI Literacy**

"There is a growing emphasis on integrating both technical and critical digital skills as part of GenAI exposure, aiming to foster responsible and effective use" [4]. The study assessed secondary technical students and pre-service technology teachers (Europe, Asia): "Rapid advances in artificial intelligence (AI) are reshaping curricula and work, yet technology and engineering education lack a coherent, critical AI literacy pathway. In this study, we (1) mapped dominant themes and intellectual bases and (2) compared AI literacy between secondary technical students and pre-service technology and engineering teachers to inform curriculum design. Moreover, we concluded that digital literacy, AI literacy, ethical reasoning about AI, understanding AI principles, and critical evaluation of AI content are increasingly targeted within GenAI-related curricular pathways" [4]. Some curriculum differences were noted between developed and developing regions, and between general secondary and technical/engineering-focused tracks [4].

**Creativity, Language, and Lesson Design**

GenAI tools were used "to brainstorm, adapt materials, and enhance creative approaches in language teaching, but critical thinking and digital literacy were required to evaluate AI-generated content" [6]. According to the case study in a South Korean university EFL context, GenAI facilitated lesson planning by enhancing creativity, subject-specific knowledge construction, and adaptation of GenAI outputs, but also necessitated digital tool use and critical evaluation [6]. The context was East Asian (Korea) and for teacher preparation; there was no reporting on SES or other demographic breakdowns [6].

**Mathematics: Problem Solving, Critical Thinking, Engagement**

"Use of GenAI-supported tools led to higher engagement and improved problem-solving skills versus traditional teacher-centered approaches" [2]. The study "Evaluating the Impact of Generative AI in Mathematics Education: A Comparative Study in Vietnamese High Schools" focused explicitly on high school students in Vietnam, demonstrating impact on problem-solving, critical thinking, engagement, mathematical reasoning, and adaptability. Study conducted in Vietnam; no subgroup (SES, gender) breakdown reported [2].

**Teacher and Student Perceptions: GenAI and 21st Century Skills**

Teachers noted student use of GenAI for writing, creativity, collaboration, and formative feedback, but also highlighted the need for guidance around digital literacy and ethical use: "In this preliminary study, K-12 teachers in the United States were surveyed on their initial perceptions of--and responses to--the use and impact of GenAI tools like ChatGPT in their schools. Teachers observed student use of GenAI primarily for generating essays and other written work, as well as engagement in creative tasks, while expressing concern about digital literacy and integrity issues" [5]. U.S. context; no explicit demographic breakdown. Some reference to differing responses by school type (urban, suburban, rural), but details limited [5].

**Summary Statement:**  
"Recent research (2022–2026) shows that GenAI in secondary/high school contexts most commonly targets digital/AI literacy, problem-solving, subject-specific skills (especially programming and mathematics), creativity, and engagement. Evidence on regional, school-type, and demographic variations is emerging, with more systematic analyses still needed. Most evidence is Rung 2–3 (case and quasi-experimental studies); RCT-level data is not yet prevalent for this population."

**Findings**

A systematic review by Gunsaldi et al. (2025) examined the impact of generative AI in middle/secondary school science education. The review included twelve studies meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria, accessed from WOS, SCOPUS, ERIC, and TrDizin indexes and databases. The review provides a summary of the effects of GenAI tools on student learning outcomes in science for this age group, though full subgroup breakdowns by geography and demographics are not presented in the abstract.[2]

Shi et al. (2024) conducted a series of experiments in China in secondary (legal) education, comparing traditional teaching versus generative AI-supported situational interactive teaching. The study measured both student 'flow' experience and learning effectiveness.[6]

Wu & Yu (2023) conducted a meta-analysis of experimental studies (aggregate across educational levels, including but not limited to high school and higher education) on the effect of AI chatbots on student learning outcomes. Their purpose was to summarize and synthesize inconsistent findings from the rapidly proliferating set of intervention studies since the popularization of AI chatbots in education. The study directly reports pooled effect sizes and moderator analyses (including education level, subject, and geography), but did not feature detailed reporting by secondary/high school subgroup. The study also found overall positive effects of chatbot interventions on student learning outcomes, but detailed results for secondary education alone are not provided in the abstract.[12]

Other relevant documents include rapid reviews of GenAI use in education (e.g., Lo, 2023), which enrich understanding of the technology’s capabilities, uses in education, and issues raised during initial months of implementation in schools and universities.[6] Such reviews typically encompass, but do not focus exclusively on, high school or secondary student populations.

RAND's American School District Panel report (Diliberti et al., 2024) discusses the anticipated potential for generative AI tools like ChatGPT to enhance learning outcomes in K-12. The report notes that to date, corresponding large-scale empirical studies or quasi-experimental evidence of impact in US/Western high schools are limited. The discussion suggests that substantial additional research and longitudinal evidence is likely needed to draw firm conclusions regarding broad K-12 or demographic-specific effects.[7]

Nathan Lindberg (2025) argues for GenAI writing tools to enhance linguistic equity for English as an additional language students. The article points to the hypothesis that these tools "can enable linguistic equity by leveling the academic playing field for English as an additional language students." However, it does not present empirical trial data.[8]

Among systematic reviews and meta-analyses focused on generative AI/AI usage in education, most recent high-quality syntheses (Wu & Yu, 2023; Ogunleye et al., 2024) primarily pull from higher ed, with relatively few K-12/secondary-specific experimental studies published post-2022. Subgroup breakdowns by geography or detailed demographics remain scant, though linguistic and inclusivity rationale is discussed in the broader review literature.

No included study reports randomized controlled trial (RCT) or quasi-experimental trial data disaggregated by race, SES, or ELL status within high school student samples as of the current literature. Some experimental and systematic review literature (Wu & Yu, 2023) reports positive aggregate effects on learning outcomes from AI chatbot interventions, with heterogeneity examined by education level, subject, and geography. However, specific effect sizes or statistically significant moderator findings for the secondary school segment were either not reported or not available in abstracts/summaries.

Key points and evidence consistencies:
- Systematic reviews and meta-analyses generally report a positive effect of GenAI and AI chatbot use on student learning outcomes where measured, but nearly always aggregate multiple education levels due to sparse K-12/high school-specific studies since 2022.
- Experimental evidence in secondary settings exists (legal education in China, Gunsaldi systematic review in science), but large-scale RCTs or quasi-experimental trials targeting US or Western high school students and reporting subgroup effects have not yet emerged in the published literature.
- There is broad expectation among education researchers, district leaders, and EdTech policy reports (e.g., RAND) for potential positive impact, but a gap between this optimism and the limited current base of rigorous causal research in secondary education.
- Calls for linguistic or equity-promoting potential of GenAI tools are made (Lindberg, 2025), but evidence is currently theoretical or based on small-scale practice, not experimental measurement.
- No detailed data reported about variations in impact by school type, race, SES, or other demographic subgroups within high school populations in the available post-2022 literature.

**Findings**

Recent literature (2022–2026) on the use of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tools for skill formation in high/secondary school environments identifies several key themes regarding limitations, risks, and implementation challenges. These have been examined and discussed via systematic reviews, sector reports, mixed-methods studies, educator/student surveys, and expert policy guidance with some studies offering region-specific perspectives and evidence.

**Overarching Risks & Challenges**  
The Brookings Global Task Force on AI in Education concluded that, at this point in its trajectory, the risks of utilizing AI in education overshadow its benefits due to the nature of risks impacting foundational development in young people. They highlight overreliance on AI as potentially putting children and youth’s fundamental learning capacity at risk, risks to student well-being, critical thinking, social-emotional development, trust, safety, privacy, and the exacerbation of educational inequities—especially in underserved communities and unevenly resourced school systems. Current deployment is often outpacing appropriate safeguards, regulations, and pedagogical adaptation, calling for urgent attention and the development of robust multi-stakeholder policy frameworks. [1]

UNESCO, in recent guidance documents and surveys, notes a “worrying lack of public scrutiny, checks, or regulations” around GenAI adoption in secondary schools, with less than 10% of institutions having formal guidance or policy for GenAI use. UNESCO asserts that GenAI cannot be ethically or safely integrated without strong public engagement, strict data privacy protection, teacher capacity-building, explicit regulation (e.g., age limits—minimum recommended age of 13 for classroom use), and adaptation to diverse cultural, gender, and linguistic contexts. UNESCO also warns GenAI models often reflect Global North values, potentially exacerbating global and local digital divides. [2][3][4]

The OECD similarly highlights the urgency of effective governance frameworks, emphasizing both potential and risks, and the need for gender/SES/regionally aware policy standards as AI transitions from novelty to saturation in classrooms. In the TALIS results (2025), lower secondary educators reported more challenges with GenAI implementation compared to primary levels, especially relating to adapting instruction, addressing ethical questions, and maintaining engagement. [5][6]

**Empirical Studies at Secondary/High School Level**  
- In Nigeria, researchers found secondary math educators recognized AI’s instructional potential but described “major constraints around technical infrastructure, uneven student digital literacy, lack of contextualized training, and a perceived risk of eroding students’ procedural fluency and computational skill.” Respondents reported concern that overreliance on GenAI could hinder skill mastery, with challenges compounded in under-resourced schools. [7]
- In Portugal, a survey of 114 secondary students highlighted that 66% viewed ChatGPT as useful for learning, but 44% expressed confusion or concern about how to use it ethically. Teachers reportedly provided inconsistent guidance, and students emphasized a need for clear school policies and instruction on responsible use. Access and digital literacy differences by SES were reported as significant barriers. [8]
- A U.S. mixed-methods study found student engagement with GenAI chatbots in high school contexts was highly variable: when introduced with structured guidance and teacher support, students reported increased behavioral and cognitive engagement, but in less regulated environments, students demonstrated lower affective and social engagement and concerns about overreliance and loss of authentic academic effort. [9]
- Preliminary research in the U.S. surveyed K-12 educators on responses to ChatGPT/GenAI. Results showed “wide variation in teacher attitudes, from enthusiastic experimentation to outright bans.” Teachers struggled with fears over academic dishonesty, loss of student voice, increased grading burdens, and privacy. District policies lagged behind GenAI adoption, with significant regional variation (e.g., more permissive policies in affluent suburban schools, stricter controls or uncertainty in resource-constrained districts). [10]
- In Australian secondary schools, system leaders identified a lack of unified national strategy, tensions between “equity and innovation,” and a critical need for capacity building. Challenges included aligning GenAI tool adoption with diverse community values and ensuring inclusive access in rural and low-SES schools. [11]

**Integrity, Assessment, and Voice**
- Several studies, including classroom-based research from the U.S. and Europe, document that GenAI’s capacity to generate essays or solve problems creates challenges to academic integrity. Teachers concerned about “preserving genuine student voice” and promoting informed approaches to plagiarism and integrity have altered assessment strategies, requiring oral defenses or hand-written submissions in some cases, but these adaptations are inconsistently applied and add to teacher workload. [12][8][10]

**Teacher Capacity, Policy Lags, and Professional Development**
- Across studies and regions, a widespread finding is that teachers feel underprepared to harness or regulate GenAI in their classrooms. Respondents note a lack of PD, few clear pedagogical models, and variable institutional support. Teachers in resource-constrained schools disproportionately report feeling “behind” in integrating GenAI in ways that promote, rather than undermine, deep skill formation. [1][7][10][11]

**Equity, Demographic & Regional Differences**
- Major sector reports and peer-reviewed evidence consistently warn of exacerbated inequity where GenAI adoption is poorly governed. This is seen across digital divides (infrastructure, hardware access), regional difference (urban vs. rural), and within-country disparities (low-SES vs. affluent schools). UNESCO specifically flags the risk that GenAI models trained on non-local data and reflecting dominant/global majority values may reinforce existing cultural or socioeconomic exclusion. Empirical studies (Nigeria, Australia, Portugal) support that rural, low-SES, or resource-constrained schools encounter more severe implementation and oversight challenges, with students less likely to have digital literacy or access supports needed for healthy use. [1][2][3][4][5][7][8][11]

**Risks Specific to Skill Formation**
- Overreliance on GenAI tools, particularly in subject areas like mathematics and writing, can “put children and youth’s fundamental learning capacity at risk.” Both skill mastery (e.g., computational fluency in math) and higher-order skills (critical thinking, synthesis, creativity) may be undermined if AI is used as a shortcut rather than a scaffold. Teachers report difficulty ensuring that GenAI augments rather than replaces rigorous skill development. [1][7][12]
