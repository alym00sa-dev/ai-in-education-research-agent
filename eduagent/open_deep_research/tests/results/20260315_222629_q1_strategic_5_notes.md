**Findings**

A broad and current empirical literature base now evaluates the impact of generative AI (GenAI) tools—such as AI-powered writing assistants, coding tutors, language learning platforms, and content creation tools—on academic, cognitive, and practical skills in secondary (high school) students. The evidence includes meta-analyses, systematic reviews, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and large-scale mixed-methods and quasi-experimental studies. Most studies focus on academic and cognitive skills (especially in STEM, writing, and language domains), sometimes with subgroup analyses for K-12 or secondary students.

**Academic and Cognitive Skills Impacted:**

- **STEM (Mathematics, Programming, Science):**  
  - GenAI tools such as ChatGPT, coding assistants, and subject-specific tutors have been broadly implemented in high school maths and science, often via quasi-experimental studies with measurable skill outcomes ([10], [13], [8], [15]).
  - A large-scale RCT across 1,000 high school students found that using GPT-4 as an unguarded tool (GPT Base) significantly improved students' performance during practice sessions (48% increase compared to control), but impaired subsequent, unassisted exam performance by 17%. This implies a negative impact on durable skill acquisition when unfettered GenAI is used as a shortcut ([8], [13]). Conversely, an AI tool scaffolded with pedagogical best practices (GPT Tutor, offering hints not answers) yielded no post-test performance decline ([8]).
  - Systematic reviews indicate most GenAI classroom integration at the high school level is in STEM areas—particularly mathematics and physics—often yielding immediate performance gains, but with risk of over-reliance, reduced originality or self-regulation, and variable long-term retention depending on scaffolding ([10], [8], [13], [15]).
  - Quasi-experimental research in programming learning supports the potential for GenAI (e.g., code generators, AI tutors) to improve both technical and cognitive competencies, but emphasizes the need for task-appropriate integration ([5]).

- **Academic Writing and Language Skills:**  
  - GenAI writing assistants (particularly ChatGPT) are associated with improved task performance, particularly among English language learners, as evidenced by a mixed-methods intervention in undergraduate ESL students ([2]), and qualitative studies in K-12 ([15]).
  - Systematic reviews and qualitative research found positive impacts on language/literacy, writing organization, creative ideation, and revision practices ([3], [2], [15]), especially when AI is used interactively (dialogic tutoring, iterative feedback) rather than as a source of copy-paste text ([15]).
  - Randomized pilot studies of automated, linguistically informed activity generation tools for English learners in middle and high school classrooms found positive impacts on RISE reading assessments (vocabulary, decoding, comprehension), with especially strong effects under teacher training but no formal ELL specialization, showing the tools' ability to individualize support in content areas ([4]).
  - Systematic review and meta-analysis evidence point to language/literacy as a primary area where GenAI can drive deeper learning and improved practice, provided issues of plagiarism and overreliance are managed ([15], [10], [13]).

- **Creativity, Socioemotional Skills, and Noncognitive Skills:**  
  - Systematic reviews and major research initiatives document GenAI's impact on creativity, motivation, feedback literacy, and self-regulation. Benefits include enhanced engagement, support for project-based/collaborative learning, and improved formative feedback ([15], [13]).
  - There is some evidence that GenAI tools, when purpose-built to scaffold noncognitive skills (such as INVITE’s focus on persistence, resilience, and collaboration), aid secondary students’ development of these skills across STEM, especially for low-income and underserved learners ([5]).
  - Risks include decreased originality and motivational loss when AI replaces rather than augments student-generation or self-reflection ([15], [13]).

- **AI Literacy and Metacognitive Skills:**  
  - Recent meta-analyses and systematic reviews highlight the growing importance of AI literacy—students’ understanding of GenAI capabilities and limitations, and effective prompting strategies. Higher AI literacy is linked to greater benefit from GenAI integration ([13], [15]).
  - Overreliance on GenAI may foster overconfidence in perceived learning; students often misjudge actual gains or losses in durable skills ([13], [8]).

- **Equity, Access, and Subgroup Impacts:**  
  - Studies note mixed results for historically underserved students: GenAI aids some students with disabilities and English learners, but may widen gaps for economically disadvantaged students and students with initial weaker skills if not scaffolded ([13], [5], [15]).
  - Teacher training and professional development are critical moderators, ensuring AI is used to foster (not replace) critical thinking and human interaction ([10], [13], [15]).
  - Most K-12 studies, however, focus broadly on high school, with limited subgroup disaggregation by race, socioeconomic status (SES), or ELL status ([10], [15]).

**Practical and Vocational Skills:**
- Empirical coverage of practical/vocational skills acquisition or hands-on skill development remains sparse; the literature is heavily weighted to academic and cognitive impacts, with only emerging research in career and technical education domains ([5], [15]).

**Mechanisms and Risks:**
- GenAI tools provide rapid feedback, scaffolding, and individualized practice—supports tied to immediate performance gains but at risk of cognitive offloading if not combined with instructional guardrails ([8], [13], [15]).
- Risks include factual hallucinations, reduced originality, ethical concerns, and equity issues when students rely heavily on unfiltered GenAI outputs ([13], [8]).

**Summary Table of Most-Impacted Skills (per systematic reviews/meta-analyses/RCTs):**
- **Most Positively Impacted:** Academic writing (organization, revision), STEM (practice performance), language/literacy (for ELL), feedback literacy, AI literacy
- **Mixed/Context-Dependent Impact:** Creativity, self-regulation, noncognitive skills (when scaffolded)
- **Negative/At-Risk if Unsafeguarded:** Skill retention, critical thinking, originality, motivation, equity for low-SES and struggling learners

**Findings**

A substantial body of empirical research (2023–2026) evaluates generative AI interventions for skill formation in high school/secondary settings, utilizing rigorous methodologies including RCTs, quasi-experiments, design-based research, and meta-analyses.

**Types of Generative AI Tools and Interventions Evaluated**

1. **AI-Powered Writing Platforms**
   - Platforms provide dialogic, automated, or reflective feedback (e.g. using ChatGPT or other LLMs), facilitating improvements in argumentation, higher-order thinking, and independent writing skills ([2], [5], [10], [1]-ERIC2, [3]-ERIC1, [6]-ERIC6, [7]-ERIC7, [8]-ERIC8, [9]-ERIC9).
   - Example implementations include guided collaborative argumentation, generative AI-supported reflective writing exercises, and standalone or teacher-integrated digital writing platforms.

2. **Coding/Programming Tutors**
   - Interventions include GenAI-based dialogic feedback during program problem solving and human-computer collaborative learning environments powered by generative AI ([1], [3], [9]).
   - Studies compare GenAI tutoring to standard approaches on outcomes such as computational thinking, programming achievement, and self-efficacy.

3. **Language Learning Tools**
   - Tools leverage generative AI or LLMs for collaborative argumentation, adaptive language learning, listening/writing practice, and digital English textbooks ([2], [4], [5]-ERIC5, [10]-ERIC10).
   - Implementation modes include collaborative scaffolding, chatbot-guided practice, and adaptive content tailored to proficiency levels.

4. **Public Speaking and Presentation Support**
   - AI-powered presentation platforms provide feedback on public speaking and oral presentations ([4]-ERIC4).

5. **Other Content Creation/Education Tools**
   - GenAI-powered teachable agents in middle school mathematics via learning-by-teaching practices ([2]-ERIC2).
   - GenAI text-to-video for prewriting idea generation ([7]).
   - Story-authoring and digital storytelling platforms evaluated for engagement and writing development ([3]-ERIC3, [8]-ERIC8).

6. **Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring Models**
   - Studies on hybrid models that combine human and AI tutoring report positive impacts on learning and engagement ([1]-OA1).

**Evidence from Empirical Designs and Implementation Practices**

- Designs include quasi-experimental studies, rigorous RCTs, and meta-analyses ([1], [2], [3], [9], [1]-OA2, [1]-OA1, [2]-ERIC2, [5]-ERIC5).
- Populations include secondary/high school students, EFL students, and other international secondary contexts.
- Implementation: Interventions range from classroom-embedded, teacher-guided, and collaborative scripts to standalone platforms and blended or remote models.
- Outcomes: Impacts reported on writing performance, argumentation, computational thinking, programming skills, self-efficacy, higher-order thinking, engagement, and learning perception.
- Subgroup disaggregation reported in some studies by language background, proficiency, or engagement metrics.

**Findings**

Research into the effectiveness of generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools for supporting skill development among high school or secondary school students has expanded rapidly since 2022, with a strong focus on specific subject areas—particularly in STEM (programming, science), social studies (e.g., legal education), language arts/communication, and applied arts/technology (e.g., maker education). However, most empirical studies to date are either case studies or focus on single-subject interventions rather than comparative evaluations across multiple subject domains. As a result, there is not yet robust (Rung 4/RCT or higher) causal evidence from head-to-head experimental studies directly comparing subject-specific outcomes for generative AI use in secondary education. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses summarize these findings and also highlight the current limitations and gaps in truly comparative research.

**Meta-Analytic and Review Evidence**

A rapid review by Lo (2023) examines ChatGPT’s capabilities across subject domains, describing how it can be used in education and discussing issues raised by researchers in the first three months of its implementation. While this review references domain-driven uses, it does not provide comparative quantitative outcomes by subject for high school students [1]. Similarly, a meta-analysis by Wu & Yu (2023) synthesized the effects of AI chatbots on student learning outcomes, indicating positive effects in general but not offering disaggregated, subject-specific results for secondary students [2]. A systematic mapping analysis and literature review in a comprehensive edited volume by Sahin et al. (2025) documents subject-specific trends and interventions using generative AI, pointing to advances in personalized learning and student motivation, but confirms the gap in comparative (multi-subject) experimental research at the secondary level [3].

**Subject-Specific Empirical Research**

**STEM/Programming:**  
Zhao et al. (2025) present a human-computer collaborative programming learning method using generative AI. Their study focuses on improving computational thinking, learning attitudes, and achievement among high school programming students. The reported improvements are for students in programming courses, but no direct comparison to other subjects is included [4].

**Social Studies/Legal Education:**  
Shi et al. (2024) analyze the impact of generative AI-supported situational interactive teaching in legal education for high school students in China. This case study finds that AI-facilitated teaching enhances students' learning outcomes and "flow" experiences in legal education, again only within that domain [5].

**Language Arts/Communication:**  
Lu (2024) explores generative AI’s role in creating textual materials for communication courses, using text generation for a communication theory class. The article underscores the potential of generative AI to address the scarcity of suitable learning materials and enhance teaching effectiveness but does not contrast outcomes with other subject areas [6].

**Applied Arts/Technology (Maker Education):**  
Quintana-Ordorika et al. (2025) assess the introduction of generative AI into maker project design. Their study examines benefits for future teachers’ motivation and technology acceptance, including a comparison between traditional and AI-supported approaches in the context of maker education [7].

**Science:**  
Cheng (2025) investigates ChatGPT integration within the Lesson Study framework for a secondary school science topic (density concept) and evaluates possible pedagogical benefits. As with other case studies, outcomes are confined to the science domain [8].

**Comparative/Methodological Insights**  
Multiple reviews and systematic mappings (e.g., Sahin et al., 2025) conclude that while evidence is growing for subject-specific benefits of generative AI tools in enhancing student engagement and certain skill development outcomes, there are few or no rigorous multi-arm RCTs or quasi-experimental studies that directly compare impact across subject areas in high school. The absence of such studies (Rung 4+) precludes strong causal claims about differential subject efficacy. The current state-of-the-field is thus best characterized as indicative of potential subject-specific benefits, especially in STEM and communication/language arts, with an urgent need for robust comparative research.

**Findings**

Empirical evidence that rigorously analyzes how the effectiveness of generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools for skill formation among high school or secondary students varies by demographic factors, geographic region, or educational context is limited. Most of the current literature consists of policy reports, systematic reviews, conceptual syntheses, and empirical studies focused on bias within AI tools rather than large-scale quantitative analyses with subgroup breakdowns.

**Equity and Variation Trends**
- The OECD’s 2024 working paper on the potential impact of AI on equity and inclusion in education categorizes educational AI tools and reviews both their transformative promise and key barriers. The report notes, “Learner-centred AI tools have the potential to mark a transformative moment in education, opening doors to new opportunities for equity and inclusion” and that such tools “offer personalized learning, support for students with special education needs, enriched content, and rapid access to information and mental health support. These tools have potential to reduce educational disparities, assist gifted and disadvantaged students, and foster inclusive classrooms” [5]. However, it also cautions that “Digital divide presents significant challenges in access to AI-powered educational technologies, with disadvantaged schools reporting greater lack or poor quality of digital resources,” and highlights algorithmic bias, implementation challenges, and lack of systematic oversight and regulation as drivers of inequity. The review calls out a lack of robust, large-scale evidence and disaggregated outcomes: “The paper underscores the need for ethical frameworks, transparent AI systems, cultural responsiveness, and systematic regulation and oversight... The paper highlights a current lack of robust, large-scale evidence on AI's long-term educational impacts, calling for ongoing research and policy attention to balance AI's promise with risks in education sectors worldwide.” [5]

- A further OECD summary (Digital Education Outlook 2026) emphasizes the need for equity and pedagogical grounding in the use of generative AI: “Generative AI can support learning when used with explicit pedagogical intent, especially when designed as a tutor or learning partner rather than an answer generator. ... Uncritical automation can undermine professional judgement and autonomy.” [3] The report stresses that “improved task performance does not necessarily translate into learning; excessive reliance on GenAI may erode metacognitive engagement and deep learning,” and that differential access and teacher empowerment are central concerns [3].

**Biases and Subgroup Implications**
- Empirical studies of bias in generative AI tools have found disparities in tool output and assessment for demographic subgroups:
    - Yamashita (2025) investigates GPT-4o’s ratings of English learners’ essays: “While previous studies have investigated the alignment between human ratings and GPT ratings, few have examined potential biases in the ratings produced by GenAI for students from different language backgrounds” [7]. The study analyzes potential biases against English Language Learners, surfacing ways in which generative AI may disadvantage particular student groups in skill assessment, even when such tools are used as part of formal or informal educational contexts (Evidence Rung 3: QED, Weak Support for equity effects by language background) [7].
    - Currie, Hewis, and Wheat (2025) assess gender and ethnicity representation in DALL-E 3’s text-to-image outputs, finding evidence that “generative artificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to be transformative or to amplify misrepresentations and biases… DALL-E 3 produced multiple iterations of images using a variety of prompts,” with noted gender and ethnic bias in outputs. This suggests generative AI tools may perpetuate or exacerbate disparities in representational and possibly educational outcomes by demographic group [10].
    - Guo & Lin (2025) conduct a mixed-methods study in translation education (outside US/Europe) and explicitly address “equity implications, and impact on learner engagement remain underexplored” [3]; the study is grounded in models relevant to cognitive load and translation skill but only references secondary/tertiary contexts and does not include robust subgroup disaggregation for secondary school students.

**Summary of Empirical Evidence**
- No randomized controlled trials or meta-analyses with subgroup heterogeneity results (by SES, gender, language, region, etc.) for GenAI’s skill-formation effectiveness in secondary students were located [5][8].
- Direct effects, when measured, tend to focus on representational and rating biases (as in Yamashita and Currie et al.), or on short-term engagement and productivity effects [3,5,7,10]. Large-scale impacts over time, disaggregated by student background or school context, remain under-researched.
- Strong policy voices (OECD, 2024) call for more equity-focused, large-scale, empirical impact studies and flag that “education systems—not technology providers—must set the rules for AI use, including clear design standards, data protection, ethical safeguards, and alignment with curricular principles” [3][5].

**Key Limitations**
- Absence of robust RCT/QED or meta-analytic evidence on groupwise variation in effect for GenAI on skill formation in secondary students.
- Most available subgroup or equity analyses pertain to bias in AI output (e.g., rating, representation) rather than measured skill-formation outcomes.
- Most findings on variation or equity are conceptual, policy-level, or small sample; few if any report on rural/urban or public/private differences in real-world effectiveness.
- Calls in the literature for more systematic regulation, transparency, and equitable access to AI educational tools, due to the risk of amplifying existing digital divides and biases if implemented without oversight and equity design [3][5][6].

**Findings**

The empirical literature on the effectiveness of generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools for skill acquisition among high school or secondary school students is limited in both scope and rigor. The following represent key findings, persistent gaps, and methodological limitations, with all relevant details preserved verbatim:

Numerous studies identified focus on the integration and use of generative AI in educational contexts, but the overwhelming majority of empirical research is preliminary (Evidence Rungs 2–3), consisting of small-scale interventions, feasibility studies, case studies, or quasi-experimental/qualitative designs. There is a notable concentration on computational thinking, programming, or English/ESP language skills, with fewer studies examining other skill areas or broader learning outcomes at the secondary level.

For example, [1] Zhao et al. (2025) specifically investigate a generative artificial intelligence-based human-computer collaborative programming learning method to improve computational thinking, learning attitudes, and learning achievement—highlighting both the potential for enhanced programming learning efficiency and the developmental benefits for computational thinking. However, this focus underscores the thematic gap in other critical subject areas. Similarly, [3] Shi et al. (2024) examine the "impact of Generative Artificial Intelligence Supported Situational Interactive Teaching on Students' 'Flow' Experience and Learning Effectiveness" in the context of legal education in China, indicating some subject diversification but still within a case study frame.

Another identified study considers critical thinking and vocabulary acquisition using a GenAI-based project for ESP students ([5] Smith, 2025), again highlighting language skill acquisition. A further example, [6] Shahzad et al. (2025), uses a dual-method quantitative approach to examine the effects of generative AI on learning performance within China's education sector, focusing on constructs such as social interaction, utilitarian benefit, knowledge acquisition, and epistemic curiosity.

Where high school or secondary populations are studied, participant samples tend to be from a limited number of schools, classes, or single country contexts (most frequently China). There is a notable gap in evidence for underserved regions outside of China and insufficient subgroup analyses. Very few studies disaggregate results by key equity dimensions such as race, SES, or ELL status.

Meta-analyses and reviews retrieved are focused predominantly on higher education (post-secondary populations) or report aggregated findings on AI in education broadly, often without distinguishing between generative AI (such as large language models or image generators) and traditional or simpler AI tools (such as chatbots or adaptive learning systems). For example, [11] Wu & Yu (2023) conducted a meta-analysis on the effect of AI chatbots on students' learning outcomes. However, the review does not provide clear or extensive coverage of high school populations nor deep subgroup analyses relevant to secondary settings.

Overall, the following gaps have been identified:

- **Limited subject coverage:** Most existing empirical studies focus on programming/computational thinking or English/ESP. There is a lack of coverage in social studies, sciences, and arts.
- **Geographical gaps:** Evidence is primarily from China, with little representation from other countries or diverse educational systems. Research from low- and middle-income regions is almost non-existent.
- **Population equity gaps:** Studies rarely provide subgroup analysis by race, socioeconomic status, English language learner status, or other key variables defining educational equity.
- **Skill specificity:** There is insufficient investigation into the effectiveness of generative AI tools for acquiring domain-specific, non-cognitive, or higher-order skills among secondary students.
- **Longitudinal impact and transfer:** Very few (if any) studies report on the retention, transfer, or long-term usefulness of skills acquired through generative AI-supported learning.
- **Rigorous causal evidence:** Very few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or longitudinal comparative studies (Evidence Rung 4+) exist specifically for this population and outcome.
- **Contextual limitations:** Most interventions studied are in tightly controlled, short-duration, classroom-based settings, limiting external validity.
- **Methodological issues:** Studies often feature small sample sizes, lack of randomization, or insufficient control groups. Some rely on self-report data or subjective measures (e.g., learning attitudes, flow experience) rather than standardized skill assessments.
- **Conceptual clarity:** Many studies conflate generative AI with broader AI, making it difficult to assess the unique effectiveness of generative AI tools.

Based on available evidence, the current empirical literature on generative AI for skill acquisition among high school or secondary school students remains in its infancy, with wide areas of uncertainty and significant opportunity for rigorous, diversified, and equity-sensitive research.
