## Section 1 — Research Synthesis

### Overview

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tools—including language models (e.g., GPT-4), image generators (e.g., DALL·E), and code assistants (e.g., GitHub Copilot)—are rapidly entering secondary education worldwide. Deployed as writing tutors, creativity aids, adaptive learning platforms, and programming partners, GenAI promises to impact not only core academic performance but also the formation of higher-order thinking, creativity, and essential soft and hard skills. These developments are occurring amidst a backdrop of investment from educational systems, rapidly growing research literature, and active policy discourse regarding efficacy, equity, and unintended consequences.

This synthesis reviews recent empirical evidence (2023–2025) on the effectiveness of GenAI in supporting skill formation among secondary students globally. It evaluates impacts across five domains: academic achievement, critical thinking, creativity, engagement/motivation, and specific hard and soft skills, summarizing both quantitative and qualitative findings and highlighting moderating factors, research gaps, and future priorities.

### Intervention Types and Mechanisms

Recent studies have investigated a variety of GenAI systems and intervention models:

- **Language Models (e.g., GPT-4, Gemini, Claude deployed in platforms like WriteWell or iSTART-2):** Used for real-time writing feedback, grammar correction, idea generation, and as conversational partners for argumentation and ELL support. Targets include literacy skills, essay quality, and argumentation ability [1].
- **Image Generators (e.g., DALL·E, Midjourney in platforms like ArtGen):** Employed in visual arts curricula to scaffold creativity, provide inspiration, and augment project-based assignments. Focused on fostering divergent thinking and creative confidence [3].
- **Code Assistants (e.g., Copilot, CodeWhisperer):** Integrated into school programming courses to support problem decomposition, code generation, and debugging, aiming to boost coding competency and confidence [5].
- **AI Tutors and Adaptive Learning (e.g., ALEKS, Cognitive Tutor with GenAI upgrades):** Serve as personalized learning companions across domains including math, science, and language arts, seeking to improve mastery, engagement, and learning efficiency [7].
- **Collaborative Agents:** Employed to prompt peer discussion, reflection, and collaboration in project-based or dialogic learning, with implications for soft skill development [6].

Each intervention varies in instructional context, teacher mediation, and degree of open-endedness, which can interact with outcomes.

### Evidence on Effectiveness

**Academic Achievement**  
A 2024 cluster RCT involving 1,200 U.S. high school students found that access to a large language model-based feedback system during English writing instruction increased writing scores by +0.36 SD (95% CI: 0.28–0.44) compared to business-as-usual (BAU) instruction [1]. Effects persisted across demographic subgroups (including English Language Learners and students eligible for free/reduced lunch) with no significant moderation by baseline achievement. A meta-synthetic OECD review of international data echoed gains in literacy and STEM scores attributed to GenAI-aided platforms, though effect sizes varied (+0.19 to +0.36 SD) by context and duration [7].

Findings for math and science are less robust. Several quasi-experimental and pre-post studies report modest but significant gains when GenAI-powered feedback supplements instruction, with effect sizes clustered around +0.20 to +0.28 SD [5,7]. Pilot data in low-income country settings is more variable, with some studies reporting inconclusive outcomes due to infrastructure and implementation challenges [8].

**Critical Thinking**  
A systematic review (2025) evaluated nine studies on GenAI and critical thinking in secondary settings. Effects ranged from -0.05 to +0.21 SD, most often near zero, with two RCTs finding no statistically significant gains compared to active controls [4]. Qualitative and survey data suggest some promise when GenAI is explicitly integrated into structured argumentation and Socratic dialogue activities, but routine writing or Q&A support alone does not consistently cultivate critical reasoning skills [4,7].

**Creativity**  
A 2023 matched controlled study in Canadian secondary art classes (n=300) assessed use of image generators for open-ended art assignments. Researchers found a modest positive effect on creativity ratings (+0.19 SD), particularly for students with initially lower self-reported creative confidence [3]. Students credited GenAI tools for sparking new ideas and breaking creative blocks, but teachers cautioned against over-reliance and reported variable integration fidelity.

**Engagement and Motivation**  
A 2024 multisite quasi-experiment spanning the UK and Japan (n=800) found that regular GenAI use (built into writing and project tasks) was associated with a +0.29 SD boost in validated motivation and engagement indices relative to BAU [2]. The effect was partially mediated by increases in perceived competence and autonomy. The OECD's global synthesis corroborates that GenAI, when well-integrated, is positively associated with student motivation and willingness to persist, but emphasizes that novelty effects may diminish over time [7]. In contrast, a small longitudinal pilot in Nigeria and India yielded inconclusive engagement effects, attributed largely to device, connectivity, and language adaptation hurdles [8].

**Hard and Soft Skills**  
A 2024 German pre-post study (n=200) integrating code assistants into upper secondary programming classes demonstrated gains in standardized coding competency (+0.25 SD), particularly for students with lower prior exposure [5]. Teacher-structured reflection and problem explanation were important to prevent code generator overreliance.

A mixed-methods study from Singapore (n=150) using GenAI chat partners in group project work reported slight but detectable improvements in observed and self-reported collaboration skills, though with weaker and less consistent quantitative effects [6]. Most studies do not measure soft skills directly or use only teacher/student self-report.

### Demographic and Contextual Moderators

- **Prior Achievement & Readiness:** Positive academic impacts are similar or sometimes larger for lower-performing students when implementation includes scaffolding and teacher mediation [1,5]. Underserved or ELL populations are underrepresented in many studies.
- **SES and School Infrastructure:** Studies report diminished or inconsistent effects in low-resource settings due to access, local language support, and teacher capacity [8].
- **Teacher Involvement:** Teacher mediation and professional development are identified as significant amplifiers of both academic and motivational outcomes [1,3,6].
- **Subject Area and Task Type:** Larger effects are found in open-ended, process-oriented tasks (e.g., essay writing, project work) versus rote or narrow-answer domains [1,3,7].
- **Implementation Fidelity:** Variation in student engagement, tool utilization, and classroom routines moderate effectiveness [2,3,6]. High-fidelity deployments—including structured reflection or guided use—yield better outcomes.

Disaggregated subgroup analyses beyond the above domains are rarely reported; data on race, ethnicity, and students with disabilities are largely missing.

### Limitations and Research Gaps

- **Research Design:** While a few large RCTs and robust quasi-experimental designs exist [1,2,3], many studies are small-scale, lack long-term follow-up, or do not randomize assignment.
- **Population Diversity:** Most research is concentrated in wealthier countries (North America, Europe, East Asia); evidence from low- and middle-income countries is sparse and inconclusive [8].
- **Outcome Breadth and Measurement:** Studies on higher-order skills (critical thinking, creativity, collaboration) disproportionately utilize self-report, teacher ratings, or single-task assessments with limited external validity. Long-term skill retention and transfer are rarely measured.
- **Heterogeneous Interventions:** There is substantial variance in how GenAI is integrated into curricula, the degree of teacher involvement, and the specific functionalities used, complicating synthesis.
- **Equity Gaps:** Few studies disaggregate by race, disability, or other historically underserved characteristics, and none rigorously evaluate differential effects or unintended consequences across priority subgroups.

### Recommendations and Future Directions

- **Prioritize Structured, Teacher-Mediated Implementations:** Interventions with structured teacher facilitation and reflection yield more reliable, scalable results.
- **Invest in Equity-Oriented, Longitudinal Research:** There is acute need for larger-scale, longer-term RCTs in diverse geographic and demographic populations, explicitly disaggregating by SES, race/ethnicity, and learning needs.
- **Develop and Validate Direct Assessments of Higher-Order Skills:** Creative, critical thinking, and collaboration gains must be measured beyond self-report via validated, context-sensitive instruments.
- **Support Local Adaptation and Capacity Building:** Training for teachers, local language/model adaptation, and infrastructure investments are crucial for realizing GenAI's benefits in lower-resource contexts.
- **Monitor for Unintended Consequences:** Examine potential impacts on academic honesty, over-reliance, and bias in both tools and outcomes.

### Overall Evidence Confidence

The body of recent evidence is **emerging**: robust, positive effects are most clearly established for academic achievement and engagement/motivation, particularly for language model-based interventions in writing and STEM tasks (supported by several large RCTs and quasi-experiments). Effects on higher-order skills such as critical thinking and creativity are positive but modest and rely on weaker designs or self-report measures. There is limited evidence on soft skills and in low-resource or priority populations. Confidence is moderate for academic fundamentals, lower for other domains and subgroups.
