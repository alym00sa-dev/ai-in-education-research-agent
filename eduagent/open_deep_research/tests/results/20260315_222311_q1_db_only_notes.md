**Findings**

**Critical Thinking**

Evidence regarding the impact of Generative AI (GenAI) tools on critical thinking among secondary (high school) students is mixed and often based on perception/qualitative rather than direct causal metrics:

- Multiple sources express concern that use of GenAI chatbots, such as ChatGPT, may "invoke mixed reviews among educators." Negative learning implications include "plagiarism, academic dishonesty, and the lack of critical thinking" [2] Mason (2024). 
- Systematic review evidence from K-12 (including high school) summarized "concerns regarding academic integrity, plagiarism detection, and the potential impact on critical thinking skills," suggesting risk of "overreliance, which limit [students'] learning and development" [6] Zhang & Tur (2023); [1] Lo (2023); [10] Hessari et al. (2025); [4] Pal et al. (2025); [1] Mason (2024).
- Pedagogical interventions designed to address this have included fact-checking assignments leveraging GenAI output, demonstrating a need to "teach students to work with this technology while also developing the 'robot proof' human skills future workers will need, such as creativity, communication, and critical thinking" [8] Cail (2025). 
- Some studies show that with structured use (e.g., explicitly teaching AI literacy or fact-checking), GenAI can serve as a platform to "teach and practice critical evaluation of AI-generated content," boosting skills of "critical reading and evidence-based analysis among students" [6] Zhang & Tur (2023); [8] Cail (2025).

There is thus both perceived risk (reduced critical thinking if used passively) and some potential for positive impact if GenAI is used in instructional settings emphasizing critical evaluation.

**Writing**

Writing is among the most researched skill areas:

- GenAI tools such as chatbots are commonly used to "generate essays based on the input of student prompts," but concerns remain regarding the temptation towards "plagiarism, academic dishonesty," and lack of original thought [2] Mason (2024); [9] Godwin-Jones (2022).
- Studies involving AI-assisted feedback systems (e.g., Grammarly, ChatGPT) demonstrate "impact...on English as a Foreign Language (EFL) learners' writing proficiency, revision practices, and writing quality." The use of these tools is associated with "higher revision frequency and better writing quality" compared to control groups receiving only traditional feedback [5] Mekheimer (2025).
- Evidence from systematic reviews of AI-enabled assessment in language learning also notes "transformative possibilities for automating and personalising language assessments," with positive effects on revision and measurable skill improvement [6] Chen et al. (2025).
- Perceptions among educators are cautious, noting that while AI can "support struggling writers," it must be balanced against risks of overreliance, particularly in high school settings [2] Mason (2024); [1] Mason (2024); [9] Godwin-Jones (2022).

**Collaboration**

- Evidence regarding direct effects of GenAI on collaboration skills in secondary students is limited and generally indirect.
- Meta-analyses and process studies of AI-enabled collaborative tools in K-12 and higher education highlight that GenAI and AI-assisted platforms can "support collaborative learning environments"—but with little direct measurement of collaboration skill gains for high school students [7] Sun (2025); [6] Zhang & Tur (2023).
- Some empirical work uses collaborative online international learning (COIL) to track "impact...on the intercultural competence" (a form of social/collaborative skill) [7] Sun (2025). However, these results are more robust for language and cultural awareness than general teamwork skills.

**Creativity**

- There is debate in the literature about whether GenAI "kills or nurtures creativity" in education. Some sources state: "AI-generative tools, such as ChatGPT, are argued to hold the potential to contribute to creative thinking in education," but the "specific nature of this contribution" remains "a critical requirement to elucidate" [4] Alzubi et al. (2025).
- The "Innovation Paradox" is described in business education literature: "AI's creativity aid could stifle original thinking," but can also foster new forms of creative output through structured prompts and assignments [7] Valcea et al. (2024).
- K-12 reviews echo this tension: "Benefits of generative AI can include creativity aid, provided students are required to critically evaluate and build on outputs, rather than passively accept them" [6] Zhang & Tur (2023); [8] Cail (2025).

**Subject-Specific Knowledge**

- Evidence from meta-analyses and systematic reviews of AI-enabled adaptive/personalized learning in K-12 indicates generally "positive effects on subject knowledge and learning outcomes," though effect sizes and disaggregation by skill or subgroup often are not reported [2] Wang et al. (2024); [5] Li et al. (2025).
- In STEM subjects: "AI-enabled personalization in STEM education is a transformative paradigm...offering students a learning experience that is dynamically responsive to their nuanced requisites," with "positive achievement effects" across studies [5] Li et al. (2025).
- However, concerns about fairness, academic integrity, and "accuracy of AI-generated information" are also noted, especially when students use GenAI for undirected content generation in subject-matter assignments [6] Zhang & Tur (2023); [6] Archila et al. (2024).

**Balance of Evidence**

- Writing and subject-specific knowledge have the most robust evidence base for impact—particularly in language classes and STEM, with measurable improvement in revision quality and personalized learning outcomes.
- Critical thinking and creativity outcomes are more dependent on instructional context: positive when GenAI is intentionally framed as a tool for analysis or creative prompting, negative or neutral when used for content substitution or shortcutting.
- Collaboration effects are generally indirect or unmeasured with existing tools as of 2023–2025.

**Evidence Limitations**

- A number of large-sample studies blend K-12 and higher education; high school-specific effects sometimes must be inferred or isolated from mixed samples.
- There is a heavy reliance on perception data from educators; controlled studies are fewer.
- Subgroup disaggregation (by race/SES/ELL) is rare.
- Potential harms (plagiarism, loss of critical thinking) are typically discussed in qualitative findings, not in quantitative outcome data.

**Findings**

Recent peer-reviewed studies and official reports—mostly from 2023-2025—document a rapid proliferation and adoption of generative AI (GenAI) tools to support skill formation among secondary (high school) students across multiple subject areas. The most frequently referenced tool types and exemplars are as follows:

**1. Text Generators (Large Language Models, LLMs):**
- The primary and most frequently adopted GenAI tools are text generators, usually built on large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, GPT-4, and similar platforms. Studies demonstrate that secondary school students and teachers use these platforms for “Socratic tutoring,” “writing assistance,” “assignment completion,” “critical thinking,” and “essay scoring” ([1] Rismanchian et al., 2025; [3] Whalen et al., 2025). Adoption among both teachers and students accelerated immediately after ChatGPT’s public release in November 2022.
- Studies point to “widespread use among high school and college students in the United States,” but also highlight that research on students’ actual use, knowledge, and impact is limited and ongoing ([1] Rismanchian et al., 2025; [3] Whalen et al., 2025).
- National UK survey data indicate students increasingly use GenAI for literacy activities including “help with reading, writing, and understanding texts” ([8] Picton et al., 2025).

**2. Image Generators:**
- AI-powered image generators (examples in literature include Midjourney, DALL-E, Stable Diffusion) are frequently used “in digital art, design, and creative-based courses,” allowing students to create visuals for design, storytelling, and art assignments ([6] Ringvold et al., 2024; [3] Basty et al., 2025). 
- These tools are integrated for “authentic teaching design tasks” as shown in studies with pre-service teachers and creative subject classes, supporting project-based and iterative creative processes ([4] Jou et al., 2025).
- Art-based AI generators are referenced as tools for “disruptions…in teaching” and as “major factors that contribute to a healthy digital art ecosystem in education” ([3] Basty et al., 2025).

**3. Code Generators:**
- In computing and computer science education, code generators such as “GitHub Copilot” and AI-powered coding assistants are increasingly available and experimented with for both instruction and student tasks. 
- These tools support “interpreting and generating both natural language instructions and source code,” and spark “urgent questions in the computing education community” regarding learning and academic integrity ([1] Prather et al., 2023).
- Code generators are discussed in the context of “personalizing learning experiences,” and enabling rapid prototyping in classroom settings ([2] Pesovski et al., 2024).

**4. Custom Quiz, Question, and Exercise Generators (Authoring Tools):**
- Generative AI tools are reported as facilitating the creation of custom exercises, quizzes, or lesson scaffolds, often integrated within or built on top of LLMs, for personalized formative assessment and differentiation ([2] Pesovski et al., 2024).
- Customizable learning enabled by GenAI is highlighted as a new and scalable opportunity ([2] Pesovski et al., 2024; [3] Kamalov et al., 2023).

**5. Emerging/Other Types:**
- There are early references to GenAI-driven tools for translation, audio generation, “multimodal” composition (image + text), and integrations with AR/VR/Metaverse environments for project-based learning, but empirical classroom data are limited ([1] Tang, 2024; [2] Koumpouros, 2024).
- In language acquisition and EFL/ESL settings (not always high school), AI-powered “audio generators” and “interactive chatbots” are being piloted especially for younger and ELL students ([1] Sadykova & Kayumova, 2025).

**Subject Areas, Patterns, and Most Common Tools:**
- Generative AI’s greatest penetration in secondary settings is in language arts/literacy, social studies, digital/computer science, and art/design subjects.
- ChatGPT (OpenAI suite) is clearly identified as the single “most widely adopted” GenAI tool, followed by image generators (Midjourney, DALL-E) in creative fields, and code generators (GitHub Copilot, Code Interpreter) in tech/CS curricula ([1] Rismanchian et al., 2025; [3] Whalen et al., 2025; [1] Prather et al., 2023; [8] Picton et al., 2025).
- A range of literature—mixed-methods surveys, teacher interviews, and policy reports—emphasize that the primary “skill formation” benefits include scaffolding student writing, critical thinking development, creative expression, code literacy, and formative assessment ([3] Basty et al., 2025; [3] Whalen et al., 2025).
- Adoption is most robust in settings with digitally active staff and students, but concerns remain around “academic integrity,” “fair access,” and “appropriate use policies” ([7] Dalyanci et al., 2025).
- GenAI literacy (knowing how to use, interrogate, and evaluate GenAI outputs) is emerging as a cross-cutting competency, as documented in several recent reports ([10] Chen et al., 2025).

**Citation Key for Tool Prevalence and Range:**
- Text generators (LLMs/ChatGPT): dominant, used across all subject areas.
- Image generators: prominent in art, design, creative writing.
- Code generators: used in computing, digital tech courses.
- Custom quiz/authoring: embedded, less tracked at scale.
- Emerging (audio, AR/VR, translation): limited classroom data, pilot stage.

**Findings**

Several recent (since 2022) peer-reviewed studies and official educational reports have assessed the impact of Generative AI (GenAI) tools on skill formation in secondary (high school) students using a variety of outcome measures. The measures of effectiveness identified across these studies include:

1. Academic Outcomes / Achievement Measures

- "Standardized Test Scores and Exam Results: Studies such as the systematic review by Yim & Su (2024) and case studies in ERIC sources report use of pre- and post-intervention standardized tests (subject-aligned, e.g., science, language, or mathematics) to gauge academic gains after GenAI tool use."
- "Course Grades: Comparison of final and interim course grades before and after GenAI interventions."
- "Task or Assignment Performance: Assessment scores on AI-assisted vs. non-AI-assisted tasks (e.g., essays, problem sets, lab reports; Tang et al., 2025)."
- "Specific Skill Assessments: Rubric-based scoring (teacher or automated) of writing, coding, or problem-solving tasks to identify changes in skill mastery."

2. Skill Formation Assessments

- "AI Literacy Assessments: Tools like those described in Yim & Su (2024) measure students’ conceptual understanding of AI, ethical considerations, and application skills (quizzes, scenario-based tasks)."
- "Metacognitive Skill Inventories: Use of self-report or teacher-observed instruments mapping to skills like critical thinking, problem-solving, and higher-order cognitive abilities (Mallik & Gangopadhyay, 2023; Cheng, 2025)."
- "Domain-Specific Skill Gains: Custom rubrics or proficiency tests in contexts like scientific inquiry (Cheng, 2025; science concepts), language drafting (Zammit, 2025), or computational thinking."

3. Engagement, Satisfaction, and Perceptions

- "Student Satisfaction Surveys: Likert-scale and open-ended surveys assessing satisfaction with GenAI tools (e.g., perceived usefulness, enjoyment, clarity, motivation; Whalen et al., 2025)."
- "Teacher Satisfaction and Perceived Utility: Interviews and surveys measuring teacher attitudes, stress, workload impact, and confidence in using GenAI for instruction (Manczka, 2024; Graefe et al., 2025)."
- "Engagement Metrics: Classroom participation rates, time-on-task analytics (sometimes extracted from GenAI platform logs), and completion rates for GenAI-augmented assignments."

4. Process and Implementation Metrics

- "Uptake and Usage Tracking: Frequency, duration, and type of GenAI tool usage by students and teachers."
- "Fidelity of Implementation: Observational checklists and logs measuring adherence to AI-integrated lesson plans (Cheng, 2025; Humble, 2024)."

5. Broader Impact Metrics

- "Equity and Accessibility: Disaggregated outcome data (when available) on GenAI’s impact by subgroup (race, SES, language background), though this is still rare in the newest studies."

A summary table of typical measures:

| Domain         | Example Measures                                                          |
|----------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Academic       | Standardized test scores, grades, task performance, skill-specific rubrics |
| Skill          | AI literacy quizzes, problem-solving inventories, domain-specific tasks    |
| Satisfaction   | Student and teacher surveys (Likert-scale, open-ended), interviews        |
| Engagement     | Participation rates, usage logs, assignment completion, time on task      |
| Implementation | Fidelity checklists, usage tracking                                       |
| Equity         | Disaggregated outcome analysis (where available)                          |

"In conclusion, the majority of recent GenAI implementation studies in secondary education utilize a mix of academic achievement, skill assessment, engagement, and satisfaction measures, with increasing attention to AI-specific literacy and classroom process metrics. Robust (Rung 4+) causal evidence remains limited but is emerging, especially for measuring objective academic and skill gains. Subgroup analysis and longitudinal skill tracking are areas for further development."

**Findings**

Recent (2024–2026) peer-reviewed literature and official reports present several relevant contextual factors affecting the effectiveness of Generative AI (GenAI) tools in supporting skill formation among secondary (high school) students.

**Curriculum Integration and Pedagogical Framing**

Integration of GenAI into the curriculum, particularly in science education, has shown promise for enhancing pedagogical development and improving learning outcomes when carefully designed. In a high school case study in Hong Kong, four science teachers implemented ChatGPT-supported Lesson Study. The study highlighted that integrating GenAI within established pedagogical frameworks (Lesson Study) facilitated professional collaboration and more effective instructional strategies. The presence of structured curricular integration was associated with improved student conceptual understanding and enhanced teacher reflection. Key findings included "[the] integration of generative AI within the Lesson Study framework...potential to enhance pedagogical development and professional learning outcomes" [1]. This supports the need for deliberate alignment of GenAI with curriculum goals and professional development for teachers.

**Supervision Requirements and Teacher Mediation**

Teacher supervision emerged as a critical contextual factor. Without careful mediation, risks such as misinformation and student over-reliance on AI-generated content become prominent, particularly among adolescents. One empirical analysis concluded: "Teacher supervision plays a critical role in mitigating these risks and ensuring the effective use of Generative AI in classrooms" [2]. The study cautioned that adolescents could easily accept false or misleading information produced by GenAI tools, thus supervision is "necessary to foster critical thinking and digital literacy when deploying GenAI for skill development" [2]. 

**Access and Equity Issues**

Issues of access and equity are increasingly prominent in the literature. A K-12 framework synthesizing U.S. federal and White House policy stressed that systematic approaches are required to assure equitable access to GenAI tools, mitigate existing digital divides, and address variances in student and teacher readiness. The framework stated: "Addressing critical gaps in curriculum development and teacher preparedness" is essential alongside ensuring "all learners have equitable access to AI-enhanced educational opportunities" [3]. The presence or absence of technology resources and infrastructure, as well as varying levels of digital literacy among students and staff, were identified as key barriers to effective and equitable GenAI-supported skill formation.

**Implementation Patterns: Teacher Attitudes and School Culture**

A U.S. survey in 2022-2023 found K-12 teachers had wide-ranging perceptions and responses to GenAI's classroom integration. Initial reactions included both enthusiasm over personalized learning potential and apprehension regarding risks to academic integrity, increased workload, and disparate student readiness. Teachers noted "the need for ongoing training and support to adapt instructional strategies, monitor student use, and set boundaries for ethical and effective GenAI-utilization" [4]. 

**Frameworks and Continuous Improvement**

Frameworks designed to guide institutions in AI strategy highlight the need for continuous improvement cycles, proactive engagement at both educator and policy-maker levels, and a focus on "comprehensive design, equitable implementation & continuous improvement" [5]. 

**Recurrent Limitations Across Sources**

- Most sources are Rung 1 (Monitoring) or Rung 2 (Implementation) on the evidence hierarchy; few if any experimental (Rung 3 or higher) or causal effect size studies exist in secondary populations.
- Subgroup disaggregation (by race/SES/ELL) is generally not reported.
- Many studies are exploratory or descriptive, focusing on teacher/student perceptions, implementation challenges, and preliminary outcomes.

**Findings**
Multiple recent syntheses and meta-analyses have evaluated the effectiveness of Generative AI (GenAI) tools in supporting skill development in K-12/secondary (high school) education, with a concentration in science education and generalized AI tool integration. While no meta-analysis targets all high school subjects exclusively, several high-quality syntheses address GenAI’s impact on secondary student skill development.
 
Key findings from these syntheses are:
 
- **Meta-synthesis in science education (Tang 2024):**  
  This meta‐synthesis reviews research in languages and literacies in science education (LLSE) in relation to generative artificial intelligence (GenAI). The analysis focuses on how language is central to GenAI functioning and examines pedagogical insights into students' science learning through language, discourse, and multimodal representation. The synthesis finds GenAI intersects with skill sets related to language use, sense-making, and the manipulation/production of representations, though it notes the theoretical stage of most current research and calls for more empirical validation. [1] Tang (2024)
 
- **Scoping review on AI tools in K-12 (Yim & Su 2024):**  
  This scoping review examines 46 studies on AI learning tools, including generative models, in K-12 settings to investigate pedagogical strategies, tools, assessment methods, and student outcomes. The review documents positive effects of AI tools—including Generative AI—on student learning outcomes such as motivation, engagement, self-efficacy, and skill acquisition; however, it notes a lack of consistency in defining and measuring “AI literacy” and “21st-century skills” and calls for more systematic approaches and targeted evaluation at the secondary/high school level.
