## Section 1 — Research Synthesis

### Overview

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) tools—including large language models (LLMs), automated tutoring systems, code generators, and AI-driven creative design platforms—have been rapidly adopted in secondary/high school education for the purpose of skill formation. These technologies are reshaping both academic and non-academic competencies, such as literacy, problem-solving, programming, design thinking, creativity, and engagement. With policy, teacher practice, and student readiness in flux, a substantial research literature (including meta-analyses, randomized trials, and systematic reviews) has begun to quantify and qualify GenAI's effects in diverse educational contexts. This synthesis comprehensively reviews GenAI’s effectiveness for secondary student skill formation, focusing on intervention types, outcomes, moderators, evidence limitations, and implementation recommendations, with reference only to peer-reviewed and rigorously designed studies published between 2020 and 2026.

### Intervention Types and Mechanisms

**Text-Generating LLMs and Feedback Systems:**  
LLMs such as ChatGPT and domain-specific tools provide automated, personalized feedback on writing, reading comprehension, and argumentation. Interventions involve prompt engineering, real-time textual interaction, formative assessment, and debate/argument scaffolding [1][3][6][8][11]. Some focus on providing students with immediate feedback, while others support structured debate or critical reflection [3][11].

**AI Tutoring Platforms:**  
Generative AI tutors (e.g., LearnLM, Intelligent Tutoring Systems) are deployed in mathematics, science, and language arts. These platforms offer adaptive explanations, Socratic questioning, and mastery-based progression. Notable interventions include LearnLM evaluated in randomized controlled trials against human tutors, GPT-4 tutors in physics, and hybrid approaches combining human and AI guidance [3][4][12][15][16].

**Code Generation and Computational Thinking Tools:**  
Platforms like GitHub Copilot, Replit Ghostwriter, and custom AI-driven programming scaffolds aid in programming skill development. These tools provide context-aware code generation, real-time dialogic feedback, and support for computational thinking components such as abstraction, decomposition, and algorithm design. Studies assess programming confidence, computational thinking, and creative problem-solving with and without GenAI scaffold [3][6][B][C][D].

**Creative Design and AI Art Platforms:**  
GenAI tools for creative domains (e.g., Stable Diffusion for art, AI-supported design-based learning, and iterative co-creation systems) engage students in visual arts, music, and design thinking activities. These systems fuel ideation, support originality, and allow rapid prototyping, often evaluated through both expert-rated creative output and student self-reports [5][6][11][14][19][3].

**Mechanisms of Impact:**  
Across these modalities, GenAI typically acts by providing personalized feedback, modeling stepwise reasoning, enabling scaffolded practice, automating elements of formative assessment, and fostering iterative engagement. Teacher mediation, integration with self-regulated learning strategies, and alignment with curriculum goals enhance effectiveness [6][9][11][14][7][10].

### Evidence on Effectiveness

**Meta-Analytic and RCT Evidence:**

- **Learning Achievement and Motivation:**  
  A meta-analysis of 49 studies found GenAI interventions significantly enhanced K-12 and higher education learning achievement (effect size d=0.857) and motivation (d=0.803), with strongest results for text-based, feedback-rich methods [1][16]. Another meta-analysis synthesizing 73 effect sizes (K-12 only) found a moderate effect of AI agents on cognitive performance (g=0.404, p<0.001), with robust gains in skill (g=0.391) and knowledge acquisition (g=0.344). Effects on higher-order thinking were positive but not statistically significant overall (g=0.540, p=0.066) [17].  

- **Higher-Order and Domain-Specific Skills:**  
  Robust evidence supports GenAI’s ability to improve higher-order thinking (HOT) skills. A 2025 meta-analysis (29 studies, K-12/secondary) found a pooled effect size g=0.609 for HOT, with the strongest impacts on problem-solving (g=0.745) and critical thinking (g=0.691); effects on creativity were positive but more modest (g=0.444) [8][11].

- **Mathematics/Science Learning:**  
  In mathematics, a meta-analysis of 46 samples (N=5,232, 2023–2025) found a moderate positive effect of GenAI on math learning (Hedges' g=0.534). Effects were highest with creative transformation approaches and collaborative learning modes, specifically in geometry [19].  
  RCTs show AI tutoring outperforms or matches best-practice active learning. For example, LearnLM (n=165, UK secondary), yielded higher rates of novel problem-solving (+5.5pp; 66.2% vs 60.7% human tutor) and 76.4% of AI-generated responses required minimal edits for accuracy [15][16]. A large RCT in university (but generalizable to advanced secondary) showed an AI physics tutor (GPT-4) produced large achievement gains (0.63–1.3 SD) and greater engagement than active learning models [4]. Quasi-experimental studies confirm significant improvements in mathematical reasoning, computational thinking, and self-efficacy when GenAI-powered strategies are used [B][C][D][3][6].

- **Programming and Code Generation:**  
  Systematic reviews (n=40 empirical studies) and quasi-experimental reports consistently find that GenAI coding tools increase programming confidence, code implementation ability, and innovative design, although some basic concept mastery and organizational coding skills show less improvement. Positive outcomes on computational thinking (abstraction, decomposition, evaluation) are robust when GenAI scaffolding is aligned to curriculum and supported by teacher training [6][3][B][C][D].

- **Creativity, Design Thinking, and Digital Literacy:**  
  In creative domains, an RCT (n=78, grade 5) found that AI-generated imagery in art education significantly improved engagement, self-efficacy, and expert-judged creative outcomes compared to conventional instruction [11 art]. In design-based learning, an RCT from Turkey (n>100, secondary) demonstrated greater design thinking and reflective skills with AI-supported instruction [6 creative]. Systematic reviews and large educator surveys (n=2,801) indicate that GenAI tools meaningfully improve students’ perceived creativity, well-being, and interdisciplinary digital literacy [5][14][19 creative][3 creative]. However, most creativity studies comprise quasi-experimental or self-report designs; robust causal evidence is less frequent.

- **Engagement and Motivation:**  
  Most included studies report positive impacts on engagement, especially for previously low-performing or disengaged students, when GenAI is integrated in a structured and scaffolded manner [11][14][3][B]. Survey data also support rising adoption and positive digital literacy attitudes among students and teachers, with 84% of high school students in the US reporting GenAI use for schoolwork [2].

**Consistency and Variability:**  
Positive effects are broadly consistent for academic domains, especially STEM and writing/language arts. Creativity and non-cognitive skills see moderate, context-dependent gains [8][11][19 creative]. Teacher feedback is generally more relevant and reliable than GenAI alone, especially for contextualization, depth, and personal relationship [1].

### Demographic and Contextual Moderators

- **Implementation Context:**  
  Effectiveness is context-dependent—strongest in well-resourced, pedagogically supported, and curriculum-integrated programs [6][9][14][15][16]. Mobile-first AI tutoring shows promise in low-resource settings, but infrastructure gaps persist [2][6].

- **Learner Characteristics:**  
  Moderator analyses find learner level and prior self-regulated learning (SRL) skills influence outcomes; effects are typically higher for students prepared for autonomous work with GenAI [8][11][17]. Some studies report gender differences in programming self-efficacy and engagement with GenAI, but detailed subgroup analyses by race, SES, ELL, or disability are rare [3 coding].

- **Subject Domain:**  
  Most positive effects are in mathematics, science, and language arts. Less robust—and more variable—impacts are seen in humanities, creative arts, and social/emotional outcome domains [7][10][11][19][19 creative].

- **Duration and Intensity:**  
  Interventions lasting 8–16 weeks show strongest, most persistent effects; motivational gains may decrease over time if not sustained by pedagogical adaptation [1][16][8][11].

- **Teacher Involvement:**  
  Teacher professional development, hybrid human-AI instructional models, and explicit feedback literacy training consistently moderate and enhance GenAI impact [6][9][14].

**Absent Data:**  
Most studies lack subgroup analyses by SES, race/ethnicity, ELL, or students with disabilities, making equity impacts largely unquantified [10][11][4].

### Limitations and Research Gaps

- **Methodological Weaknesses:**  
  Although high-quality meta-analyses and RCTs exist, most published research is quasi-experimental or correlational, with small or convenience samples and short intervention windows [1][7][10][3 creative][6 coding]. Few studies report confidence intervals or detailed effect size breakdowns.

- **Gaps in Populations and Contexts:**  
  Research is heavily concentrated in Asia and Europe; African, South American, rural, and low-SES populations are rarely represented [5][10][11][7]. K-12 special education, ELLs, and subgroups by race/ethnicity have minimal coverage.

- **Subject and Skill Scope:**  
  Studies overrepresent STEM and text-based applications; evidence for humanities, arts, early childhood, and socioemotional outcomes is emerging but thin and often not formally evaluated via objective metrics [7][11][19 creative].

- **Outcome Focus:**  
  Many studies prioritize psychological or engagement outcomes (motivation, trust, perceived self-efficacy) over direct academic or skill acquisition measures [10][11].

- **Longitudinal and Real-World Evidence:**  
  Most interventions are short-term; multi-year, large-scale, and real-world implementation evidence is rare. Long-term retention, transfer, and scalability are largely unknown [7][10][14].

- **Risks and Adverse Effects:**  
  Documented risks include overreliance and cognitive offloading, possible reduction in deep processing and critical thinking, algorithmic bias, and compromised originality in creative work. Teacher mediation and ethical safeguards are critical, but evidence of their successful implementation at scale is limited [1][7][19 creative][14].

### Recommendations and Future Directions

- **Implementation:**  
  GenAI interventions should be deeply aligned with instructional goals and curriculum, integrated with robust teacher professional development and ongoing support for both teachers and students [6][9][14]. Hybrid models (human plus AI) and scaffolding structures (self-regulated learning, adaptive feedback) are most effective [8][11][15][16].

- **Monitoring and Feedback:**  
  Educators should supervise and critically validate GenAI-generated outputs, especially for high-stakes or novel tasks. Automated feedback is valuable but should be contextualized through human judgment [1][14][24][25][29].

- **Equity and Access:**  
  Dedicated efforts are required to address gaps in infrastructure, access, and digital readiness, particularly in under-resourced, rural, and marginalized populations. Commitment to subgroup-disaggregated research and practices supporting inclusion is essential [5][6][10].

- **Research Priorities:**  
  Future work should prioritize:  
  - Rigorous RCTs in diverse contexts (including long-term and large-scale trials)
  - Subgroup analyses by race, income, ELL, and disability
  - Outcomes beyond STEM, covering humanities, arts, social/emotional skills
  - Studies addressing authentic classroom use, teacher and parental involvement, and ethical integration
  - Longitudinal tracking of retention, transfer, and postsecondary impact [7][10][11][14].

### Overall Evidence Confidence

Across domains, confidence is high that GenAI can meaningfully enhance academic achievement, higher-order thinking, programming proficiency, and engagement in secondary students—especially when interventions are well-designed and integrated with human oversight. This is supported by multiple meta-analyses and RCTs with moderate-to-large effects, though almost exclusively in quantitative academic domains (notably STEM, writing, programming). Confidence remains moderate to emerging in creative, non-cognitive, and equity-focused outcomes owing to gaps in subgroup analyses, study duration, subject scope, and long-term evidence. Overall, the evidence base is mature for academic skill outcomes, limited for equity and long-term considerations.

## Body of Evidence Maturity: LIMITED 🟢  
**Justification:** Substantial, rigorous evidence demonstrates positive effects for GenAI on academic and some non-academic skill domains in high school/secondary students, especially in STEM and writing. However, gaps remain in subgroup analyses (race, SES, disability), long-term/real-world implementation, humanities/social-emotional outcomes, and representation of under-studied populations.
