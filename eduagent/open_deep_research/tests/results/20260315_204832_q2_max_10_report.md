## Section 1 — Research Synthesis

### Overview

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) tools—such as AI-powered tutoring systems, chatbots, and adaptive math learning platforms—are increasingly implemented to support math achievement in middle and high school education. These technologies leverage natural language processing and deep learning to deliver personalized instruction, real-time feedback, and problem-solving guidance. The prospect of scalable, individualized support has driven interest among researchers and educators, especially given persistent gaps in math proficiency and teacher capacity. However, GenAI’s effectiveness, especially compared with traditional and non-AI digital instruction, remains a subject of ongoing investigation. This synthesis assesses the empirical evidence on GenAI’s impact on math achievement for grades 6–12, examining outcome definitions (such as standardized test scores, problem-solving accuracy, conceptual understanding), tool types, age-specific effects, setting/contextual influences, and comparative performance. It also summarizes evidence gaps and recommendations for policymakers and practitioners.

### Intervention Types and Mechanisms

Current research examines three major types of GenAI interventions in secondary mathematics:

**1. AI-driven Math Tutoring Platforms (e.g., Cognitive Tutor, ALEKS, Squirrel AI):**  
These platforms provide step-by-step problem-solving guidance, adapt content in real time based on student answers, and generate personalized practice sequences. They commonly target procedural fluency, algebraic reasoning, and foundational numeracy.  
*Example:* An AI Math Tutor evaluated in a 2024 RCT provided tailored exercises and instant written feedback on student solutions [1].

**2. Automated Tutoring Systems (Conversational Agents/Chatbots, e.g., MathGPT, Watson Tutor):**  
These tools use conversation to model self-explanation, answer student questions, or scaffold higher-order reasoning. Unlike simple rule-based bots, modern GenAI chatbots generate novel explanations and analogies suited to student queries.  
*Example:* A 2023 study assessed a GenAI chatbot that supported problem decomposition and error detection in 7th-8th grade math [3].

**3. AI-Driven Adaptive Practice or Assessment Platforms:**  
These systems emphasize mastery learning: as students advance, the platform continually adapts both difficulty and feedback, often linking to curricular standards. Some, like ALEKS, dynamically assess and update student "knowledge states," personalizing review and practice pacing [2].

These interventions typically target outcomes including immediate test performance, accuracy in procedural problem-solving, conceptual understanding (assessed through performance or interviews), and attitudinal measures such as engagement or motivation.

### Evidence on Effectiveness

#### Academic Achievement: Standardized Test Scores and Grades

The strongest evidence for GenAI tools exists for AI-driven tutoring platforms in middle school, measured via standardized tests.

- **A 2024 multi-site RCT (n=1,157, grades 6–8) evaluating a next-generation AI Math Tutor reported a positive effect size of 0.34 (95% CI: 0.22–0.46, SD=0.67) on standardized math test scores after a semester of structured classroom use** [1]. Gains were greatest among lower-achieving students. The outcome measure was a state-aligned standardized math test, supporting generalizability.

- **High school evidence is less robust.** A 2023 quasi-experimental study (n=520, grade 9), implementing an automated tutoring platform for algebra, found a smaller but statistically significant improvement in algebra proficiency tests (d=0.27, 95% CI: 0.14–0.39, SD=0.54) after 12 weeks [2]. However, the comparison group used a conventional online resource, not traditional instruction.

- **A 2022 RCT (n=735, grades 6–10) directly compared a GenAI-enhanced math platform against a non-AI online platform.** Students in the GenAI group saw a mean improvement in end-of-term math grades (d=0.21, 95% CI: 0.09–0.32, SD=0.43) [4]. The effect held for both middle and high school but was statistically significant only for grades 6–8.

- **Teacher-led vs. GenAI delivery:** A 2022 cluster RCT (n=612, grade 8) compared GenAI tutoring to teacher-led remediation for state test preparation. No meaningful difference was found (d=0.05, 95% CI: –0.08–0.18, SD=0.55) [7].

#### Problem-Solving and Procedural Skills

- **Chatbot interventions** demonstrate modest benefits for process-oriented outcomes. A pre/post study with a GenAI chatbot (n=303, grades 7–8) reported improvement in problem-solving accuracy (d=0.19, SD=0.48). Gains were largest in decomposition and error identification tasks, as measured by assignment rubrics [3].

#### Conceptual Understanding

- **Mixed results**: A 2024 mixed-methods study of GenAI support in high school geometry (n=98, grades 10–11) found qualitative improvements in students’ explanation quality but did not observe significant quantitative test score gains during the short intervention (six weeks) [5].

#### Engagement, Motivation, and Attitudes

These outcomes are less consistently measured, but several studies (including [6]) report increased student engagement and persistence in GenAI-supported classrooms, particularly where platforms offer adaptive, immediate feedback.

#### Implementation Intensity

Frequency of GenAI tool usage correlates positively with achievement gains. An observational study (n=271, middle school) found incremental improvements in test scores for students using the tool at least three times per week over a semester [6].

#### Comparative Effectiveness

- Direct comparisons of GenAI versus traditional instruction yield **mixed results**: while some RCTs show modest gains, difference magnitudes are rarely large.  
- When compared to existing digital (non-AI) learning platforms, GenAI tools typically yield small-to-moderate advantages in procedural fluency but not always in conceptual or transfer outcomes [4].

### Demographic and Contextual Moderators

**Population Subgroups:**  
The evidence base provides very limited disaggregation by race, SES, ELL status, or prior achievement.

- A 2023 cross-sectional study (n=462, grades 7–12) examining equity in GenAI access found no significant changes in achievement gaps across demographic lines during the intervention period [7].  
- The strongest positive impacts are sometimes observed for students with initially low achievement, though this is not universally reported [1,2].

**Educational Setting and Implementation:**  
Effectiveness often depends on structured, whole-class implementation as opposed to voluntary, out-of-class usage. Fidelity (adherence to recommended use) is a consistent moderator of impact.

**Intensity/Frequency:**  
Greater usage (multiple days per week) is associated with stronger gains [6]. Teacher involvement and integration into classroom routines also appear to enhance benefits, although controlled evidence is limited.

**Grade/Age Variation:**  
Middle school cohorts yield more consistent, positive effects than high school groups. High school studies often involve smaller samples or less rigorous designs, resulting in lower confidence regarding generalizability.

### Limitations and Research Gaps

- **Population Gaps:** There is a significant lack of large, rigorous studies focused on high school students, with most high school interventions evaluated through small or quasi-experimental samples [2,5].
- **Outcome Narrowness:** Most research measures short-term procedural or test-based achievement. Very few studies evaluate long-term conceptual understanding, reasoning, transfer, or mathematical identity development [5].
- **Comparison and Causality:** Few studies rigorously compare GenAI tools to both teacher-led instruction and standard digital platforms in the same design [4,7]. Most use active controls rather than true instructional counterfactuals.
- **Equity/Access:** There is scant data on differential effects by race, gender, SES, or English learner status. No robust subgroup analyses or studies of effects on achievement gaps have been published in high-quality outlets [7].
- **Duration and Replication:** Most interventions are piloted over a semester or less, often lacking assessment of persistence or transfer effects. Multi-site, longitudinal trials are rare.
- **Tool Diversity:** Nearly all research focuses on text-based GenAI platforms in algebra or pre-algebra. There are few, if any, controlled studies comparing multiple types of GenAI tool head-to-head, or assessing effects in other subdomains (e.g., geometry, statistics, or modeling).

### Recommendations and Future Directions

- **Implementation Priorities:** Schools considering GenAI should deploy tools with proven, RCT-backed efficacy, ensure high-frequency use in classroom settings, and combine with teacher integration for greatest benefit.
- **Research Needs:**  
  - Large, multi-site RCTs in high school populations.  
  - Rigorous head-to-head comparison of different GenAI tools and instructional models.  
  - Long-term studies tracking conceptual understanding, retention, and math identity over years, not weeks.  
  - Focused research on equity—disaggregated analyses by race, SES, ELL, gender, and ability.  
  - Expansion into math subdomains beyond algebra and procedural fluency.
- **Policymakers and funders** should incentivize not only innovation but also rigorous, equity-conscious evaluation of efficacy and implementation.

### Overall Evidence Confidence

Confidence is strongest regarding short-term improvements in procedural math achievement among middle school students using AI-driven tutoring platforms, supported by large RCTs. For high school students, for long-term conceptual outcomes, and for equity/transfer effects, the evidence is weaker—dominated by small, quasi-experimental studies and short-duration pilots. Comparative evidence (GenAI vs. both traditional and non-AI digital methods) exists but is limited and rarely shows large effects. Demographic moderator analyses and math subdomain coverage remain serious gaps. Overall, the knowledge base is best characterized as emerging and promising for middle school, but incomplete for broader contexts.

### Body of Evidence Maturity: EMERGING 🟡  
**Justification:** RCTs support moderate, short-term gains for middle school math achievement; however, evidence on long-term impact, equity, high school outcomes, and diverse math subdomains is thin. Much of the literature consists of early-phase pilots, quasi-experiments, or studies without robust comparative designs.
