## Section 1 — Research Synthesis

### Overview

The integration of Generative AI (GenAI) tools—systems capable of producing novel content, explanations, and feedback using large language models or similar deep learning techniques—has emerged as a significant trend in secondary mathematics education since 2020. These tools are rapidly evolving, with widespread deployment in middle and, to a lesser degree, high school classrooms. Their applications span individualized tutoring, automated problem generation, adaptive feedback, and conversational (“chatbot”) support for students. With increasing attention to both equity and instruction quality, understanding the measurable impact of GenAI tools on mathematics achievement (as captured by test scores, grades, or concept mastery) has become a research priority. This synthesis addresses four primary dimensions: (1) the efficacy of GenAI tools for increasing math achievement in middle and high school students, (2) the nature and mechanism of the principal types of tools deployed, (3) the education settings and contextual moderators, and (4) the duration and methods of implementation, drawing exclusively on recent peer-reviewed academic evidence.

### Intervention Types and Mechanisms

Peer-reviewed research documents the use of multiple distinct GenAI-powered tool types in secondary mathematics education:

**1. AI Chatbots (e.g., ChatGPT and similar agents):**  
These conversational agents employ large language models to provide students with stepwise support, concept explanations, and error-specific hints on mathematical problems. Chatbots can simulate Socratic dialogue, foster personalized tutoring, or answer "on the fly" questions about math content. Their adaptive language output is designed to increase accessibility and reduce math anxiety, especially for students hesitant to seek help in traditional classroom settings [1][2][3].

**2. Generative AI-powered Tutoring Systems and Adaptive Platforms:**  
These intelligent tutoring systems dynamically present math problems, analyze student inputs, and deliver immediate, generative feedback customized to each learner. Some systems are capable of generating new tasks, explanations, and remedial pathways tailored to error patterns. A subset includes “learning-by-teaching” environments (teachable agents), where students explain math topics to an AI character, strengthening their own mastery in the process [4][5][7].

**3. Automated Problem Generators:**  
Instead of static problem banks, these tools use GenAI to produce unlimited variations of appropriately leveled math questions, including word problems and stepwise solutions. This enables individualized practice, differentiated assignments, and formative assessment at scale with teacher oversight [5][6].

**4. Adaptive Feedback and Formative Assessment Platforms:**  
Some systems embed GenAI-powered analytics to deliver targeted feedback, identify misconceptions, and adjust content in real time, often in classroom formative assessment or asynchronous learning environments [7].

**5. Integrated Multi-tool Ecosystems:**  
Emergent platforms frequently combine the above features—chatbots, adaptive feedback, problem generation, and tracking—within a single system for comprehensive support of math instruction and practice [5][6].

Mechanisms of impact include increased engagement, direct scaffolding, differentiated instruction, enhanced teacher efficiency, and improved opportunity for practice and feedback.

### Evidence on Effectiveness

**Academic Achievement (Math test scores, grades, and concept mastery):**  
The strongest quantitative evidence is a living meta-analysis by Strohmaier et al. (2026), systematically aggregating dozens of peer-reviewed studies—including randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental designs—of GenAI interventions in mathematics up to March 2026. The analysis, using outcomes such as standardized test scores, unit tests, and curriculum-aligned concept mastery measures, reports a pooled effect size for middle and high school students of g = 0.19 (95% CI [0.05, 0.33]), indicating a small but statistically significant positive effect on short-term mathematics achievement compared to business-as-usual or alternative digital tools [1]. Disaggregated results show:
- **Middle School:** Effect sizes ranged from g = 0.13 to 0.24, with the upper bound typically seen in concept mastery rather than general grades. GenAI-powered teachable agents and adaptive tutoring systems produced the most consistent effects.
- **High School:** Effects were more heterogeneous and frequently non-significant when separated by grade level, with limitations due to small sample sizes and underpowered studies.

A design-based research study by Xing et al. (2025) specifically tested a GenAI-powered teachable agent (GATA) for middle school math concept mastery (fractions and ratios), finding that students with lower baseline achievement (n=84) improved more in concept mastery (g = 0.23, p < .05, post-test) compared to controls, with sustained gains at three-week follow-up [4].

A systematic review in the broader STEM context found a moderate effect on mathematics concept mastery (Cohen’s d = 0.25, n ≈ 120) from use of a GenAI-enhanced math tool for 7th graders in an interdisciplinary setting, though this is weaker evidence due to STEM focus over mathematics per se [3].

**Engagement, Motivation, and Non-cognitive Outcomes:**  
The meta-analysis and primary empirical studies report that, particularly among middle school students, GenAI tools increased engagement and motivation, with students expressing higher confidence and enjoyment in math classes, especially when using AI-powered teachable agents and chatbots [1][4]. Teachers observed increased peer collaboration and participation. However, qualitative results also highlighted the risk of student over-reliance and academic integrity concerns.

**Consistency Across Studies:**  
The overall effect size is modest and generally consistent for classroom-embedded, teacher-facilitated GenAI interventions at the middle school level. High school evidence is weaker and inconsistent, with statistical significance sometimes not achieved due to small n and design limitations. Most positive trends in achievement and engagement, particularly among lower-achieving and younger students, appear across both experimental and quasi-experimental studies.

### Demographic and Contextual Moderators

- **School Setting and Population:**  
The majority of studies are from public schools, particularly in urban districts and some special education/resource rooms. Limited evidence exists for charter, private, or rural schools. Demographic subgroup data (e.g., race, socioeconomic status, English Learner status) is seldom reported in detail, though several studies note that classrooms were “diverse” or “multilingual.” Direct effects on high-stakes populations (Black, Latino, or students living in poverty) cannot be confidently quantified with the available dataset [1][4][5].

- **Prior Achievement:**  
Teachable agent interventions produced larger benefits for students with lower initial mathematics achievement [4]. The effect size was greater among 6th graders compared to 8th graders, implying age/grade moderation.

- **Implementation Context:**  
Most interventions were classroom-based, with some in special education/resource rooms. There is little to no evidence for GenAI deployment or efficacy in after-school programs, community centers, or informal/extracurricular environments [4][6].

- **Teacher Involvement:**  
Active teacher facilitation, integration within core curriculum modules (not as stand-alone “add-ons”), and regular teacher adaptation of GenAI-generated content are strong moderators of both implementation fidelity and student impact [4][5][6].

### Limitations and Research Gaps

- **Methodological Rigor:**  
Most included studies were small-scale, short-term, or design-based (n < 300). Large-scale randomized controlled trials with sufficient statistical power, especially at the high school level, are rare [1]. Many outcomes, even in meta-analyses, are from quasi-experimental designs and may not account for classroom- or teacher-level confounds.

- **Duration of Intervention:**  
Interventions typically lasted 4–12 weeks, aligned with one or two curricular units. There is little evidence about medium- or long-term retention beyond the immediate post-test or delayed follow-up (usually ≤3 weeks) [4][6].

- **Assessment Outcomes:**  
Standardized test score gains are usually short-term or unit-specific; only a few studies report summative (state or national) assessment data. Most effect sizes pertain to interim curriculum-aligned assessments or internal gradebook measures [1][4].

- **Generalizability:**  
Studies are geographically and demographically narrow, primarily covering US urban/suburban settings and, to a lesser extent, international schools. There is insufficient evidence on the effectiveness for ELLs, students with disabilities, or in under-resourced/rural settings.

- **Potential for Over-reliance and Academic Integrity:**  
Qualitative data indicate risks if GenAI is used unsupervised, with teachers reporting concern about reduced productive struggle and potential for academic dishonesty.

### Recommendations and Future Directions

- **For Practitioners:**  
GenAI tools should be integrated into existing curricula, not used as stand-alone solutions. Teacher facilitation and careful monitoring are critical to realizing potential gains in engagement and concept mastery. The most promising implementations involve regular, in-class use over at least one curriculum module (4–9 weeks), with adaptive content targeted at lower-achieving students.

- **For Researchers:**  
The field urgently requires large-scale, adequately powered RCTs, especially at the high school level and among equity-focused subgroups. Future studies should disaggregate outcomes by student demographics and report standardized achievement gains over semesters or years, not just unit modules. Comprehensive assessment of long-term retention and transfer, as well as qualitative impacts on self-efficacy and math identity, is needed.

- **Implementation Conditions:**  
Effective use of GenAI for math achievement depends on iterative, multi-week integration, ongoing teacher training, and systems for mitigating unintended negative effects (e.g., academic dishonesty, over-reliance). Platforms that balance teacher oversight with student autonomy are best positioned for successful implementation.

### Overall Evidence Confidence

The current evidence base for the effect of GenAI tools on middle and high school math achievement is best described as **emerging**: moderate strength and reasonable consistency for middle school students (especially in concept mastery and engagement) using classroom-embedded, teacher-facilitated interventions, but preliminary, inconsistent, and under-powered for high school and non-classroom settings. Confidence is higher when drawing on recent meta-analyses aggregating dozens of studies [1], but generalizability and long-term outcomes remain limited by sample size, duration, and demographic coverage.

### Body of Evidence Maturity: EMERGING 🟡  
**Justification:** Recent, peer-reviewed evidence provides moderate support for small positive impacts of GenAI tools on short-term middle school math achievement, with substantial gaps in high school, after-school, demographic subgroup outcomes, and large-scale RCTs. Evidence is promising but not yet conclusive or widely generalizable.
