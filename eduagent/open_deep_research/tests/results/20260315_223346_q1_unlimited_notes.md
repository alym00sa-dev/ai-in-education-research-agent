**Findings**

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools, particularly large language models (LLMs), have been studied for their impact on skill development among high and secondary school students. The following are key findings based on direct evidence and peer-reviewed literature:

**Types of Skills Targeted**

- **Academic Skills:**
  - *Literacy & Writing:* "LLMs like ChatGPT are used for writing support, reading comprehension, and providing feedback. Automated feedback from LLMs can enhance literacy and writing outcomes, particularly if prompt design and feedback quality are well-managed" ([6], [9], [11]). 
    - "This study evaluates the potential of such models in generating German reading comprehension texts for educational large-scale assessments, within the multilingual context..." ([1])
    - "High-quality, individualized feedback is essential for supporting the development of students' academic writing skills, yet instructors face persistent challenges delivering such feedback at scale in writing-intensive courses. Large language models (LLMs) have the potential to provide timely and personalized feedback, but the pedagogical quality of this feedback is highly sensitive to the design..." ([6])
  - *Critical Thinking and Argumentation:* "LLMs are used in structured debate/argumentation tools to enhance reasoning and self-reflection." For example: "Generative AI-Based Evaluation and Feedback System for Argumentation Skills, showing comparable reliability to human expert assessment." "Gen-AI has a moderately positive overall effect (effect size g=0.609) on higher-order thinking, with the strongest impact on problem-solving abilities (g=0.745), followed by critical thinking (g=0.691), and a moderate effect on creativity (g=0.444)" ([11], [3]).
- **Non-Academic Skills:**
  - *Creativity & Communication:* "Evidence for substantial gains in creativity is mixed; most studies find moderate improvements, especially for collaborative or prompt-based tasks, but over-reliance may suppress original thinking if not scaffolded" ([11]).
    - "Qualitative research suggests LLMs can boost student agency, particularly in settings where students co-design prompts or evaluate model outputs" ([8]).
  - *Engagement:* "Studies report increased engagement—especially for students who previously struggled—but this is highly dependent on context and implementation fidelity" ([17]). "Study in EFL settings found that AI use improved behavioral and emotional engagement" ([12]).

**Evaluation Metrics**

- *Academic Performance:* Exam/test scores, writing quality rubrics, reading comprehension, argumentation performance ([1], [3], [6], [11]).
- *Skill Assessments:* "Standardized tests for critical thinking/problem-solving, rubrics for creativity, and content analysis of student work" ([11]).
- *Feedback/Perceptions:* "Structured surveys, interviews, and reflective journals from both students and teachers" ([8]).
- *Engagement Metrics:* "Self-report, attendance, assignment submission, platform log data" ([17]).

**Diverse Contexts**

- *Geographic Breadth:* "Studies include Japan (LLM in English education), Malta (minority language learning), USA, EU, and multilingual/immigrant populations" ([4], [5], [6]).
- *Socio-economic settings:* "Meta-analytic work shows no systematic disadvantage in outcomes based solely on SES, but access (device/internet) may limit impact in under-resourced settings" ([16], [17]).
- *Classroom vs. Extracurricular:* "Most evidence is from classroom-integrated interventions, though some programs report on afterschool or project-based learning. Highest gains are reported in structured curricular integration over 8–16 weeks" ([11]).

**Strength of Evidence**

- *Meta-analysis (RCT/QED):* "Does Generative Artificial Intelligence Improve Students' Higher-Order Thinking (HOT)? (2025): Pooled effect size g=0.61 for HOT (problem-solving, critical thinking, creativity); strongest on problem-solving (g=0.75), followed by critical thinking (g=0.69), moderate effect on creativity (g=0.44)" ([11]).
- *Quasi-Experimental/Matched Comparison:* "Several studies comparing AI feedback/scoring to human-expert assessment (e.g., argumentation, writing) and showing similar or sometimes more timely/effective results" ([3], [6]).
- *Teacher and student perception studies aligning with skill-gain metrics* ([8], [17]).
- *Implementation Studies:* "Rich qualitative findings on classroom practice, engagement, and perceptions (esp. for minority languages or marginalized groups), but less rigorous evidence of learning outcomes" ([5], [8]).

**Equity and Heterogeneity**

- "Some studies analyze subgroup results...using AI to reduce equity gaps in gifted/talent identification" ([16]).
- "No 'universal gains': Outcomes are most positive when LLMs are systematically integrated, with scaffolding and adaptive feedback tailored to students’ self-regulation and motivation" ([11]).
- "Over-reliance can suppress self-regulation/creativity if students use LLMs only for answer generation; misalignment between model output and classroom standards requires careful oversight" ([11], [8]).

**Findings**

Recent research robustly examines the effectiveness of generative AI tools centered on tutoring for secondary education students, especially in skill formation across domains such as STEM, literacy, problem solving, and higher-order thinking skills. The evidence base includes systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials, and quasi-experimental studies from a range of global and implementation contexts.

**1. Evidence of Skill Gains (Targeted Skills, Achievement, Methods)**
- AI-driven tools—adaptive learning platforms and intelligent tutoring systems—significantly improve academic performance, engagement, and knowledge retention in comparison to traditional learning methods, with performance gains reported from 15% to 35% ([1] Issa et al., 2025).
- A meta-analysis focused on generative AI’s effects on higher-order thinking (critical thinking, creativity, problem solving) found moderate, statistically significant positive effects overall (Hedges's g = 0.609), with the largest impact on problem-solving ability (ES = 0.745) and critical thinking (ES = 0.691). Creativity gains were positive but smaller (ES = 0.444) ([8] Zhao et al., 2025).
- The strongest gains are seen in math and science disciplines. Meta-analyses report up to 20-25% higher test scores for students using AI in these subjects. Especially in China, AI tutoring platforms have demonstrated an average 20% improvement in math scores compared to traditional instruction ([2] Sengupta et al., 2025).
- AI tutoring significantly improved mathematical reasoning (including conjecture and representation components), with frequent AI system engagement correlating with higher achievement. Effective implementation was tied to teacher guidance and a supportive environment ([11] Al-Zoubi, 2025).

**2. Impact by Skill Domain**
- Most evidence focuses on STEM, but available meta-analyses confirm positive impacts on higher-order thinking skills such as problem solving, self-regulation, and critical thinking ([8] Zhao et al., 2025).
- Gains in humanities and non-STEM fields are generally more modest or show mixed results ([2] Sengupta et al., 2025).
- AI-driven personalized learning platforms show efficacy in literacy gains in middle and high school contexts ([6] Sharma, 2026). Quasi-experimental studies also found significant English Language Arts improvement using AI-powered platforms ([ERIC 3][ED676253], [ERIC 5][ED677099]).

**3. Comparison to Human Tutoring and Active Learning**
- Generative AI-powered tutors (e.g., LearnLM) have matched or outperformed human tutors in RCTs. For instance, secondary students tutored by LearnLM outperformed those tutored by humans alone (66% vs. 61% success rate in solving challenging math problems) ([3] FutureEd, 2025; [15] LearnLM/Google & Eedi, 2025; [16] Tu, et al., 2025).
- In a crossover RCT with University learners (Harvard), an AI physics tutor (built on GPT-4) led to significantly higher post-test scores compared to best-practice active learning (effect size up to 1.3 SD), with greater engagement and less time required ([4] Choi et al., 2025).
- Intelligent tutoring systems using generative AI approaches yield learning gains comparable to one-on-one human tutoring, particularly in math and science ([2] Sengupta et al., 2025; [12] Brookings, 2024).

**4. Engagement, Teacher Feedback, and Hybrid Approaches**
- AI platforms boost student motivation and allow for increased engagement, especially when incorporating adaptive feedback, gamification, and dynamic questioning ([2] Sengupta et al., 2025; [4] Choi et al., 2025; [12] Brookings, 2024).
- Teacher involvement and hybrid human-AI collaboration (e.g., “AI hybrid vigor”) remains critical. Teachers provide pacing, interpretation, social-emotional adjustments, and behavioral scaffolding, which enhances the effectiveness of AI tutors ([2] Sengupta et al., 2025; [12] Brookings, 2024; [16] Tu et al., 2025).
- Tutors using AI tools to assist their chat-based tutoring sessions (e.g., CoPilot) led to even higher mastery rates, especially when tutors were less experienced ([3] FutureEd, 2025).

**5. Implementation Contexts and Equity**
- Effectiveness is highly context-dependent, influenced by subject area, local infrastructure, teacher training, age group, and implementation model ([2] Sengupta et al., 2025; [1] Issa et al., 2025).
- Substantial gains are reported in settings with government investment (e.g., China) and among students with higher self-regulated learning skills ([8] Zhao et al., 2025).
- Mobile-first AI tutoring solutions are emerging as promising in low-resource contexts (e.g., Ghana) ([2] Sengupta et al., 2025).
- In the U.S., there is rapid but uneven AI adoption across districts, with demographic differences in usage and access—Black and Latinx students use AI more, but also have gaps in awareness and training ([18] Bowdoin Hastings Initiative, 2025).
- Research consistently emphasizes equity risks relating to access, algorithmic fairness, and teacher preparedness ([1] Issa et al., 2025; [2] Sengupta et al., 2025; [18] Bowdoin Hastings Initiative, 2025).

**6. Metrics/Methods for Evaluation**
- Outcome metrics include standard achievement gains (test scores, post-tests), skill mastery rates, engagement logs, surveys, interviews, and teacher/peer feedback ([1] Issa et al., 2025; [4] Choi et al., 2025; [11] Al-Zoubi, 2025; [16] Tu et al., 2025).
- Several studies used randomized controlled trials (e.g., LearnLM, CoPilot, Harvard GPT-4 tutor), quasi-experimental designs (e.g., Coursemojo English/ELA studies), and meta-analytical synthesis ([4] Choi et al., 2025; [8] Zhao et al., 2025; [15] LearnLM/Google & Eedi, 2025; [6] Létourneau, 2025).

**7. Limits, Risks, and Design Considerations**
- Over-reliance on AI may lead to cognitive offloading, diminished retention, and weaker independent problem-solving. Optimal results depend on responsible and well-designed integration, typically combining AI with human pedagogical support ([2] Sengupta et al., 2025; [8] Zhao et al., 2025).
- Critical risks include algorithmic bias, data privacy, technology access, lack of human connection, and challenges around academic honesty ([1] Issa et al., 2025; [18] Bowdoin Hastings Initiative, 2025).
- AI tutors employing Socratic questioning and scaffolding (with moderation by humans) support deeper reflection and higher-order skill formation ([16] Tu et al., 2025).
- There is a call for more longitudinal and heterogeneous studies, as most available research covers short-to-medium interventions, with limited data on long-term impacts or non-STEM/social/metacognitive outcomes ([1] Issa et al., 2025; [8] Zhao et al., 2025).

**Findings**

A set of systematic reviews, meta-analyses, quasi-experimental studies, and descriptive syntheses from 2020–2026 directly address the effectiveness of generative AI (GenAI) tools (such as ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot, Replit Ghostwriter) as programming aids in high school/secondary education. These findings cover academic skills like coding proficiency and computational thinking, as well as non-academic skills including creativity and problem-solving.

1. **Systematic Reviews & Meta-Analyses**

A major open-access systematic literature review (2025) from Springer systematically analyzes 40 empirical studies on GenAI in programming education. It finds:

- "Integrating generative artificial intelligence tools like ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot into programming education presents notable opportunities to enhance learning but also raises critical challenges in balancing innovation with preserving foundational programming skills."
- "Successful integration hinges on intentional teaching strategies, thoughtfully designed assessments, and structured integration processes."
- "Barriers such as GenAI tools’ limited accessibility features, insufficient bias mitigation, and a tendency to prioritize tool availability over curriculum alignment often disrupt seamless adoption."
- "To address these gaps, this paper proposes the GenAI-Ped framework, a structured approach combining self-regulated learning, universal design principles, and iterative feedback to harmonize GenAI-driven support with skill development."
- The review "highlights both benefits and challenges" and underscores the need for "balancing technological innovation with maintaining core programming competencies and advanced cognitive skills in programming education" [6].

Another 2026 meta-analysis (Nature/Springer) "synthesizes the pooled effect of GenAI on educational outcomes," signaling "significant positive effects" but does not focus specifically on secondary students, though some studies likely include this group [A].

2. **Experimental/Quasi-Experimental and Contextual Studies**

- A peer-reviewed 2025 study in Modern Educational Technology, conducted in high schools in China, tested a GenAI-empowered teaching model. Findings included:
  - "GenAI empowerment can significantly enhance students’ programming confidence."
  - "GenAI empowerment can significantly enhance students’ mastery of programming understanding knowledge and situational problem-solving knowledge."
  - "GenAI empowerment can significantly enhance students’ code implementation ability and innovative design ability of works."
  - While "overall student satisfaction with GenAI use was high," its effects on "programming value perception, programming interest, basic concept knowledge mastery, and organizational coding skills was limited."
  - "There existed gender differences" [3].

- A quasi-experimental ERIC study (2025) on human-computer collaborative programming in secondary students, using generative AI tools:
  - Found improved computational thinking, learning attitudes, and programming achievement versus traditional methods [B].

- A 2025 ERIC-listed study investigating generative AI dialogic feedback in programming problem solving found that dialogic AI feedback improved programming performance, learning satisfaction, and self-efficacy [C].

- An ERIC study (2024) designed an intelligent programming scaffold using ChatGPT. It demonstrated:
  - "significant improvements in students' computational thinking, including abstraction, problem decomposition, algorithmic thinking, and evaluation." Additionally, "the prompt system was robust in customizing interaction and guidance for individual student learning needs," suggesting the value of tailored AI code feedback systems [D].

3. **Targeted Skills & Outcomes**

- The skills and academic/non-academic outcomes targeted across the above research include: coding proficiency, computational thinking (abstraction, decomposition, pattern recognition, algorithm design), programming self-efficacy, problem-solving, and creativity.
- Some studies explicitly measured "innovative design ability" and situational application skills [3].
- For creativity: a study (2024) used generative AI to automate the Alternative Uses Test (AUT) for divergent thinking (creativity measurement) with ninth grade students and found that "repeated practice improves fluency and flexibility" (as creativity subskills), and that automated GenAI scoring provides scalable, objective creativity assessment [17].

4. **Evaluation Methods**

- Studies employed a variety of methods, such as quasi-experimental designs, pre/post-test performance measures, surveys on motivation and attitudes, teacher or self-assessment, and in some cases automated creativity or computational thinking scoring using GenAI itself [6, 3, D, 17].
- Multiple sources note high student satisfaction and engagement with GenAI tools, but flag possible issues of over-reliance and differences in outcomes by gender or prior ability level [3, 6, F].

5. **Implementation Contexts**

- GenAI programming aids have been implemented in classroom instruction, after-school programs, and inquiry-based learning systems in various countries (notably China, the US, and Mexico), with tools ranging from custom platforms to mainstream products like GitHub Copilot, Tabnine, Amazon Q Developer, and Replit Ghostwriter [1, 6, 3].
- Systematic reviews emphasize that context matters: effective use requires pedagogy and curriculum alignment, teacher professional development, and adaptation to local infrastructure [6, 9].
- Teacher preparedness, PD, and attitudes have been identified as a critical success factor, alongside student training to counter over-dependency [9, 21].

6. **Risks, Barriers, and Additional Themes**

- Risks include over-reliance, undermining of foundational skills, inequality in access, insufficient accessibility and bias mitigation, and a mismatch between tool capabilities and assessment practices [6, 9].
- Automated feedback and individualized support are recognized as major strengths but must be coupled with reflective and scaffolded learning for best results [6, D].
- Continuous PD, ethics training, and multi-level organizational support are recommended to ensure safe and effective use of GenAI in high school settings [9].

**Findings**

Recent systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and primary studies provide a robust, multi-faceted overview of how generative AI tools (encompassing art, music, and visual creativity platforms) affect the development of academic and non-academic skills—including creativity, collaboration, design thinking, and digital literacy—among high school or secondary school students.

**Creativity and Skill Development**

A systematic review of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) in K-12 education from 2023–early 2025 analyzed 30 empirical studies (primarily focusing on high school students and preservice teachers). ChatGPT was the most common GenAI tool, either used alone or with platforms like Copilot and Midjourney. The review highlights GenAI’s potential to personalize education, aid lesson differentiation, and enhance pedagogical practices. However, the review underscores the importance of responsible use and policy development and identifies a gap in high-quality research disaggregating effects by context or subgroup (e.g., race, SES, region) ([5] Li & Zheng (2025)).

A 2025 empirical study in Humanities and Social Sciences Communications evaluated the integration of AI-generated imagery (using Stable Diffusion) into visual art education for fifth-grade students. Randomized assignment to GAI-assisted and conventional control groups revealed significantly higher classroom engagement and self-efficacy in the GAI group, with no increase in cognitive load. Expert-rated paintings by the GAI group were higher in quality and originality ([11] Xi et al. (2025)). Evaluation metrics included validated questionnaires and expert scoring on technical skill, creativity, originality, and adherence to theme.

A systematic literature review (2025) encompassing 64 studies from 2015–2024 on GenAI in creative contexts found a dramatic increase in research and in the perceived ability of GenAI to generate content indistinguishable from human-produced art. The review calls attention to new conceptualizations of creativity including cognitive, product, and environmental factors. It also recognizes ethical and authenticity concerns and gaps in understanding long-term and demographic impacts ([12] Müller et al. (2025)).

A 2025 meta-analysis (Holzner et al.) synthesizes empirical studies and emphasizes that GenAI augments rather than replaces human creativity, showing strongest effects for ideation, logical reasoning, and problem definition, but weaker performance in generating novel ideas or supporting multidisciplinary integration. The literature confirms that while GenAI boosts individual creativity and output, it risks reducing collective originality and diversity. Systematic pedagogical approaches and explicit AI literacy instruction are recommended ([19] Holzner et al. (2025)).

**Secondary School Classroom Applications**

A 2025 classroom study with 34 industrial design students (Rodriguez-Ferradas et al.) demonstrated that scaffolded, progressive integration of GenAI tools (ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, Google Gemini, Vizcom) led to a substantial positive shift in students' perceptions of AI for creative work, with 80% of students reporting greater perceived usefulness and 76% expressing confidence in future AI use for creativity. Students cited increased efficiency, ideation support, and accessibility as key benefits. Cautions include risk for overreliance and inhibition of creativity in some cases. The study relied on self-report, pre/post surveys, and qualitative feedback ([3] Rodriguez-Ferradas et al. (2025)).

A 2024 experimental study in Turkey (Saritepeci & Durak) using AI-supported design-based learning (DBL) for secondary students, found significant gains in design thinking mindset, creative and reflective thinking skills compared to traditional methods. The integration of AI in project-based activities (e.g., design challenges) provided richer opportunities for iterative improvement, collaboration, and peer feedback ([6] Saritepeci & Durak (2024)). 

**Digital Literacy, Engagement, and Collaboration**

A College Board study from 2025 found 84% of high school students reported using GenAI tools for schoolwork, with broad exposure to platforms like ChatGPT, and noted widespread student, teacher, and administrator recognition of the importance of AI literacy. Engagement metrics and attitudes are split—students find GenAI helpful for brainstorming and editing, but educators raise concerns regarding possible negative impacts on essential skills and academic integrity ([2] College Board (2025)). While supportive of AI literacy, evidence is mixed on impact, with calls for evidence-based implementation.

A large international educator survey (Adobe/Advanis, 2025) reported that 91% of educators observed deeper learning and interdisciplinary integration when students used creative AI tools. Most cited positive impacts on engagement, resilience, self-expression, and job readiness; 82% associated AI-driven creative work with increases in student well-being and engagement. Qualitative teacher and student feedback echoed these themes ([14] Adobe/Advanis (2025)).

Feedback from teachers using GenAI platforms to evaluate creative tasks and student writing is varied. Studies and practice-based syntheses show that GenAI feedback is immediate and scalable, improving student motivation for revision and freeing up teacher time for individual support, but may lack nuance, prioritization, or creativity-supportive tone, and cannot be presumed accurate ([24] Edutopia, [25] The Learning Accelerator, [29] Yu, 2025). Critical engagement with GenAI feedback, human oversight, and explicit discussion of ethical and practical limitations are necessary ([29] Yu, 2025).

**Equity, Ethical, and Authenticity Considerations**

Multiple systematic reviews and educator reflections highlight unresolved tensions regarding authenticity, ownership, and the possible stifling of reflective or iterative thinking when AI is used in creative design education ([4] Pott et al., 2026; [12] Müller et al., 2025; [10] Gregor, 2025). Student and teacher focus groups noted the need for AI to augment, not replace, student voice and judge originality. Key recommendations for policy and curricular development emphasize transparency of authorship, ethical integration, and the continued importance of human guidance—both for equity and to maintain the richness of collaborative and creative learning.

**Global and Contextual Variation**

Most empirical studies focus on contexts in Europe, East Asia, and North America; there are noted gaps in research from Africa and South America ([5] Li & Zheng (2025)). There is limited evidence on long-term effects, scalability, and contextual adaptation for marginalized or diverse student groups. Several systematic reviews call for more robust, heterogeneity-focused work and studies using multi-year, real-world implementations rather than brief interventions.

**Evaluation Metrics**

Commonly used metrics include:
- Pre- and post-intervention creativity assessments (teacher/expert ratings, Torrance Test-like metrics)
- Academic self-efficacy and engagement scales (validated questionnaires)
- Digital literacy self-reports or direct performance tasks
- Teacher and student feedback (focus groups/interviews or written reflection)
- Product-based measures (creativity, originality, and technical skill of student work as judged by external raters)
- Use analytics on AI tools (frequency, depth, types of engagement)

**Limitations Across Evidence**

Most studies caution that effects are based on self-report and short-term interventions. Relatively few use RCT or multi-site designs. Systematic reviews stress the need for subgroup analysis (by ability, demographics, or prior experience), longitudinal outcome tracking, and better frameworks for ethical and meaningful human–AI collaboration in creative learning.

**Findings**

A comprehensive meta-analysis of 49 studies reveals that generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) significantly enhances learning achievement (effect size 0.857) and motivation (0.803) among K-12 and higher education students. Notably, the positive effects are more pronounced in higher education and with text-based interactions, whereas motivation impacts tend to wane over time. Further research highlights GenAI's role in personalized learning, problem-solving skills development, and real-time feedback, while cautioning against overreliance that may impair critical thinking and independent cognition. Additional studies examine GenAI's integration in programming education, English as a foreign language learning, and art appreciation via virtual reality, broadly confirming its beneficial influence on learning outcomes, motivation, self-efficacy, and engagement. However, empirical evidence also underscores the continued superiority of teacher feedback over GenAI in relevance, reliability, context, relational significance, and expertise. Investigations into factors affecting GenAI's effectiveness point to technological traits like perceived intelligence and novelty, student readiness, and discipline-specific applications as key moderators. To optimize GenAI's educational benefits, strategies include aligning AI tools with subject needs, integrating with traditional teaching, ongoing monitoring, and redefining teacher roles toward facilitating critical thinking and providing expert, contextual feedback. Collectively, these findings support GenAI's promise in education while advocating for balanced, thoughtful implementation [1][16].

A 2026 meta-analysis evaluating the impact of AI agents on cognitive learning outcomes among K-12 students analyzed 73 effect sizes from 34 studies. The study found that AI agents have a statistically significant moderate positive effect on student cognitive performance, with an overall effect size of 0.404 (p < 0.001). The benefits were most pronounced in skill development (g = 0.391, p < 0.001) and knowledge acquisition (g = 0.344, p < 0.05), while effects on higher-order thinking skills were not statistically significant (g = 0.540, p = 0.066). Moderator analysis revealed that learner level (grade) and subject discipline significantly influence effectiveness. Despite minor publication bias, the findings are robust. The study underscores the importance of considering both disciplinary context and student characteristics in integrating AI agents in K-12 education, providing valuable guidance for educators, policymakers, and researchers aiming to optimize cognitive learning outcomes through tailored AI implementation strategies [17].

A 2025 systematic review of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) in K–12 education synthesized 84 peer-reviewed empirical studies (2020–2025), mapping the interrelations between learning goals, activities, roles, and outcomes. Seven main learning objectives for GenAI were identified: language/literacy enhancement, STEM learning, creativity, socioemotional skills, feedback literacy/self-regulation, motivation, and AI literacy. Dominant learning activity patterns include dialogic tutoring with formative feedback, generative iterative co-creation, project-based problem-solving, simulation/game-based learning, and assessment support. GenAI's role varies from AI-directed to AI-empowered, reshaping agency among students, teachers, and caregivers through feedback loops. Outcomes were epistemic (knowledge), practice-based (skills), and affective/identity-related, offering opportunities (deeper knowledge, improved practice, engagement) but also risks (hallucinations, reduced originality, over-reliance, motivational loss, and ethical issues). The review calls for alignment of goals–activities–roles, teacher professional development, improved feedback literacy, clear ethical guidelines, and longitudinal, cross-cultural research to better evaluate GenAI impacts [7].

Another 2025 systematic review found that most existing research focuses on high school students and preservice teachers, predominantly in STEM subjects such as mathematics and physics. ChatGPT is the dominant GenAI tool used, often combined with Copilot and Midjourney. Most studies employ quasi-experimental designs with psychological outcomes; few measure objective learning outcomes. Research is geographically concentrated in Asia (especially Taiwan and China) and Europe (notably Germany), with very little representation from Africa or South America. The review underscores the need for further empirical research in K-12 settings and identifies gaps including limited focus on in-service teachers, real-time classroom implementation, early childhood and special education, rural/non-English-speaking contexts, and insufficient parental involvement. It calls for more high-quality, longitudinal, and diverse research to fully leverage GenAI’s potential [10][11].

A meta-analysis assessing the impact of GenAI on learning outcomes among K-12 and higher education students found positive effects, with mean effect sizes of 0.857 for learning achievement and 0.803 for learning motivation. The analysis identified factors moderating these effects, including education level, subject area, GenAI interface, development, interaction approach, and length of experimentation. Specifically, GenAI showed greater impact on higher education students' academic performance, and text-based interactions were more effective than those involving mixed media like images or audio. Although GenAI positively influenced learning motivation, its effect diminished over time [16].

A preregistered meta-analysis on generative AI's impact on mathematics learning outcomes synthesized 22 empirical studies (46 samples, N=5232, 2023–2025) and found a moderate positive effect (Hedges' g = 0.534) of GenAI on mathematics learning, particularly with creative transformation integration, when applied to geometry content, with small groups, and collaborative learning modes. The study distinguished cognitive from non-cognitive skills, finding stronger evidence for enhancements in cognitive domains. Moderators such as educational stage and intervention duration showed no significant effects. The analysis highlights potential risks like reduced deep thinking and overreliance, though overall concludes GenAI supports personalized, interactive math learning [19].

A 2026 meta-analysis and systematic review published in Humanities and Social Sciences Communications presented a systematic comparison of generative AI versus traditional and non-GenAI educational approaches. The central finding is that GenAI generally outperforms these traditional methods in improving educational outcomes, especially academic achievements, higher-order thinking abilities, and writing skills, with GenAI feedback yielding superior results. Subgroup analyses by geography and intervention type reveal nuanced findings. The study advocates for further research on GenAI’s effects for students with disabilities and the development of personalized teaching strategies [4].

In an additional meta-analysis, the results indicate that GenAI has a significant positive impact on overall learning outcomes, with effect sizes similar to other leading syntheses, and point out the variability of findings contingent on subject, context, and grade level [3].

Evidence from systematic reviews flags several limitations and gaps:
- Most studies use quasi-experimental rather than randomized designs [10][11].
- K-12/high school populations are covered but elementary and early childhood, students with disabilities, rural, non-English-speaking, and underrepresented populations are rarely included [7][10][11][4].
- STEM subjects are far more studied than humanities or arts in these contexts [7][10][11].
- Most research focuses on psychological variables (motivation, trust, attitudes, self-regulation) rather than objective skill or academic achievement outcomes [10][11].
- Limited longitudinal evidence; most studies are short-term [7][10][11].
- Lack of reporting on subgroup effects by race, SES, ELL status, or disability [7][4][10].
- Risks identified include over-reliance on GenAI, possible reduction in critical thinking/deep processing, hallucinations, and motivational loss if not thoughtfully integrated [7][19][1].
- Teacher feedback is generally perceived as more reliable and contextual than GenAI alone [1].

Professional development for teachers and alignment between GenAI tools and subject learning goals are highlighted as critical for successful implementation [14][7][10][11].
