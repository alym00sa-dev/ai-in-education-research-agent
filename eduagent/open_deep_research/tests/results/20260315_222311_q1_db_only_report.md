## Section 1 — Research Synthesis

### Overview

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tools—including large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, image generators (e.g., DALL-E, Midjourney), and code generation platforms—are rapidly being integrated into secondary (high school) education settings worldwide. Their adoption aims to support the development of various student skills, including writing, critical thinking, creativity, collaboration, and subject-specific knowledge. Research from 2022–2025, including meta-analyses, systematic reviews, quasi-experimental studies, teacher surveys, and case studies, is beginning to map domains of impact, contextual moderators, and implementation challenges associated with these tools. The current synthesis presents a comprehensive, evidence-based review of the types of GenAI interventions deployed in high school contexts, the outcome domains most affected, factors moderating effectiveness, methodological limitations, and priority recommendations for future research and practice, with a focus on all subject areas and recent, peer-reviewed or official sources.

---

### Intervention Types and Mechanisms

**Text Generators (LLMs, e.g., ChatGPT, GPT-4):**  
Text generators are the most widely-adopted GenAI tools in secondary education, used extensively in language arts, social studies, and STEM. Their functions include generating essays, summaries, explanations, and reflections in response to student prompts, as well as supporting revision and scaffolding writing tasks. ChatGPT and its derivatives are especially prevalent since late 2022, with evidence documenting their widespread use among both students and teachers for academic tasks, writing improvement, critical reasoning exercises, and personalized tutoring [1][2][3][8].

**Image Generators (e.g., DALL-E, Midjourney):**  
In art, design, and creative writing classes, image generators are integrated to facilitate visual creativity, storytelling, and authentic project-based assignments. These tools automate or support the creation of digital artwork, allow for exploration of visual concepts, and serve as aids in iterative creative processes [4][5][6].

**Code Generators (e.g., GitHub Copilot, Code Interpreter):**  
AI-assisted coding tools are emerging in computing and digital technology curricula, enabling students to generate, debug, and understand code efficiently. These tools interpret natural language instructions and accelerate prototyping while posing questions around academic integrity and the depth of student understanding [2][5].

**Custom Quiz and Exercise Generators:**  
Authoring tools built atop GenAI platforms allow educators to rapidly produce personalized formative assessments, quizzes, and practice tasks. These tools contribute to differentiation, scaffolding, and formative feedback for students, with positive implications for academic outcomes when implementation is intentional [2][3].

**Other/Emerging Tools:**  
Early-stage experiments include multimodal GenAI (text and image/audio), translation, and integration with AR/VR for immersive or project-based experiences. However, empirical data on their impact in secondary classrooms is sparse [2][6].

**Mechanisms of Action:**  
GenAI tools affect learning through several routes:
- Providing immediate, personalized feedback, especially in writing and STEM [7][8]
- Supporting revision and deeper engagement by making iterative improvements visible [7]
- Scaffolding language, sense-making, and conceptual understanding in science [10][12][14]
- Facilitating creative expression and cross-disciplinary task completion [6][14]
- Enabling adaptive learning environments that can respond to individual student needs [9][12]
- Risking over-reliance, shortcutting, and academic dishonesty when unsupervised [3][4]

---

### Evidence on Effectiveness

**Subject-Specific Knowledge and Academic Achievement**  
Meta-analyses offer the strongest evidence of GenAI’s positive effect on subject knowledge and academic performance, especially in STEM and language disciplines:
- **Wang et al. (2024):** A meta-analysis of 45 studies (K-12 students, various designs) found consistent, positive effects of AI-enabled adaptive learning (including GenAI systems) on achievement and subject-specific knowledge, although specific effect sizes are not reported [9].
- **Li et al. (2025):** Meta-analysis in K-12 STEM education found AI-personalized interventions yielded positive achievement effects (effect size not detailed in extraction) [10], with adaptability and individualized feedback as mediators of learning gains.
- **Wu & Yu (2023):** Meta-analysis including AI chatbots (some generative models) showed statistically significant improvements in student learning outcomes across age groups (effect size magnitude not provided in extract). While not exclusive to secondary or GenAI tools, these results suggest educational chatbots enhance learning performance [12].
- **Tang et al. (2025):** A quasi-experimental study with high school students integrating Generative AI as teaching assistants found measurable improvements in assignment performance and engagement (exact effect sizes not reported), contingent on teacher supervision and structured use [14].

**Writing Skill, Revision, and Literacy**  
- **Mekheimer (2025):** RCT with 60 EFL (near-secondary) students demonstrated improved writing proficiency, greater revision frequency, and enhanced writing quality in groups receiving AI-assisted feedback compared to controls using only traditional teacher feedback (effect sizes not reported; p < .05 for writing quality measures) [7].
- **Zhang & Tur (2023):** Systematic review (13 studies, K-12) reported that GenAI tools fostered higher writing quality, particularly for struggling writers, with mixed educator perceptions regarding originality and the risk of plagiarism [11].
- **Picton et al. (2025):** Large national survey of UK secondary students found increasing use of GenAI for reading, writing, and comprehension tasks; however, the study is descriptive and does not estimate learning gains [8].

**Critical Thinking and Creativity**  
- Evidence for impact on critical thinking is mixed and context-sensitive:
  - Systematic and scoping reviews [11][13] describe both educator concerns (e.g., reduced critical engagement, risk of shortcutting) and distinct positive effects when GenAI is used in structured assignments (e.g., fact-checking AI outputs, critical evaluation tasks).
  - Case studies show that integrating GenAI for fact-checking and analysis assignments can foster deeper critical evaluation and creativity skills, but passive use may erode these competencies [15][11][14].
  - Studies of image generators in art and design contexts reveal their capacity to augment creative expression, though also raising concerns about dependency versus nurturance of originality [6].

**Collaboration and Social Skills**  
- Limited empirical data exist for GenAI’s impact on collaboration in the secondary context. One mixed-methods QED study (Sun, 2025) in language learning found AI-facilitated collaborative platforms (COIL) improved intercultural competence, with more robust evidence for language and cultural skills than for general teamwork [16].
- Reviews indicate indirect support for AI-assisted collaboration, but direct, experimentally-measured gains are infrequently reported [11][13].

**Engagement, Motivation, and Satisfaction**  
- Surveys and qualitative studies consistently report increased engagement, curiosity, and motivation among students using GenAI tools for assignments and creative work [8][14][17].
- Teacher surveys reveal mixed attitudes, with both excitement about personalization and apprehension about workload, academic integrity, and readiness [3][8][18].

---

### Demographic and Contextual Moderators

**Curriculum Integration and Teacher Mediation**  
- Effective integration is heightened when GenAI is embedded within curricular frameworks and supported by professional development. Case studies (e.g., science lesson study in Hong Kong) highlight that professional collaboration and alignment with instructional goals are key to realizing benefits [15].
- Teacher supervision is critical. Without structured mediation, adolescents are at risk of accepting misleading GenAI outputs and underdeveloping scrutinizing skills. Teacher-guided use promotes safer and more effective skill development [14].

**Equity and Access**  
- Equitable access to GenAI tools is highlighted as a prerequisite for effective implementation. Disparities in student and school technology access, digital literacy, and readiness limit who benefits from these tools [19][17].
- Policy frameworks underscore the importance of addressing technology gaps and ensuring all groups (including English Language Learners, students from low-income households) can leverage GenAI-enhanced learning opportunities [19][20].
- Studies note a lack of subgroup disaggregation. Published research rarely reports differential impacts by race, SES, or ELL status—representing a significant evidence gap [9][10][11][19].

**Implementation Patterns**  
- Teacher attitudes, school digital culture, and available support infrastructure significantly modulate GenAI’s effectiveness. Teachers’ openness, capacity for adaptation, and workload constraints influence adoption and classroom use [3][15][17].
- Fidelity of implementation (i.e., whether GenAI is used as intended, with adequate scaffolding and ethical clarity) correlates with stronger learning outcomes [15][14].

---

### Limitations and Research Gaps

The evidence base surrounding GenAI in secondary education is rapidly expanding, yet methodological and reporting limitations persist:
- **Outcome Measurement Gaps:** Many studies report only perception or descriptive implementation data; robust causal evidence (RCTs, quasi-experiments with effect sizes) is limited to writing and subject knowledge, especially in STEM [7][9][10][12][14].
- **Sparse Disaggregated and Longitudinal Data:** There is scant reporting on differential impacts for priority populations (race, SES, ELL), and virtually no longitudinal outcomes tracking sustained skill development [9][10][19].
- **Non-Randomized and Non-Representative Samples:** Many studies use mixed K-12 populations, small samples, or single-school case studies, reducing generalizability [15][14].
- **Effect Size and Confidence Intervals:** Most meta-analyses and RCTs do not report exact effect sizes or confidence intervals in reviewed summaries; reported improvements are generally positive but summary metrics are unavailable [9][10][11][12].
- **Collaboration and Creativity:** Current research on impact in creativity and collaboration relies on qualitative/theoretical studies, with few controlled, outcome-driven assessments [6][11][16].
- **Academic Integrity and Misuse:** Multiple sources flag risks of plagiarism, shortcutting, and inaccurate GenAI outputs, but these are discussed narratively rather than measured systematically [3][4][11][18].

---

### Recommendations and Future Directions

**For Practice:**
- Prioritize integrating GenAI within robust curricular and pedagogical frameworks; passive or unstructured adoption increases risk and diminishes impact [15][14][19].
- Invest in ongoing teacher professional development to support effective, ethical, and critical use of GenAI systems in classrooms [15][3][19].
- Ensure equitable access to GenAI technologies and adapt support to meet the needs of disadvantaged students and ELLs [19][17].
- Emphasize critical appraisal, ethical handling, and digital literacy as foundational outcomes when deploying GenAI [11][14][20].

**For Research:**
- Conduct large-scale, randomized controlled trials with robust reporting of effect sizes and subgroup outcomes, focusing on priority populations [9][10].
- Measure long-term impacts of GenAI integration on skill retention and transfer, especially in writing, critical thinking, and STEM domains.
- Systematically assess the effects of GenAI on collaboration, creativity, and social/emotional learning through mixed-methods or experimental designs [6][16].
- Develop validated, context-sensitive measures for GenAI-related skill outcomes and fidelity of implementation [20].

---

### Overall Evidence Confidence

The evidence base for GenAI’s effectiveness in supporting skill formation in secondary education is strongest for writing, revision, and subject-specific (notably STEM) knowledge, with multiple meta-analyses, systematic reviews, and experimental studies reporting positive learning outcomes. Effects on critical thinking, creativity, and collaboration are supported primarily by descriptive, qualitative, or small-scale interventions and remain highly dependent on instructional conditions. Evidence on subgroup effects, longitudinal outcomes, and reliable effect size estimation is weak or absent. Overall, confidence is "emerging" (EMERGING 🟡) for most domains, with moderate-to-strong confidence in writing and STEM when implementation is structured and supervised.
