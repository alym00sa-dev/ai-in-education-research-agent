## Executive Summary

The evidence base for generative AI tools in K-8 mathematics is still too thin to support a strong claim that these tools improve math outcomes relative to standard instruction, non-AI digital tools, or established human supports. The most relevant studies in the pool are dominated by systematic reviews, design papers, teacher-facing tools, and adjacent AI tutoring studies rather than direct student-outcome trials in elementary or middle school math. Where direct evidence does exist, it is mostly on hybrid or teacher-mediated systems rather than student-alone generative AI, and the clearest positive effects often come from broader AI tutoring or personalized practice systems rather than GenAI proper ((Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?, n.d.); (Tutor CoPilot, 2024); (Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring, 2024); (The effect of ChatGPT on students’ learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking, 2025)).

The strongest experimental and meta-analytic findings do show that AI-supported math learning can produce measurable gains under some conditions, but these findings are not yet specific enough to establish efficacy for K-8 student-facing generative AI. The K-12 intelligent tutoring system meta-analysis reports pooled benefits for learning outcomes, but the extracted profile does not provide a single pooled effect size in the source pool summary, and its evidence base is broader than GenAI and broader than K-8 math alone ((Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?, n.d.)). In a randomized trial of Tutor CoPilot with live K-12 math tutoring, students whose tutors had access to the generative-AI support were 4 percentage points more likely to pass their math exit ticket than controls, 62% versus 58% in the ITT analysis across 4,136 tutoring sessions, and the system also increased high-quality instructional prompting in real time ((Tutor CoPilot, 2024)). In a quasi-experimental three-site study of hybrid human-AI tutoring in middle school, adding human support to AI-driven math software improved process outcomes; site-level effects included β=0.202 and increases such as 0.36 more workspaces per hour, along with a weekly time-on-task increase from 24 to 33 minutes in one site ((Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring, 2024)). These are promising, but they are not clean tests of student-facing GenAI versus standard instruction.

The quasi-experimental and observational evidence generally points in the same direction: GenAI-like support appears most plausible when it is embedded in structured tutoring, teacher mediation, or tightly designed scaffolds. Tutor CoPilot improved tutors’ use of guiding questions and prompts to explain reasoning, suggesting a mechanism through which student learning could improve, but student survey outcomes were mostly null and the authors note that Socratic style could sometimes frustrate students ((Tutor CoPilot, 2024)). The hybrid tutoring studies similarly suggest that human oversight can increase engagement and work rate with software, which is more robustly evidenced than gains in deep conceptual understanding ((Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring, 2024)). By contrast, design and usability studies such as ChatGPT-MWPS for fifth-grade word problems report strong usability and engagement signals, including a correlation of r=0.969 with intention to use, but they do not establish causal learning gains because the profile does not report a controlled outcome estimate ((Design and evaluation of ChatGPT-MWPS, 2025)). Broader reviews of ChatGPT in mathematics and educational chatbots also emphasize heterogeneity, design variability, and unresolved safety and effectiveness questions rather than definitive K-8 math benefits ((Unveiling the potential, 2024); (A Scoping Survey of ChatGPT in Mathematics Education, 2025); (Interacting with educational chatbots, 2022)).

Across the tiered questions, Tier 1 is partially answered: the literature defines GenAI in math mainly as explanation, hinting, problem generation, feedback, and conversational tutoring, but the evidence base is still skewed toward older learners and broad AI categories rather than K-8 math specifically ((Current practices and future direction of artificial intelligence in mathematics education, 2025); (Unveiling the potential, 2024); (Digital learning using ChatGPT in elementary school mathematics learning, 2024)). Tier 2 is better answered: the relevant counterfactuals commonly include standard instruction, teacher-authored hints, human tutoring, adaptive software, and intelligent tutoring systems, with strong evidence that non-GenAI AI supports can help learning and engagement ((Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?, n.d.); (Question Personalization in an Intelligent Tutoring System, 2022); (Personalized Recommendations in EdTech, 2022)). Tier 3 is only partially answered: implementation studies show that teacher mediation, scaffold quality, and human oversight matter, but direct tests in K-8 classrooms are sparse ((Tutor CoPilot, 2024); (Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring, 2024)). Tier 4 remains largely unresolved for the exact focal question: there is not yet adequate direct experimental evidence that student-facing generative AI improves K-8 math achievement, conceptual understanding, procedural fluency, transfer, or retention relative to strong comparators. Overall confidence is low to moderate. The single most important caveat is that much of the positive evidence comes from broader AI tutoring, hybrid models, or design/feasibility studies rather than direct K-8 student-outcome trials of generative AI.

## Scope, Definitions, and the Shape of the Evidence

The literature retrieved for this question defines generative AI in mathematics mainly as systems that can explain, hint, generate worked examples or problems, provide feedback, and carry on conversational tutoring or coaching interactions ((Current practices and future direction of artificial intelligence in mathematics education, 2025); (Unveiling the potential, 2024); (A Scoping Survey of ChatGPT in Mathematics Education, 2025)). Across reviews, ChatGPT and related large language models are repeatedly framed as tools for individualized explanation, adaptive support, and rapid content generation, but the same reviews also emphasize that the field is still early, fragmented, and methodologically uneven, especially for school mathematics and younger students ((The effect of ChatGPT on students’ learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking, 2025); (Digital learning using ChatGPT in elementary school mathematics learning, 2024); (A Scoping Survey of ChatGPT in Mathematics Education, 2025)).

The main difficulty in answering the focal question is that the evidence base does not line up cleanly with the intervention definition. Many studies are about teacher-facing supports, general educational chatbots, or broader AI tutoring rather than student-facing generative AI in K-8 mathematics classrooms. The most rigorous studies in the pool are often not GenAI studies at all: they include traditional intelligent tutoring systems, personalized recommendation engines, and hybrid human-AI tutoring models ((Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?, n.d.); (Personalized Recommendations in EdTech, 2022); (Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring, 2024)). That matters because the strongest evidence for learning gains comes from systems that are structured, adaptive, and mediated, not from open-ended chat alone.

## Direct Experimental and Quasi-Experimental Evidence

### Tutor CoPilot: teacher-facing generative AI in live math tutoring

Tutor CoPilot is one of the strongest directly relevant experimental studies in the pool because it tests a generative-AI system in live K-12 math tutoring sessions, though the AI is used by tutors rather than directly by students (RCT, n=4,136 tutoring sessions; 1,800 students; 900 tutors at study launch, with 782 tutors in the launch sample). In the primary ITT analysis, students whose tutors had access to Tutor CoPilot were 4 percentage points more likely to pass their math exit ticket than control students, 62% versus 58% (Tutor CoPilot, 2024). The same study reports that the system improved the quality of real-time instructional prompting, with NLP analysis of more than 550,000 messages and 2,000+ system uses showing more guiding questions and prompts for student explanation, which aligns with deeper thinking and error correction even though the source pool summary does not provide a single standardized effect size for those behaviors ((Tutor CoPilot, 2024)).

The learning implication is important but limited. The study supports the idea that generative AI may help novice tutors provide stronger math support, and that this could move student performance modestly upward. However, it is not a test of direct student interaction with ChatGPT-like tools, and the outcome is a short-horizon exit-ticket measure rather than longer-term conceptual understanding or transfer ((Tutor CoPilot, 2024)). Student survey outcomes were mostly null, which suggests that the achievement gain was not accompanied by a clear broad shift in student perceptions in the extracted profile ((Tutor CoPilot, 2024)).

### Hybrid human-AI tutoring in middle school

Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring is a three-study quasi-experimental investigation in middle school mathematics, and it is one of the better corroborating studies because it examines a realistic support model rather than a laboratory-only prototype (QED, sites with n=125, n=385, and n=75). Across the three site studies, adding human tutor support to AI-driven math software improved process outcomes relative to software-only conditions. In Site 1, the effect is reported as β=0.202, and students produced 0.36 more workspaces per hour; in Site 2, weekly time on task rose from 24 to 33 minutes; and the authors describe a statistically significant increase in tutoring-related participation with CI=[0.057, 0.347] for the tutoring effect on time spent ((Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring, 2024)).

This study is not pure generative AI, but it is highly informative about the likely conditions under which AI support can matter in school mathematics. The evidence suggests that AI functions best as a scaffold inside a human-supervised tutoring workflow rather than as an autonomous replacement for instruction. The outcomes are intermediate rather than distal, so the study does not settle whether the model improves achievement, conceptual understanding, or transfer. It does, however, strengthen the case that AI-assisted math learning depends on implementation and adult mediation, not just model capability ((Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring, 2024)).

### ChatGPT-generated hints and algebra learning

Two experimental studies in the pool offer additional but indirect evidence because they involve adult learners rather than K-8 students. In ChatGPT-generated help produces learning gains equivalent to human tutor-authored help on mathematics skills, the authors report statistically significant learning gains on mathematics skills in the overall sample, but the extracted profile does not provide the exact effect size; the analyzed sample was n=274 overall, with n=98 in the ChatGPT condition (RCT, adult sample) ((ChatGPT-generated help produces learning gains equivalent to human tutor-authored help on mathematics skills, 2024)). In Learning gain differences between ChatGPT and human tutor generated algebra hints, both groups showed positive pre-to-post learning gains, but the summary indicates substantially higher gains for the human-authored hint condition and does not report the exact numeric effect in the extracted profile (RCT, n=77, adult sample) ((Learning gain differences between ChatGPT and human tutor generated algebra hints, 2023)).

These adult studies matter only as mechanism-level evidence. They suggest that ChatGPT can produce educational hints that are at least capable of supporting learning under controlled conditions, but the direct applicability to K-8 students is limited. Children’s developmental needs, prerequisite knowledge, and vulnerability to misleading explanations make it inappropriate to infer K-8 efficacy from these adult algebra studies alone ((ChatGPT-generated help produces learning gains equivalent to human tutor-authored help on mathematics skills, 2024); (Learning gain differences between ChatGPT and human tutor generated algebra hints, 2023)).

## Meta-Analytic and Review Evidence on Generative AI and Closely Related AI Supports

The broadest synthesis in the pool, The effect of ChatGPT on students’ learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking, is a meta-analysis of 51 quasi-experimental and experimental studies published from late 2022 through early 2025 (meta-analysis, 51 studies). Its scope includes primary through college but is predominantly undergraduate and college, so it is not a clean K-8 math meta-analysis ((The effect of ChatGPT on students’ learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking, 2025)). Even so, it is useful because it indicates that the field is beginning to accumulate experimental evidence on ChatGPT and student learning. The key limitation is that the source pool summary does not provide a pooled effect size for K-8 mathematics, and the population mismatch is substantial. This means the review can support a claim that ChatGPT-like tools are being studied for learning, but not that they reliably improve elementary or middle school math outcomes.

A more directly relevant synthesis is Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students? A meta-analysis and evaluation of heterogeneity of treatment effects in the U.S. This review is highly important because it spans elementary, middle, and high school and focuses on learning outcomes in K-12 settings (meta-analysis, K-12 U.S.). The source pool summary states that the paper estimates whether ITS improve learning outcomes and examines moderators such as study design, student characteristics, intervention features, outcome type, and setting, but it does not include a pooled effect size in the extracted text ((Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?, n.d.)). As a result, this review is strong evidence that structured AI tutoring systems have a measurable learning literature in K-12, yet it still does not directly answer whether generative AI specifically outperforms standard instruction in K-8 mathematics.

The broader mathematics-specific review literature reinforces that the field is active but not settled. Unveiling the potential: A systematic review of ChatGPT in transforming mathematics teaching and learning synthesizes 31 studies and highlights learning, engagement, and broader educational experiences, but it is not a K-8-only outcome review and the source pool summary does not provide pooled effects (meta-analysis, 31 studies) ((Unveiling the potential, 2024)). A Scoping Survey of ChatGPT in Mathematics Education similarly maps benefits and challenges, but the result is descriptive rather than causal and does not establish efficacy for student math outcomes ((A Scoping Survey of ChatGPT in Mathematics Education, 2025)). Current practices and future direction of artificial intelligence in mathematics education also functions as a broad review of AI uses in math education across grade bands and tool types rather than a direct estimate of GenAI impact on K-8 learning ((Current practices and future direction of artificial intelligence in mathematics education, 2025)).

## K-8-Relevant Design and Feasibility Studies

Several retrieved studies are highly relevant to K-8 math, but they do not yet provide rigorous causal outcome evidence. Digital learning using ChatGPT in elementary school mathematics learning is a systematic review focused on elementary mathematics and ChatGPT use; it is important because it indicates that primary-grade work exists, but the extracted summary does not report a controlled effect estimate or a pooled achievement finding (meta-analysis, elementary) ((Digital learning using ChatGPT in elementary school mathematics learning, 2024)). Design and evaluation of ChatGPT-MWPS is more concrete: it targets fifth-grade word problem solving, reports usability evidence from n=52 students, and shows a strong positive correlation of r=0.969 between cognitive engagement and intention to use (QED/design study, n=52). The system was designed to support step-by-step reasoning, adaptive scaffolding, and learner autonomy, but the profile does not provide a controlled achievement estimate, so this is evidence of promise and acceptability rather than proven learning impact ((Design and evaluation of ChatGPT-MWPS, 2025)).

The elementary rational-number game study, Improving rational number knowledge using the NanoRoboMath digital game, is not generative AI, but it helps anchor the comparison literature because it shows what a relatively well-specified digital intervention can do in primary math (QED, n=195; regression analyses on n=110 experimental-group students). The intervention produced a small positive effect on conceptual rational-number knowledge, with partial eta-squared values of ηp²=0.04 for conceptual knowledge and ηp²=0.03 for operations, while the adaptive-knowledge interaction was not significant ((Improving rational number knowledge using the NanoRoboMath digital game, 2022)). This is useful context because it shows that modest gains are possible from structured digital math supports, but it also underscores how much stronger the evidence base is for non-GenAI interventions than for student-facing generative AI in K-8 math.

## Counterfactuals: What GenAI Is Being Compared Against

The relevant counterfactuals in this literature are varied. Some studies compare GenAI-like or AI-enhanced tools with software-only conditions, as in the hybrid tutoring study, while others compare AI-generated help against human-authored help, as in the algebra hint studies ((Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring, 2024); (ChatGPT-generated help produces learning gains equivalent to human tutor-authored help on mathematics skills, 2024); (Learning gain differences between ChatGPT and human tutor generated algebra hints, 2023)). Still others compare personalized AI recommendations with status-quo curation in education apps, though not math-specific. In Personalized Recommendations in EdTech, a randomized trial in a reading app with n=7,750 shows that personalized recommendations increased total story engagement by 14% across app sections and by 60% within the recommended section, demonstrating that personalization can affect participation even when math learning is not under study (RCT, n=7,750) ((Personalized Recommendations in EdTech, 2022)).

Within tutoring systems, personalization of question phrasing also matters. Question Personalization in an Intelligent Tutoring System reports that personalized question phrasing in the Korbit ITS improved exercise performance in an A/B test with expected n=190, non-expected n=139, and control n=141, and it reduced skip rates in the matched group (QED/A/B test, n=470 total) ((Question Personalization in an Intelligent Tutoring System, 2022)). This is not GenAI in the narrowest sense, but it is directly relevant to the causal architecture of tutoring: language, pacing, and prompt design can shape engagement and performance. That point is central because GenAI’s value may lie less in raw intelligence and more in improved interaction quality.

## Mechanisms, Implementation Conditions, and Practical Use

Across studies, the most plausible mechanisms for GenAI in math are individualized explanation, step-by-step hinting, immediate feedback, and metacognitive prompting. Tutor CoPilot provides the clearest implementation evidence: the system made novice tutors more likely to ask guiding questions and prompt students to explain reasoning, indicating that the likely mechanism is not autonomous AI teaching but improved human pedagogical behavior ((Tutor CoPilot, 2024)). The hybrid tutoring study similarly suggests that human oversight can channel AI-supported practice into more sustained engagement and more work completed per hour, which is consistent with the idea that structure and accountability matter ((Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring, 2024)).

Evidence on student-alone use in K-8 mathematics is much thinner. The fifth-grade ChatGPT-MWPS study suggests that a well-designed tool may support step-by-step reasoning and intent to use, but the extracted profile does not tell us how often students used it, whether use was integrated into lessons or homework, or whether the tool improved actual achievement beyond usability outcomes ((Design and evaluation of ChatGPT-MWPS, 2025)). This is a major gap because implementation conditions such as teacher mediation, prompt quality, access to devices, and curriculum alignment likely determine whether GenAI helps or misleads students. The review Interacting with educational chatbots underscores that interaction styles, educational roles, and deployment contexts matter, but it does not resolve how those factors translate into K-8 math gains ((Interacting with educational chatbots, 2022)).

The evidence also raises caution about the limits of open-ended GenAI. The adult algebra and tutorial studies show that ChatGPT-generated help can support learning, but not necessarily as well as human-authored help in every setting, and the source pool includes multiple reviews warning that outcome claims are highly heterogeneous and often tied to careful instructional design rather than the AI model alone ((Learning gain differences between ChatGPT and human tutor generated algebra hints, 2023); (The effect of ChatGPT on students’ learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking, 2025); (Unveiling the potential, 2024)). For K-8 students, this means that guardrails, teacher oversight, and age-appropriate scaffolds are likely to be more important than in adult settings.

## What the Evidence Says About Outcomes

Achievement is the most weakly established outcome for direct K-8 generative AI use. The strongest direct student outcome in the retrieved pool is Tutor CoPilot’s 4 percentage-point gain in exit-ticket pass rates, but this is mediated through tutor behavior, not student-alone GenAI ((Tutor CoPilot, 2024)). In middle school, hybrid human-AI tutoring improved process outcomes and time on task, but not a clearly reported standardized achievement effect in the extracted summary ((Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring, 2024)). For conceptual understanding and transfer, the best evidence in the pool is still indirect or from non-K-8 samples. For example, the high school LearnLM tutoring RCT reports a 5.5 percentage-point increase for knowledge transfer and strong immediate correction outcomes, but it is outside the target age band ((AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students, 2025)).

Engagement and usability are better represented than achievement. ChatGPT-MWPS reports strong cognitive engagement and intention to use, with r=0.969, and the chatbot and personalization literature repeatedly finds engagement gains when systems are well matched to learners and tasks ((Design and evaluation of ChatGPT-MWPS, 2025); (Personalized Recommendations in EdTech, 2022); (Question Personalization in an Intelligent Tutoring System, 2022)). But engagement is not the same as learning. The evidence base does not yet show that higher engagement from GenAI in K-8 math consistently translates into stronger conceptual understanding, procedural fluency, problem solving, or retention.

## Differential Effects and Equity

Differential effects by grade level, prior achievement, language status, or disability status are largely unresolved in the retrieved evidence. The K-12 ITS meta-analysis explicitly examines heterogeneity of treatment effects, but the source pool summary does not provide the subgroup results needed to answer the question directly ((Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?, n.d.)). The GenAI-specific literature in the pool is even thinner on subgroup analysis. The reviewed studies do not provide adequate evidence on multilingual learners, students with disabilities, rural students, or low-SES learners in K-8 mathematics. That absence is consequential because access and developmental appropriateness are likely to vary substantially across groups.

The closest relevant point is that implementation quality matters and may interact with learner readiness. Personalized question phrasing in the ITS study improved performance and reduced skip rates when questions matched student level, suggesting that fit and cognitive load can shape engagement and outcomes ((Question Personalization in an Intelligent Tutoring System, 2022)). Likewise, the adult modelling study shows that students prefer competent AI roles over a struggling-student role, and that confidence, helpfulness, and ease of interaction vary meaningfully by AI behavior ((Investigating Students’ Preferences for AI Roles in Mathematical Modelling, 2025)). Although this is not K-8 outcome evidence, it suggests that student perception of AI competence may shape use patterns and potentially magnify inequities if some learners are more likely to be confused or discouraged by poorly tuned systems.

## Synthesis and Implications

The cross-study pattern is clear even though the direct K-8 GenAI evidence is sparse: generative AI is most promising when it supports human instruction rather than replacing it, and when it is constrained by strong pedagogical design. The strongest positive signals come from tutor-facing support, hybrid tutoring, and highly structured hinting or personalization systems rather than open-ended student chat alone ((Tutor CoPilot, 2024); (Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring, 2024); (Question Personalization in an Intelligent Tutoring System, 2022)). Reviews of ChatGPT in mathematics and educational chatbots reinforce that the field is still exploratory and that effectiveness depends on implementation, not just model access ((Unveiling the potential, 2024); (A Scoping Survey of ChatGPT in Mathematics Education, 2025); (Interacting with educational chatbots, 2022)).

For schools and practitioners, the practical implication is caution with selective adoption. The evidence is not strong enough to recommend student-alone generative AI as a replacement for teacher instruction, tutoring, or established adaptive math systems in K-8. A more defensible use case is teacher-mediated support for explanation, hinting, and feedback, especially in after-school, tutoring, or homework-help contexts where the interaction can be monitored and corrected. Even then, the evidence base suggests that benefits are likely to be modest, contingent on high-quality prompts, and dependent on the surrounding instructional system rather than the AI tool itself ((Tutor CoPilot, 2024); (Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring, 2024); (Design and evaluation of ChatGPT-MWPS, 2025)).

## Limitations and Research Gaps

The evidence base has major methodological weaknesses. Most retrieved studies are reviews, design studies, or adjacent AI interventions rather than direct K-8 generative AI outcome trials. Even the strongest relevant experiments are either teacher-facing, hybrid, or outside the K-8 age range, which limits causal inference for the focal population and intervention. Sample sizes vary widely, but the strongest K-8-relevant evidence is still sparse, short-term, and often focused on process outcomes instead of durable learning.

There are also important equity and external-validity gaps. The pool provides little direct evidence on primary grades, students with disabilities, multilingual learners, or low-SES settings. There is almost no direct evidence on homework help, family-mediated use, or after-school support, despite those being plausible and important deployment contexts. The literature also does not yet provide strong evidence about hallucination rates, accuracy under authentic classroom use, or whether GenAI reduces productive struggle or fosters dependency in young learners.

Finally, the comparator problem remains unresolved. Many of the strongest non-GenAI supports, including intelligent tutoring systems, personalized recommendations, and structured human tutoring, already show benefits or engagement gains in K-12 settings ((Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?, n.d.); (Personalized Recommendations in EdTech, 2022); (Question Personalization in an Intelligent Tutoring System, 2022)). That means any claim that generative AI “works” for K-8 math must be tested against strong baselines, not business-as-usual. At present, the field is still in an early-to-emerging stage for the exact question of whether student-facing generative AI improves K-8 mathematics outcomes.
