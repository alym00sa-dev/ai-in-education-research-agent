## Executive Summary

The evidence base indicates that tutoring can improve K-8 mathematics outcomes, but the strength of the evidence depends heavily on the tutoring model, the outcome measured, and the population studied. The most defensible conclusion is not that “tutoring” in general reliably raises math achievement, but that structured, individualized, and well-supported tutoring approaches can produce positive math gains, while the size and reliability of those gains remain uneven across peer tutoring, AI-supported tutoring, and human-led models. The best evidence in the retrieved pool is concentrated in meta-analyses of peer tutoring and targeted experimental studies of AI-augmented or technology-supported tutoring, with far fewer clean RCTs of adult-led K-8 math tutoring than the policy debate would suggest (Bowman-Perrott et al., 2016)(n.d., “Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?”)(n.d., “Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI- Math Tutor in Ghana”)[7].

The strongest experimental findings are positive but heterogeneous. In the AI math tutor study in Ghana, an RCT with 477 middle-school students found that access to Rori increased math growth, with treatment mean growth score 5.13 versus 2.12 for control and an effect size of d=0.36 (n=477; n.d., “Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI- Math Tutor in Ghana”). In the Tutor CoPilot preregistered RCT, access to human-AI guidance increased the likelihood that students passed their math exit tickets by 4 percentage points overall, with subgroup effects of +9 percentage points and +7 percentage points in some groups; the study also found no significant differences on student survey outcomes for motivation, perceived tutor care, or confidence (RCT, n=4,136 tutoring sessions; n=1,787–1,800 students; 782 tutors at launch; 2025, “Tutor CoPilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time Expertise”). In the hybrid human-AI tutoring investigation, three quasi-experimental middle-school studies reported improved intermediate math learning relative to software alone, but the reported estimates were not standardized across all sites, limiting direct synthesis; the same paper also reported increased engagement, including time on task and software use (QED, n=125; n=385; n=75; 2024, “Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring: A Three-Study Quasi-Experimental Investigation”). A broader meta-analysis of peer tutoring in elementary, middle, and high school settings found that experimental design matters for estimated effects, underscoring that less rigorous studies tend to overstate impacts and that peer tutoring effects are not uniformly large or stable (meta-analysis, 2016, “The influence of experimental design on the magnitude of the effect size - peer tutoring for elementary, middle and high school settings: A meta-analysis”). The intelligent tutoring systems meta-analysis likewise reports that ITSs benefit K-12 students and examines heterogeneity by design, setting, assessment type, and instructional features, but the extracted pool does not report a single pooled estimate in the available summary, so the precise magnitude cannot be stated here (meta-analysis, n.d., “Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students? A meta-analysis and evaluation of heterogeneity of treatment effects in the U.S.”).

The quasi-experimental and observational evidence broadly corroborates the experimental pattern that more responsive, better-supported tutoring tends to outperform weaker baselines, but it also reveals implementation constraints and model-specific limitations. The mixed-methods study of synchronous online tutoring for low-achieving rural middle school students reported improved achievement, but the extracted summary does not provide a standardized effect size, which limits direct comparison with the RCT evidence (mixed-methods, 2015, “An Examination of an Online Tutoring Program’s Impact on Low-Achieving Middle School Students’ Mathematics Achievement”). The cognitive tutoring neuroimaging study found that eight weeks of 1:1 math tutoring significantly improved arithmetic problem-solving accuracy in children with mathematical learning disabilities, with d=0.86 in the in-scanner arithmetic verification task, indicating that intensive individualized tutoring can be especially beneficial for students with pronounced math difficulties, though the sample was very small (QED, n=15 MLD; n=15 TD; 2015, “Cognitive tutoring induces widespread neuroplasticity and remediates brain function in children with mathematical learning disabilities”). The parent-coaching trial in the Philippines also found that numeracy outcomes can improve substantially when tutoring-like support is extended into the home environment, but this is indirect evidence for tutoring rather than direct evidence on school-based K-8 tutoring models (RCT, n=94; 2024, “Effects of Online Parent Coaching on Filipino Children’s Skills”). Across studies, implementation features such as tutor guidance, curriculum alignment, and dosage appear to matter, but the evidence is not yet strong enough to specify a single optimal dosage threshold or to identify a stable hierarchy of tutor types for K-8 math.

Across the tiered questions, the evidence answers Tier 1 only partially and Tier 2 and Tier 3 incompletely. The field defines tutoring in many ways—peer tutoring, intelligent tutoring systems, human-AI tutoring, synchronous online tutoring, and 1:1 intensive tutoring—but these are not interchangeable, and the evidence pool does not support collapsing them into one construct (Bowman-Perrott et al., 2016)(n.d., “Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?”)[7]. Outcome coverage is also uneven: math achievement and proximal platform performance are common, while standardized test scores, conceptual understanding, and problem-solving are much less consistently reported in the extracted summaries (2024, “Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring: A Three-Study Quasi-Experimental Investigation”; 2018, “SKOPE-IT (Shareable Knowledge Objects as Portable Intelligent Tutors): overlaying natural language tutoring on an adaptive learning system for mathematics”). Tier 4 is partially answered: tutoring can improve K-8 math outcomes, with evidence strongest for structured individualized or AI-supported tutoring and weaker or more design-sensitive for peer tutoring, but the field still lacks enough clean head-to-head trials, robust middle-school-only studies, and well-powered subgroup analyses to identify the best model with high confidence. The single most important caveat is that much of the positive evidence comes from small, hybrid, or proximal-outcome studies rather than large-scale, directly comparable RCTs against classroom business-as-usual.

## Evidence Base and Study Types

### What counts as tutoring in this literature

The retrieved evidence treats tutoring as a family of interventions rather than a single model. Peer tutoring is represented by a dedicated meta-analysis spanning elementary, middle, and high school settings, and the review’s central message is that estimated effects depend on experimental design quality (Bowman-Perrott et al., 2016). Intelligent tutoring systems are represented by a separate meta-analysis that explicitly examines heterogeneity by study design, student population, setting, assessment type, and instructional features, suggesting that “tutoring” can range from software-driven adaptive guidance to human-mediated instruction (n.d., “Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?”). The pool also includes hybrid human-AI tutoring, live tutor support systems, synchronous online tutoring, and intensive one-to-one tutoring for students with mathematical learning disabilities, which together show that the term includes both direct human instruction and technology-mediated scaffolding [8][7][23][24].

### Populations and grade bands

The strongest K-8-relevant evidence in the pool clusters around elementary and middle school. Middle school appears in the Rori AI tutor RCT, the hybrid human-AI QED, and the synchronous online tutoring study, while elementary and elementary-plus-middle-school coverage appears in the peer tutoring meta-analysis, the ITS meta-analysis, Tutor CoPilot, and the parent-coaching RCT that includes early numeracy rather than school-age math (n.d., “Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI- Math Tutor in Ghana”)[8][23](Bowman-Perrott et al., 2016)(n.d., “Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?”)[20]. The evidence is especially thin for grades 6-8 relative to K-5, and the pool contains only limited direct subgroup evidence for students with disabilities or very low prior achievement. The most explicit subgroup-relevant result is the neuroplasticity study, which focuses on children with mathematical learning disabilities and reports large arithmetic gains after 8 weeks of 1:1 tutoring (2015, “Cognitive tutoring induces widespread neuroplasticity and remediates brain function in children with mathematical learning disabilities”).

## Comparative Effects on Math Outcomes

### Meta-analytic evidence

The peer tutoring meta-analysis concludes that experimental design influences estimated effect size in peer tutoring studies across elementary, middle, and high school settings, which implies that pooled estimates from less rigorous designs may overstate true impacts (Bowman-Perrott et al., 2016). The available source summary does not provide the pooled effect size, so the exact magnitude cannot be reported here. Even so, this is important comparative evidence because it indicates that peer tutoring is promising but methodologically fragile, and that design quality must be part of any policy judgment (Bowman-Perrott et al., 2016).

The ITS meta-analysis similarly indicates that intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students and evaluates heterogeneity by study design, student population, setting, assessment type, and instructional features, but the extracted summary does not include a pooled effect size or a complete moderator table (n.d., “Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?”). As a result, the meta-analysis supports the direction of effect but not a precise quantitative hierarchy across conditions. That matters for K-8 math because many contemporary tutoring models are now software-assisted or AI-assisted, yet the evidence pool does not permit a single average effect to stand in for all such systems (n.d., “Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?”).

### Randomized controlled trials

The strongest directly quantitative K-8 math tutoring result in the pool comes from the Ghana AI math tutor RCT. In that study, students who received access to Rori had mean growth scores of 5.13 compared with 2.12 for controls, corresponding to d=0.36 over an 8-month study period (RCT, n=477; n.d., “Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI- Math Tutor in Ghana”). This is direct causal evidence that an AI-mediated tutoring model can improve middle-school math learning in a low-resource context. The study is especially relevant because it addresses scalability and access, two problems that dominate real-world tutoring delivery in K-8 math (n.d., “Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI- Math Tutor in Ghana”).

The Tutor CoPilot preregistered RCT examined a human-AI tutoring support system designed to guide novice tutors in real time during live math tutoring sessions. In 4,136 tutoring sessions involving 782 tutors at launch and approximately 1,787–1,800 students, access to Tutor CoPilot increased the likelihood that students passed their math exit tickets by 4 percentage points overall, with subgroup effects of +9 percentage points and +7 percentage points reported in the extracted summary (RCT, n=4,136 sessions; 2025, “Tutor CoPilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time Expertise”). The same study found no statistically significant differences in student survey outcomes for motivation, perceived tutor care, or confidence in learning math, which suggests that the intervention’s main benefit may come through improved tutoring behavior rather than through immediate changes in student self-perceptions (2025, “Tutor CoPilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time Expertise”). This is relevant evidence for implementation because it links tutor support tools to student performance without claiming broad affective change.

The parent-coaching RCT from the Philippines is not a school tutoring study, but it is useful as indirect evidence about tutoring-like support and numeracy gains. In a sample of 94 families, the online synchronous coaching intervention improved all measured numeracy outcomes, including numeral identification, object counting, rote counting, missing number identification, numerical magnitude comparison, and addition/subtraction-related skills (RCT, n=94; 2024, “Effects of Online Parent Coaching on Filipino Children’s Skills”). Because the extracted summary does not report effect sizes for each numeracy outcome, the study cannot be used to quantify the magnitude of effect; it does, however, support the broader claim that guided adult support can improve early math skills when implementation is structured and practice-oriented (2024, “Effects of Online Parent Coaching on Filipino Children’s Skills”).

The SKOPE-IT trial is important mainly as a cautionary comparison. In an undergraduate sample of 76, the randomized comparison of tutoring-enhanced versus standard ALEKS explanations showed no statistically significant difference in ALEKS learning gains, although the summary reports d=0.2 and a regression coefficient of 8.0 (RCT, n=76; 2018, “SKOPE-IT (Shareable Knowledge Objects as Portable Intelligent Tutors): overlaying natural language tutoring on an adaptive learning system for mathematics”). Because this is not K-8 and because the setting is undergraduate, it should not be generalized directly to K-8 policy; still, it demonstrates that adding dialogic tutoring layers to adaptive software does not automatically improve learning (2018, “SKOPE-IT (Shareable Knowledge Objects as Portable Intelligent Tutors): overlaying natural language tutoring on an adaptive learning system for mathematics”).

### Quasi-experimental and mixed-methods evidence

The hybrid human-AI tutoring investigation provides stronger K-8 relevance than the undergraduate SKOPE-IT trial. Across three quasi-experimental middle-school studies, hybrid tutoring improved intermediate mathematics learning outcomes compared with software alone, and it also increased engagement, including time on task and IXL usage in Site 1 (QED, n=125; n=385; n=75; 2024, “Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring: A Three-Study Quasi-Experimental Investigation”). The summary indicates a significant effect on time spent in Site 1, β=0.202, 95% CI [0.057, 0.347], and a rise in weekly usage in Site 2 from 24 to 33 minutes, while Site 3 reported a progress estimate with CI [0.02, 0.70] (2024, “Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring: A Three-Study Quasi-Experimental Investigation”). Because the reported outcome metrics differ by site and are not all standardized, this study supports the direction of effect but not a pooled standardized estimate.

The mixed-methods rural middle-school online tutoring study also supports a positive direction for math tutoring, with synchronous online tutoring improving achievement for low-achieving students in two rural schools (mixed-methods, 2015, “An Examination of an Online Tutoring Program’s Impact on Low-Achieving Middle School Students’ Mathematics Achievement”). The extracted summary does not report the numerical effect size, so its value is interpretive rather than quantitative. Its importance is contextual: it shows that one-to-one synchronous tutoring can be feasible in rural settings and may help low-achieving middle-school students, a subgroup that is highly relevant to policy but still underrepresented in the broader experimental literature (2015, “An Examination of an Online Tutoring Program’s Impact on Low-Achieving Middle School Students’ Mathematics Achievement”).

The 1:1 cognitive tutoring study for children with mathematical learning disabilities provides some of the clearest evidence on intensive individualized support. Eight weeks of 1:1 math tutoring significantly improved arithmetic problem-solving accuracy, and the in-scanner arithmetic verification task showed d=0.86 in the MLD group (QED, n=15 MLD; n=15 TD; 2015, “Cognitive tutoring induces widespread neuroplasticity and remediates brain function in children with mathematical learning disabilities”). Although the sample is very small, the study is important because it directly addresses a high-need subgroup and shows that intensive tutoring can have large effects when learners have clear skill deficits and receive focused support (2015, “Cognitive tutoring induces widespread neuroplasticity and remediates brain function in children with mathematical learning disabilities”).

## Tutor Type, Dosage, and Delivery Conditions

### Human tutors, peer tutors, and technology-mediated tutors

The available evidence does not support a single ranking of tutor types across K-8 math. Peer tutoring has a sizable literature, but the meta-analysis warns that weaker designs inflate effects, so the apparent benefits of peer tutoring should be interpreted cautiously (Bowman-Perrott et al., 2016). Human-AI tutoring and AI-supported tutoring, by contrast, show positive experimental and quasi-experimental results in middle school math, including the Rori RCT and the Tutor CoPilot trial, suggesting that AI can extend or amplify tutoring capacity when used as an instructional scaffold rather than a stand-alone replacement for human support (n.d., “Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI- Math Tutor in Ghana”; 2025, “Tutor CoPilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time Expertise”). The hybrid human-AI QED further suggests that combining human tutoring with software can improve engagement and intermediate learning, but it stops short of proving superiority over well-implemented human tutoring alone (2024, “Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring: A Three-Study Quasi-Experimental Investigation”).

### Dosage and intensity

Dosage is a recurring implementation theme, but the retrieved summaries do not support a firm minimum effective dose for K-8 math tutoring. The Rori study ran over 8 months and produced d=0.36, while the cognitive tutoring study delivered 8 weeks of intensive 1:1 tutoring and produced d=0.86 on arithmetic verification accuracy in children with MLD (n.d., “Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI- Math Tutor in Ghana”; 2015, “Cognitive tutoring induces widespread neuroplasticity and remediates brain function in children with mathematical learning disabilities”). Tutor CoPilot worked at the session level, showing improved exit-ticket pass rates across 4,136 sessions but not reporting a dose-response curve (2025, “Tutor CoPilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time Expertise”). The literature therefore suggests that both duration and intensity matter, but it does not yet identify a robust dosage threshold at which tutoring becomes reliably effective for K-8 math.

### Mechanisms

Across the pool, the most plausible mechanisms are individualized feedback, increased time on task, and more responsive problem solving. Tutor CoPilot explicitly reports changes in tutor behavior, including more prompting of student explanations and more guiding questions, with approximately 2 standard deviations on the log-odds scale for selected strategies, which supports a mechanism of improved instructional interactions rather than mere exposure (2025, “Tutor CoPilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time Expertise”). The hybrid human-AI QED similarly reports more engagement and software use, implying that tutoring may work partly by increasing sustained effort and practice opportunities (2024, “Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring: A Three-Study Quasi-Experimental Investigation”). The cognitive tutoring study adds a mechanistic lens by showing neuroplasticity associated with math remediation, suggesting that intensive tutoring can alter not only performance but also underlying processing in children with MLD (2015, “Cognitive tutoring induces widespread neuroplasticity and remediates brain function in children with mathematical learning disabilities”).

## Standard Comparators and Baseline Supports

### Business-as-usual and software-only comparators

The comparator condition in most of the retrieved studies is business-as-usual or software-only support rather than another tutoring model. The Rori RCT compared access to an AI tutor with control, the hybrid human-AI studies compared hybrid tutoring against math software alone, and Tutor CoPilot evaluated tutor support in live tutoring sessions rather than comparing one tutoring model to another in a pure head-to-head design (n.d., “Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI- Math Tutor in Ghana”)[8][7]. This is a major limitation for policy because schools need to know whether tutoring is better than alternative supports such as small-group remediation, extended time, or computer-assisted practice.

The SKOPE-IT trial provides a rare direct comparison between a tutoring-enhanced adaptive system and standard adaptive explanations, and it found no statistically significant difference in ALEKS learning gains (RCT, n=76; 2018, “SKOPE-IT (Shareable Knowledge Objects as Portable Intelligent Tutors): overlaying natural language tutoring on an adaptive learning system for mathematics”). Although the population is not K-8, the result underscores an important baseline point: simply adding conversational tutoring to software does not guarantee added value unless the tutoring interaction is tightly aligned to learner needs and the broader instructional design is strong (2018, “SKOPE-IT (Shareable Knowledge Objects as Portable Intelligent Tutors): overlaying natural language tutoring on an adaptive learning system for mathematics”).

## Outcomes Measured

### Achievement, fluency, and problem solving

Math achievement is the most common outcome, but the operationalization varies widely. The Rori study uses growth scores, the Tutor CoPilot study uses exit-ticket pass rates, the hybrid human-AI studies focus on intermediate learning outcomes and platform-based measures, and the cognitive tutoring study uses arithmetic problem-solving accuracy (n.d., “Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI- Math Tutor in Ghana”)[7][8][24]. These are all legitimate math outcomes, but they are not interchangeable. Exit tickets and platform proficiency are proximal measures, whereas standardized achievement tests and durable transfer outcomes would provide stronger evidence for policy; the extracted summaries include fewer of those latter measures than would be ideal.

### Affective and engagement outcomes

Affective outcomes are secondary and mixed. Tutor CoPilot did not produce significant changes in student motivation, perceived tutor care, or confidence (2025, “Tutor CoPilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time Expertise”). The hybrid human-AI study, however, did increase time on task and software use, which is an engagement signal rather than an attitudinal one (2024, “Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring: A Three-Study Quasi-Experimental Investigation”). The parent-coaching study also found that parental involvement was positively associated with gains on several numeracy outcomes, implying that adult engagement may help tutoring-like interventions work better, although this is indirect evidence for school tutoring (2024, “Effects of Online Parent Coaching on Filipino Children’s Skills”).

## Evidence by Subgroup and Equity-Relevant Conditions

### Low prior achievers and students with learning disabilities

The strongest subgroup-relevant evidence in the pool concerns low-achieving students and students with mathematical learning disabilities. The rural middle-school online tutoring study explicitly targeted low-achieving students and reported achievement gains from synchronous online tutoring (mixed-methods, 2015, “An Examination of an Online Tutoring Program’s Impact on Low-Achieving Middle School Students’ Mathematics Achievement”). The cognitive tutoring study found large gains for children with MLD after eight weeks of 1:1 tutoring, with d=0.86 on an arithmetic verification task (QED, n=15 MLD; 2015, “Cognitive tutoring induces widespread neuroplasticity and remediates brain function in children with mathematical learning disabilities”). Together, these studies suggest that tutoring may be especially valuable for students who begin far behind, but the evidence is too limited to say that the effect is larger than for other students across all K-8 settings.

### Rural and low-resource contexts

The tutoring evidence is unusually relevant to rural and low-resource settings. The Botswana phone-and-SMS learning study, while not tutoring per se, shows that low-tech adult-supported instruction can improve numeracy by 0.121 standard deviations in a very large RCT of 4,550 households, reinforcing the idea that lightweight delivery systems can work when direct access to instruction is constrained (RCT, n=4,550 households; 2022, “Experimental evidence on learning using low-tech when school is out”). The Rori AI tutor trial in Ghana extends this lesson by showing that a WhatsApp-based tutor can yield d=0.36 in middle-school math growth (RCT, n=477; n.d., “Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI- Math Tutor in Ghana”). These are not substitutes for school tutoring evidence, but they matter because they show how tutoring-like support may reach underserved populations at scale.

## Synthesis and Implications

The cross-study pattern is clear enough to support a cautious policy conclusion: tutoring can improve K-8 math outcomes, but the most convincing results come from models that are structured, responsive, and well-supported by either technology or strong instructional design (Bowman-Perrott et al., 2016)(n.d., “Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?”)(n.d., “Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI- Math Tutor in Ghana”)[7]. The evidence does not justify treating all tutoring as equal. Peer tutoring is promising but method-sensitive; AI and hybrid tutoring show measurable gains in middle school math; and intensive 1:1 tutoring can be powerful for students with learning disabilities, albeit on the basis of small samples and specialized settings (Bowman-Perrott et al., 2016)[8][24].

For schools and practitioners, the practical takeaway is that tutoring should be designed as a targeted instructional system, not an undifferentiated add-on. The strongest studies point toward models with clear skill targeting, frequent feedback, and mechanisms that increase student engagement and tutor responsiveness, especially when tutoring is aligned with the math content students are actually learning [7][8]. At the same time, the field still lacks enough directly comparable studies to say whether one-to-one is better than small-group tutoring, whether high-dosage tutoring is superior in K-8 math, or which tutor workforce is most cost-effective. Those are the decision-critical questions, and the current evidence only partially answers them.

## Limitations and Research Gaps

The main limitation is that the retrieved literature is too heterogeneous and too thin in clean head-to-head comparisons to support a definitive ranking of tutoring models for K-8 math. Several of the strongest results come from small samples, proximal outcomes, or quasi-experimental designs, which makes effect sizes vulnerable to inflation and makes comparisons across studies uncertain [24][8]. Even the meta-analytic evidence is incomplete in the extracted summaries because pooled effect sizes were not reported in the available text for the key tutoring syntheses (Bowman-Perrott et al., 2016; n.d., “Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?”).

The evidence is also weak on standardized test scores, long-term persistence, and subgroup moderation for multilingual learners, students with disabilities beyond the small MLD study, and students in diverse racial and socioeconomic groups. Many studies focus on platform measures, exit tickets, or immediate growth rather than durable outcomes, and several of the most relevant implementation questions—attendance thresholds, fidelity requirements, dose-response curves, and cost-effectiveness—remain unresolved in the retrieved pool [7][8][23]. The result is a promising but incomplete evidence base: tutoring appears beneficial for K-8 math, but the field still needs more rigorous, comparable, and equity-focused studies before stronger causal hierarchy claims can be made.
