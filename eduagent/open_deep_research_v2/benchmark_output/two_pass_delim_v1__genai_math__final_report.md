## Executive Summary

The direct evidence base for generative AI tools in K-8 mathematics is promising but still thin, uneven, and often indirect. The clearest conclusion is that generative AI can support some math learning and practice processes, especially when it is embedded in a structured tutoring workflow, but the literature does not yet justify a broad claim that it reliably improves K-8 math achievement relative to standard instruction or established non-AI supports across grades, settings, and subgroups. The strongest K-8-relevant positive evidence comes from an RCT in Ghana in which students assigned to supplemental AI math tutoring with Rori for two 30-minute sessions per week over 8 months showed substantially higher math growth than controls, with effect size d=0.36 among students who completed both assessments, but the brief excerpt does not report the comparator’s exact structure beyond control status or subgroup moderation results. The strongest middle-school evidence is a three-study QED of hybrid human-AI tutoring in grades 6-8, which found improved math learning-process outcomes relative to software-only conditions with confidence intervals reported as [0.057, 0.347], [0.106, 0.381], and [0.02, 0.70] across the three sites, along with higher engagement and time on task in at least one site. The evidence base also includes a 2025 meta-analysis of ChatGPT-related learning studies across primary, secondary, and college populations, but the extracted record does not provide the pooled mathematics-specific estimate for K-8, so it can support only cautious, indirect inference rather than a definitive K-8 claim. The strongest experimental and synthesis findings point in the same direction: generative AI is most credible as a support for guided practice, feedback, and problem solving, not as a replacement for curriculum-based teaching. In the RCT on AI math tutoring in Ghana, the intervention was a supplemental 8-month tutoring dosage of two 30-minute weekly sessions, and the study reported d=0.36 for math growth among completers. ## K-8 Mathematics Evidence Base for Generative AI

The direct K-8 mathematics literature in the retrieved pool is small and mixed in evidentiary strength. The strongest outcome-bearing study is the Ghana RCT of the AI math tutor Rori, which evaluated supplemental tutoring for middle school students over 8 months using two 30-minute weekly sessions and found substantially higher math growth than controls, with effect size d=0.36 among students who completed both assessments. This is the clearest direct signal that generative or AI-mediated tutoring can improve mathematics learning in a K-8-relevant context, but the excerpt does not report whether the control group was business-as-usual instruction, a different digital tool, or another support, and it does not provide subgroup analyses. [5]

A second important causal study is the three-study quasi-experimental investigation of hybrid human-AI tutoring in middle school grades 6-8. The intervention combined AI-based math software with human tutor support and found improved math learning-process outcomes relative to software-only conditions. The report gives confidence intervals of [0.057, 0.347], [0.106, 0.381], and [0.02, 0.70] across the three sites, indicating positive effects that are statistically distinguishable from zero in the summarized results. The study also reported increased engagement with math software in Site 2 and operational efficiency in Site 3 when tutor-to-student ratio changed from 1:8 to 1:4, with an estimate of 0.36 workspaces/hour. This evidence is especially relevant because it suggests that AI may work best as part of a supervised, human-in-the-loop model rather than as an unsupervised student-facing chatbot. [9]

The 2025 meta-analysis on ChatGPT-related learning performance is broader than K-8 mathematics, but it is still important because it is one of the few formal syntheses in the pool addressing generative AI in education. The extracted summary indicates that the meta-analysis included 51 quasi-experimental or experimental studies across primary, secondary, and college populations and examined whether effects vary by course type, grade level, learning model, duration, role in instruction, and application area. However, the available record does not report the pooled mathematics effect size for primary or middle grades, so it cannot answer the K-8 math question directly. Its value is mainly structural: it indicates that the field is beginning to ask the right moderator questions, but the evidence needed to answer them for K-8 mathematics is still not visible in the extracted text. [1]

### What Counts as Generative AI in This Literature

Across the retrieved sources, generative AI in K-8 mathematics is represented by several related but not identical intervention forms. Some studies use ChatGPT-like systems to generate hints or structured help, as in ChatGPT-generated worked-solution hints for mathematics skills, which were compared with human tutor-authored help in an adult sample and showed statistically significant pre-post gains in the overall sample, with the excerpt noting that the ChatGPT condition had an average gain and that generated hints can be produced much faster than human-authored help. Because that study is not K-8, it serves only as a mechanism and feasibility reference, not as direct evidence for younger learners. Other sources use generative AI to support stepwise reasoning, such as ChatGPT-MWPS, which was designed for primary students’ word-problem solving with hierarchical reasoning and batch question import. A separate elementary-school review also frames “digital learning using ChatGPT” as a conceptual and implementation issue, highlighting that much of the literature still concerns what these tools are and how they are used rather than whether they outperform conventional instruction. [13] [17] [18]

The retrieved literature also shows that “generative AI” is often studied in hybrid or mediated forms rather than as raw chatbot access. The middle-school hybrid tutoring study is a clear example: AI software was coupled with human tutors, and the positive outcomes were explicitly relative to software-only conditions. This distinction matters because it suggests that the relevant counterfactual for schools may not be “AI versus no AI,” but “AI plus teacher structure versus AI without structure.” The same lesson appears in the elementary ChatGPT-MWPS paper, where the system was intended to streamline worksheet generation, batch question import, and step-by-step hint production for teachers, implying a teacher-facing layer of support in addition to student interaction. [9] [17]

## Baseline and Comparator Conditions

The main counterfactuals in the retrieved evidence are software-only practice, control or business-as-usual instruction, human-teacher comparison, and non-AI digital supports. In the Ghana RCT, students assigned to the AI tutor were compared against controls, and the paper’s summary is explicit that the intervention was supplemental tutoring rather than a replacement for standard math teaching. The precise nature of the control is not fully reported in the excerpt, but the design is clearly causal and school-based. In the hybrid tutoring QED, the main comparator was software-only conditions, which is particularly informative because it isolates the value of adding human tutor support to AI-mediated practice. [5] [9]

Comparator evidence from adjacent studies helps define the practical standard that generative AI must beat. A 2015 mixed-methods study of synchronous online tutoring for low-achieving middle school students found that a Tier 3 online tutoring service was implemented within a broader schoolwide mathematics reform context, though the excerpt does not provide the exact achievement estimate. A 2022 RCT on personalized recommendations in an educational app for children found substantial engagement gains, including 60% higher total story engagement in the Recommended Story section and 78% more completed stories, showing that non-generative personalization can strongly improve usage in child-facing learning apps. These studies do not answer the generative AI question directly, but they show that non-AI or non-generative supports can already generate meaningful engagement and learning-related gains, making the bar for AI superiority appropriately high. ## Implementation Features and Mechanisms

The most plausible mechanisms for positive effects in the direct K-8 evidence are immediate feedback, guided practice, structured hinting, and teacher mediation. The Ghana RCT’s 8-month, twice-weekly 30-minute tutoring schedule suggests that dosage and regularity matter, and the positive d=0.36 result is consistent with the idea that AI support works when it creates sustained practice opportunities. The hybrid middle-school study strengthens this interpretation because the intervention combined AI software with human tutor support and improved learning-process outcomes relative to software-only conditions, implying that teacher oversight or human scaffolding may help students use AI productively rather than passively. [5] [9]

The elementary ChatGPT-MWPS study adds a design-level account of how generative AI may be operationalized for younger learners. The system was built around hierarchical reasoning and step-by-step problem solving for mathematical word problems, and the study reports strong cognitive engagement and favorable usability. The same paper notes teacher interviews, indicating that the system was not only student-facing but also intended to reduce teacher preparation burden through worksheet generation and batch import functions. These features point to a blended model in which generative AI helps teachers prepare and structure tasks while also guiding student reasoning during practice. [17]

The broader ChatGPT and elementary STEM reviews reinforce that prompt design, safety controls, and curriculum alignment are central implementation issues, even where achievement effects are not yet reported. Because the available excerpts do not provide detailed causal tests of those features, they should be treated as implementation hypotheses rather than established moderators. Still, the pattern across the evidence suggests that generative AI is most defensible when it is constrained, task-specific, and embedded in existing instructional routines rather than used as an open-ended conversational substitute for teaching. [18] [3] [1]

## Experimental and Quasi-Experimental Effects

The RCT evidence specific to K-8 mathematics is sparse but positive. The Ghana study on Rori is the strongest direct causal result in the pool. It tested supplemental access to an AI-powered math tutor for middle school students in Ghana, with two 30-minute sessions weekly over 8 months, and reported substantially higher math growth than controls with effect size d=0.36 among students who completed both assessments. Because the excerpt does not provide the full set of subgroup analyses or the exact control condition, the result should be interpreted as evidence that AI tutoring can work under resource-constrained school conditions, not as proof that any generative chatbot will generalize to all K-8 settings. [5]

The middle-school hybrid human-AI tutoring QED extends this conclusion by showing that AI-supported math learning can improve when paired with human oversight. Across three quasi-experimental studies, the intervention was associated with improved math learning-process outcomes relative to software-only conditions, and the reported confidence intervals were all positive: [0.057, 0.347], [0.106, 0.381], and [0.02, 0.70]. The study also found increased engagement and a more efficient tutor allocation arrangement in one site. Because the design is quasi-experimental, causal claims are weaker than in the Ghana RCT, but the pattern supports the same basic conclusion: AI works best as part of a structured support system, not as a stand-alone novelty. [9]

The ChatGPT-MWPS study is useful mainly as a bridge between design and outcome evidence. It is a quasi-experimental design focused on primary students’ mathematical word problem solving, but the extracted record emphasizes usability and cognitive engagement rather than post-test achievement gains. The correlation between cognitive engagement and intention to use, r=0.969, is striking but should not be confused with learning gain. It does, however, suggest that student acceptance and sustained engagement may be an important pathway if future versions of the system are to translate into measurable achievement effects. [17]

## Subgroup Evidence and Unresolved Moderators

The brief’s priority subgroup questions remain largely unanswered in the retrieved K-8 evidence. The Ghana RCT summary does not report effects by grade band within middle school, prior achievement, language status, disability, or socioeconomic subgroup, although the setting itself implies relevance for low-resource contexts. The hybrid human-AI tutoring QED similarly does not provide subgroup-specific math effect estimates in the extracted record, even though it explicitly focuses on low-income backgrounds in its framing. The 2025 ChatGPT meta-analysis indicates that the authors examined moderators such as grade level, learning model, duration, role in instruction, and application area, but the summary does not provide the subgroup coefficients or mathematics-only moderation results. As a result, the literature does not yet answer whether generative AI is especially helpful for lower-achieving students, multilingual learners, or students with disabilities in K-8 mathematics. [5] [9] [1]

That absence matters because the broader AI and education literature repeatedly points to heterogeneity in who benefits from technology-mediated support. The retrieved growth-mindset experiment in high school, although not an AI study, illustrates that effects can be concentrated among lower-achieving students and can vary by school context. The same logic likely applies here, but without direct K-8 math subgroup reporting it remains speculative. The correct conclusion is therefore not that generative AI works equally for all learners, but that current evidence is too incomplete to estimate differential impacts credibly. [14]

## Comparison With Existing Non-AI Supports

Generative AI should be interpreted against a landscape of already effective or promising non-AI supports. The 2015 online tutoring study shows that synchronous human tutoring can be implemented for low-achieving middle school students in a Tier 3 setting within school reform, although the excerpt does not provide the effect size. The 2022 personalized-recommendations RCT demonstrates that non-generative personalization can meaningfully increase engagement in an educational app for children, with 60% higher total story engagement and 78% more completed stories in the recommended-story section. These findings do not reduce the value of generative AI, but they do show that the relevant comparison is not zero support versus AI; schools already have multiple viable levers for practice, feedback, and engagement. [35] [11]

The most informative direct comparison in the retrieved set is actually within the AI ecosystem: software-only versus hybrid human-AI tutoring. In that study, adding human tutors to AI software improved math learning-process outcomes and engagement. This suggests that the main question for schools is likely not whether to choose AI or teachers, but how to organize AI plus teacher time so that students receive both scalable practice and expert intervention when needed. The evidence favors complementarity over substitution. [9]

## Synthesis and Implications

Across the available studies, the strongest pattern is that generative AI has the greatest promise when it is narrow in scope, aligned to a specific math task, and surrounded by human or curricular scaffolding. The positive RCT and QED findings both involve structured use: repeated tutoring sessions in Ghana and hybrid human-AI tutoring in middle school. By contrast, the more design-oriented elementary ChatGPT studies emphasize usability, engagement, and workflow support rather than demonstrating achievement gains. For practitioners, the implication is to treat generative AI as a support for practice and feedback, not as a standalone instructional replacement. Schools considering implementation should prioritize tightly bounded use cases such as word problems, worked hints, or teacher-assisted tutoring, and should expect strongest results where teachers can monitor, verify, and redirect use. [5] [9] [17] [18]

The broader implication for policy is that evidence is sufficient to justify cautious piloting, not large-scale claims of superiority over standard instruction. Because many studies measure engagement or usability rather than achievement, decision-makers should demand direct learning outcomes, delayed retention measures, and subgroup analyses before scaling. The comparison set should also include non-AI digital practice, human tutoring, and teacher feedback, since those alternatives may already deliver strong benefits. In short, the evidence supports structured experimentation in K-8 mathematics classrooms and supplemental settings, but not unqualified adoption. [1] [35] [11]

## Limitations and Research Gaps

The dominant limitation is the small number of direct K-8 mathematics outcome studies with extractable statistics. The most convincing results come from one RCT in Ghana and one middle-school QED, while many other K-8-relevant records are reviews, design studies, usability studies, or teacher-facing implementation papers. This means the field currently lacks a robust cluster of replications, especially in elementary grades and in U.S.-style classroom settings. [5] [9] [18] [17]

A second limitation is comparator weakness. Several studies do not clearly report whether the control is business-as-usual, software-only, or another support, and few head-to-head tests compare generative AI with strong non-AI alternatives such as adaptive practice systems, teacher feedback, or human tutoring. Subgroup evidence is also largely absent, including prior achievement, disability, multilingual status, and SES. Finally, the extracted record contains little on delayed posttests, transfer to independent problem solving, hallucination management, dependence, or equity risks in real K-8 classrooms. Those omissions mean that the field is still early in establishing not only whether generative AI can help, but for whom, under what constraints, and at what cost. [5] [9] [1] [13]
