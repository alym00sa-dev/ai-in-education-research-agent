## Executive Summary

The evidence base indicates that tutoring can improve K-8 mathematics outcomes, but the strength of that conclusion depends heavily on the tutoring model, the comparison condition, and the quality of implementation. The most credible direct evidence comes from randomized trials and a small number of meta-analyses. Across those sources, math tutoring effects are generally positive, with larger and more reliable gains appearing when tutoring is intensive, curriculum-linked, and delivered with strong instructional support. However, the literature is uneven: elementary grades are better represented than kindergarten through grade 2, middle-school causal evidence is thinner, and many studies measure broad achievement or curriculum mastery rather than fine-grained skills such as number sense, conceptual understanding, or procedural fluency.

The strongest pooled evidence comes from the meta-analysis of intelligent tutoring systems in K-12 U.S. schools, which finds that ITS improve achievement on average, though the exact pooled effect size is not reported in the extracted source summary (meta-analysis, K-12; effect size not reported) (Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?). The meta-analytic literature also supports positive effects for tutoring more broadly in elementary mathematics, including syntheses that indicate small-group tutoring can be as effective as or sometimes more effective than one-to-one formats, while cross-age and loosely structured peer tutoring are less consistently positive; however, the extracted summaries do not report a single pooled effect size for those reviews. Among direct trials, Tutor CoPilot (RCT, n=4,136 sessions) found that giving human tutors real-time AI support increased the likelihood that students passed math exit tickets from 62% to 66%, a 4 percentage-point increase, with subgroup gains up to 9 percentage points (Tutor CoPilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time Expertise, 2025). In a separate RCT in Ghana, access to an AI math tutor delivered by WhatsApp produced greater math growth than control over 8 months, with treatment mean growth 5.13 versus control 3.97 and effect size d=0.36 (n=477) (Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI-Math Tutor in Ghana, n.d.). In Dutch primary education, a high-dosage tutoring RCT showed gains in math achievement of d=0.28 for the pooled 5th-grade sample over one year, d=0.25 over a half-year, and d=0.26 for the pooled 4th-grade sample (n=265 and n=233, respectively) (Closing the income-achievement gap? Experimental evidence from high-dosage tutoring in Dutch primary education, n.d.). In the UK classrooms exploratory RCT, interactive LearnLM tutoring improved mathematics learning relative to static hints by 5.5 percentage points, with CI=[-1.4, +12.4], and was at least as effective as human tutoring in that study context (n=165) (AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students: An exploratory RCT in UK classrooms, 2025).

The quasi-experimental evidence is directionally consistent but less definitive. In middle-school math, hybrid human-AI tutoring was associated with improved learning-process outcomes relative to software-only or lower-support conditions across three site studies, with reported coefficients beta=0.202, beta=0.2437, and 0.36 workspaces/hour, but these were not direct achievement impacts and therefore cannot be treated as equivalent evidence of math learning gains (n=125, n=385, and n=75 across the sites) (Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring: A Three-Study Quasi-Experimental Investigation, 2024). A mixed-methods evaluation of MathSpring/Wayang Outpost describes a middle-school adaptive math tutoring system that supports cognition, metacognition, and affect, but the extracted summary does not provide a causal effect estimate on achievement (A Multimedia Adaptive Tutoring System for Mathematics that Addresses Cognition, Metacognition and Affect, 2014). Similarly, Reasoning Mind Genie 2 is primarily a design and implementation study rather than causal evidence for effectiveness (Reasoning Mind Genie 2: An Intelligent Tutoring System as a Vehicle for International Transfer of Instructional Methods in Mathematics, 2014). Taken together, the evidence answers the main question partially: tutoring generally helps K-8 math, but the magnitude and reliability of benefits vary substantially by tutoring model and implementation quality.

The tiered questions are only partly resolved. Tier 1 is partially answered: tutoring is defined in the literature as one-to-one, small-group, peer, high-dosage, and AI-supported supplemental instruction, and the most common outcomes are broad math achievement or mastery measures; detailed measures of number sense, conceptual understanding, and procedural fluency are underrepresented. Tier 2 is only partially answered: some studies use standard classroom instruction or static hints as controls, but many counterfactuals are enhanced supports rather than pure business-as-usual, and the exact composition of comparison conditions is often not fully reported. Tier 3 is partially answered: dosage, tutor support, curriculum alignment, and delivery mode matter, but the evidence does not yet support clean causal estimates of optimal hours or group size. Tier 4 is partially answered: there is enough evidence to say tutoring can raise K-8 math performance, but direct comparisons among one-to-one, small-group, peer, remote, and AI-supported tutoring remain limited, and subgroup and durability evidence is thin. Overall confidence is moderate. The single biggest caveat is that the strongest positive findings cluster in a handful of elementary and technology-supported studies, while clean RCT evidence for traditional K-8 math tutoring against strong comparators remains sparse.

## Evidence Base and Study Types

The source pool contains a small set of directly relevant randomized trials, a handful of quasi-experimental studies, several mixed-methods or design-focused evaluations of tutoring systems, and multiple broader reviews or meta-analyses that include K-12 or adjacent populations. The most decision-relevant evidence for the brief comes from the RCTs of high-dosage tutoring in Dutch primary education, AI math tutoring in Ghana, Tutor CoPilot in live tutoring sessions, and the LearnLM classroom RCT in the UK (Closing the income-achievement gap?; Effective and Scalable Math Support; Tutor CoPilot; AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students). The strongest synthetic evidence comes from the meta-analysis of intelligent tutoring systems in K-12 U.S. schools and the broader review literature on tutoring systems and differentiated instruction (Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?; Advancing Education through Tutoring Systems; Differentiated Instruction in Secondary Education).

A major limitation of the source pool is that many studies are not clean tests of K-8 math tutoring outcomes. Some are primarily system-design papers, some measure intermediate learning processes rather than achievement, and some involve adults, undergraduate students, or high school populations rather than K-8 students. Those sources can inform mechanisms or implementation, but they do not carry equal weight for causal claims about the focal population.

## What Counts as Tutoring in K-8 Math

The literature treats tutoring as a family of instructional supports rather than a single intervention type. Within the source pool, tutoring includes human one-to-one and small-group models, peer or cross-age support, high-dosage tutoring, AI-supported tutoring, and hybrid human-AI models embedded in school settings (Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?; Closing the income-achievement gap?; Tutor CoPilot; Effective and Scalable Math Support; Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring; AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students). The AI and intelligent tutoring studies are especially useful for understanding how tutoring is being redefined in scalable digital formats, but they also broaden the concept beyond traditional adult-delivered tutoring.

The most common outcome is generalized math achievement or curriculum mastery. Tutor CoPilot reports exit-ticket pass rates tied to lesson mastery, while the Ghana RCT uses growth scores over 8 months and the Dutch primary study uses math achievement outcomes across grades and time horizons (Tutor CoPilot; Effective and Scalable Math Support; Closing the income-achievement gap?). The LearnLM RCT uses math learning outcomes relative to static hints, while the Building Bridges study reports a mathematical performance test on proportional relationships, though it is not strictly a tutoring trial and the overall test effect is not significant (AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students; Building Bridges – AI Custom Chatbots as Mediators between Mathematics and Physics). Measures of conceptual understanding, procedural fluency, number sense, and problem solving appear in the descriptions of several studies, but the available summaries overwhelmingly collapse these into broad achievement categories rather than separate domain-specific effects.

## Counterfactuals and Baseline Supports

The evidence base rarely gives a clean picture of what “standard instruction” means. In several trials, the comparator is not plain business-as-usual but a lower-support technology condition, static hints, or a software-only version of the same program (AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students; Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring). Tutor CoPilot evaluates whether AI support improves the quality of live human tutoring rather than comparing tutoring to no tutoring, so its causal estimate is about augmenting tutors, not replacing classroom instruction (Tutor CoPilot). The Dutch high-dosage tutoring RCT compares a tutoring program against ordinary schooling conditions in primary education, but the extracted summary does not fully specify the full counterfactual package beyond the comparison design (Closing the income-achievement gap?).

This matters because many reported gains are relative to enhanced or partial-support controls, not to pure no-intervention baselines. The literature therefore supports the conclusion that more intensive or better-supported tutoring outperforms weaker supplemental supports, but it does not yet establish a single average effect against a uniform “standard instruction” condition across K-8 math.

## Direct Experimental Evidence on Math Achievement

The Dutch primary education high-dosage tutoring RCT provides one of the clearest direct causal estimates in the source pool. In the pooled 5th-grade experimental sample, the tutoring program produced math gains of d=0.28 after one year and d=0.25 after half a year; in the pooled 4th-grade sample, the effect was d=0.26 after one year (n=265 pooled 5th grade; n=233 pooled 4th grade) (Closing the income-achievement gap?; n.d.). This is directly relevant to K-8 math because it targets elementary students and reports standardized achievement effects. It also suggests that tutoring can raise math achievement above business-as-usual schooling, though the source summary does not provide a head-to-head comparison with alternative remediation models.

The Ghana RCT is another strong causal study. Students with access to the AI math tutor Rori showed larger math growth than controls over an 8-month period, with treatment mean growth 5.13 compared with control 3.97 and effect size d=0.36 (n=477; 241 control, 236 treatment) (Effective and Scalable Math Support: Experimental Evidence on the Impact of an AI-Math Tutor in Ghana, n.d.). This is especially important because it shows a remote, low-bandwidth tutoring model can improve outcomes in a grades 3-8 setting. The finding broadens the evidence base beyond face-to-face tutoring and suggests that scalable digital tutoring may produce meaningful gains, although the context is a low-resource school system and the intervention is AI-based rather than human-led.

Tutor CoPilot offers causal evidence about improving tutor performance during real-time math tutoring. Students whose tutors had access to Tutor CoPilot were 4 percentage points more likely to pass exit tickets assessing math lesson mastery, with 62% in control and 66% in treatment, p<0.01 (Tutor CoPilot: A Human-AI Approach for Scaling Real-Time Expertise, 2025). The study also reports subgroup gains up to 9 percentage points. The sample is session-based rather than a straightforward student-level cohort, with 4,136 sessions and 550,000+ messages, so the evidence is strong for the tutoring process and lesson mastery but should be interpreted carefully as a student-learning study (Tutor CoPilot, 2025). Even so, it provides rare evidence that tutor expertise support can improve immediate math learning in live tutoring.

The LearnLM exploratory RCT in UK classrooms adds evidence on AI-supported tutoring in a school context. Interactive tutoring improved mathematics learning outcomes relative to static hints by 5.5 percentage points, with CI=[-1.4, +12.4], and supervised LearnLM tutoring was at least as effective as human tutoring in the study’s comparison (n=165) (AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students: An exploratory RCT in UK classrooms, 2025). Because the confidence interval includes zero, this finding is suggestive rather than definitive, but it is still useful evidence that AI-supported tutoring may approximate human tutor performance under supervision.

The pooled evidence from the meta-analysis of intelligent tutoring systems in U.S. K-12 schools indicates that ITS benefit student learning on average, but the extracted summary does not report a pooled effect size or exact moderator estimates (Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students? n.d.). This review is still important because it supports the broader proposition that tutoring systems can improve learning in real school settings and that effects vary by implementation and study characteristics. However, because the source summary does not provide exact pooled coefficients in the extracted text, the review can only support a directional conclusion here.

## Quasi-Experimental and Observational Evidence

The most relevant quasi-experimental evidence in the pool comes from hybrid human-AI tutoring in middle school math. Across three site studies, the intervention was associated with improved learning-process outcomes relative to software-only or lower-support conditions, with beta=0.202 at Site 1 (n=125), beta=0.2437 at Site 2 (n=385), and 0.36 workspaces/hour at Site 3 (n=75), with reported confidence intervals of [0.057, 0.347], [0.106, 0.381], and [0.02, 0.70] respectively (Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring: A Three-Study Quasi-Experimental Investigation, 2024). These are not direct achievement effects, but they show that added human-AI support can increase engagement and productive work in middle-school mathematics software environments. The study is therefore suggestive about mechanisms and implementation, not conclusive about achievement.

The MathSpring/Wayang Outpost paper is similarly informative but not causal in the strict sense. It describes a multimedia adaptive tutoring system for middle school mathematics designed to support cognition, metacognition, and affect (A Multimedia Adaptive Tutoring System for Mathematics that Addresses Cognition, Metacognition and Affect, 2014). The summary does not provide a standardized effect size on math achievement, so the study cannot be used to infer direct learning gains. Its value is in showing that adaptive tutors often bundle mathematical instruction with motivational and metacognitive scaffolding.

Reasoning Mind Genie 2 likewise demonstrates how tutoring systems are designed to transfer instructional methods from Russian mathematics education to U.S. classrooms, but the extracted summary does not report causal outcome estimates (Reasoning Mind Genie 2: An Intelligent Tutoring System as a Vehicle for International Transfer of Instructional Methods in Mathematics, 2014). It is relevant to implementation and curriculum alignment, not to causal effectiveness.

## Comparators, Delivery Formats, and Implementation Conditions

The source pool suggests that delivery format matters, but it does not yet allow a strong ranking of all tutoring models. The high-dosage Dutch program and the Ghana AI tutor are both supportive of intensive, structured tutoring, while Tutor CoPilot indicates that enhancing tutor decision-making in live sessions can improve immediate lesson mastery (Closing the income-achievement gap?; Effective and Scalable Math Support; Tutor CoPilot). The LearnLM study suggests that supervised AI tutoring may be a viable alternative to static hints and potentially comparable to human tutoring, but its estimate is not precise enough to settle equivalence (AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students).

The broader review literature suggests that small-group tutoring can sometimes match or exceed one-to-one tutoring, and that peer and cross-age tutoring are less consistent when attention is restricted to math achievement. However, the extracted summaries do not provide a single pooled effect size or enough detailed subgroup statistics to support stronger claims about relative superiority (Advancing Education through Tutoring Systems; Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?; Differentiated Instruction in Secondary Education). In other words, the evidence implies that intensity and instructional quality are more important than tutor format alone, but this remains a hypothesis supported by heterogeneous studies rather than a settled causal law.

Implementation features recur across the studies as plausible drivers of effect. Tutor CoPilot emphasizes real-time expert guidance for novice tutors; the Ghana study leverages WhatsApp delivery in a low-resource environment; the Dutch trial uses a high-dosage model; and the LearnLM trial embeds tutoring in classroom routines (Tutor CoPilot; Effective and Scalable Math Support; Closing the income-achievement gap?; AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students). These studies collectively imply that curriculum alignment, feedback quality, and feasible delivery infrastructure are central. Yet only a few studies experimentally isolate these features, so the field still lacks definitive evidence about which implementation ingredient matters most.

## Populations, Grade Bands, and Equity-Relevant Patterns

Elementary grades dominate the strongest causal evidence. The Dutch RCT is elementary-focused, and the Ghana AI tutor covers grades 3-8 but includes a broad age span (Closing the income-achievement gap?; Effective and Scalable Math Support). Middle-school evidence exists, but it is more often quasi-experimental or design-focused than randomized, as seen in the hybrid human-AI tutoring study and the MathSpring and Genie 2 papers (Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring; A Multimedia Adaptive Tutoring System for Mathematics that Addresses Cognition, Metacognition and Affect; Reasoning Mind Genie 2). The source pool therefore supports greater confidence in elementary tutoring effects than in middle-school effects.

The equity evidence is promising but incomplete. The Dutch study’s framing around income-achievement gaps indicates relevance for disadvantaged students, but the extracted summary does not provide subgroup effect estimates by SES (Closing the income-achievement gap?). Tutor CoPilot reports subgroup gains up to 9 percentage points, but the source summary does not specify which subgroups these were or whether they were statistically robust (Tutor CoPilot). The Ghana study is particularly important for low-resource contexts because it demonstrates gains in a setting with limited access to high-dosage tutoring and basic mobile-phone delivery (Effective and Scalable Math Support). Still, there is little direct evidence in the source pool on multilingual learners, students with disabilities, or K-2 students specifically.

## What the Evidence Says About Alternative Supports

The current source pool does not contain enough direct head-to-head trials comparing tutoring with intervention blocks, teacher-led remediation, computer-assisted practice, or homework support in K-8 math. The closest evidence comes from studies comparing tutoring or AI support with static hints, software-only conditions, or ordinary classroom instruction (AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students; Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring; Closing the income-achievement gap?; Tutor CoPilot). These are useful, but they do not answer the comparator question fully. The strongest defensible conclusion is that tutoring tends to outperform weaker versions of supplemental support and can add value beyond standard classroom instruction, but the literature still does not allow a precise comparison against all realistic alternatives schools use.

## Synthesis and Implications

Across the source pool, the central pattern is that mathematics tutoring for K-8 students is more likely to work when it is structured, curriculum-linked, and supported by either high dosage or strong instructional scaffolding. This is visible in the Dutch high-dosage tutoring trial, the Ghana WhatsApp AI tutor, the Tutor CoPilot study, and the LearnLM classroom trial, all of which show positive or suggestive effects on math learning outcomes (Closing the income-achievement gap?; Effective and Scalable Math Support; Tutor CoPilot; AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students). The broader meta-analytic evidence reinforces that tutoring systems improve learning on average, though the extracted summaries do not provide a single pooled effect size for the most relevant syntheses (Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?; Advancing Education through Tutoring Systems).

For schools and practitioners, the practical implication is not that any tutoring is sufficient, but that tutoring must be designed to deliver sustained, well-targeted mathematics practice with enough instructional quality to move achievement. AI-supported tutoring and tutor augmentation look especially promising as scaling strategies, because they can support more frequent feedback and more consistent instructional moves than unassisted novice tutoring (Tutor CoPilot; Effective and Scalable Math Support; AI tutoring can safely and effectively support students). At the same time, the evidence base remains too thin to recommend a single universal format, especially for K-2, special education, multilingual learners, or direct comparisons against other supplemental interventions.

## Limitations and Research Gaps

The main limitation is that the strongest evidence is concentrated in a small number of studies and in a narrow set of contexts. There are several randomized trials, but few are clean K-8 traditional tutoring studies with explicit strong comparators and long follow-up. Many of the most relevant middle-school sources are quasi-experimental or design-focused rather than causal, and several reviews aggregate across grade spans, intervention types, or even populations beyond K-8 mathematics (Improving Student Learning with Hybrid Human-AI Tutoring; A Multimedia Adaptive Tutoring System for Mathematics that Addresses Cognition, Metacognition and Affect; Reasoning Mind Genie 2; Do intelligent tutoring systems benefit K-12 students?; Advancing Education through Tutoring Systems).

A second limitation is measurement. The evidence is dominated by broad achievement, test-score, or mastery outcomes, while domain-specific outcomes such as number sense, conceptual understanding, procedural fluency, and problem solving are under-measured or not separately reported. A third limitation is the lack of precise counterfactual reporting. Many studies do not clearly specify whether the control condition was standard instruction, software-only practice, or another enhanced support, which makes comparative policy interpretation difficult. Finally, subgroup evidence, durability of effects, and cost-effectiveness remain thin in the extracted pool, despite being central to real-world decision-making.
