## Executive Summary

The body of evidence amassed on tutoring interventions for K-8 mathematics demonstrates strong support for tutoring as an effective strategy to improve math achievement and skills development compared to standard classroom instruction. High-quality experimental studies and meta-analyses show that tutoring produces positive effects with effect sizes typically in the small to moderate range (approximately d=0.20 to 0.36), and benefits are particularly pronounced for lower-performing, economically disadvantaged, and minority student populations. One-to-one and small group tutoring supervised by trained adults or paraprofessionals consistently outperforms standard instruction alone. Hybrid human-AI tutoring models have emerged as promising scalable innovations, showing robust increases in student engagement and proficiency in quasi-experimental contexts, and initial randomized controlled data suggest these models can enhance learning outcomes and operational efficiency at manageable costs [24, 27, 28, 26].

Among the strongest RCT evidence, the Tutor CoPilot trial tested an AI-augmented tutoring system across 900 tutors and 1,800 K-8 students, revealing a 4 percentage point increase in mastery exit ticket passing rates and up to 9 percentage points for students paired with lower-performing tutors, supporting the utility of AI to scale expert guidance efficiently [28]. Similarly, an RCT evaluating the AI math tutor “Rori” in Ghana with 477 students documented a standardized effect size of d=0.36 on math learning gains, evidencing the feasibility of AI tutoring in low-resource settings [26]. Quasi-experimental research on hybrid human-AI tutoring with 585 middle school students across three U.S. sites identified significant increases in software engagement indicators, lessons completed, and skills proficiency, particularly favoring students below grade level [27]. The systematic review and meta-analysis of intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) found a pooled effect size of about 0.27 for math outcomes in K-12 populations in the U.S., underscoring moderate but consistent benefits of AI-driven personalized learning [22].

Complementary quasi-experimental and observational research affirms the positive impact of tutoring and hybrid models, but also highlights implementation challenges including variability in tutor training quality, tutoring dosage, and student baseline achievement. Observational studies report that tutor discourse aligned with student mastery improves math achievement, and tutor training in motivational and socio-emotional supports enhances engagement, crucial for sustained learning [19, 31]. Evidence on peer tutoring, while less abundant in large RCTs, suggests medium to large positive effects on math achievement, motivation, and self-concept, with program fidelity and tutor preparation strongly mediating outcomes [1, 2]. However, definitive randomized evidence for peer tutoring in K-8 math education remains limited.

Regarding the tiered research questions, the evidence fully answers foundational framing of tutoring and math achievement measurement via numerous meta-analyses and trials establishing tutoring definitions, typical metrics, and diverse educational contexts [24, 26]. Baseline instructional approaches and common alternative supports including differentiated instruction and ITS are partially answered; evidence on these alternatives is growing but less definitive compared to tutoring [22, 125, 18]. Mechanisms and implementation factors such as fidelity, dosage, tutor training, and discourse strategies receive partial answers, with emerging but incomplete causal evidence elucidating how tutoring achieves gains [19, 31, 156]. Comparative effectiveness remains partially resolved: tutoring outperforms standard instruction with moderate effect sizes, yet rigorous randomized comparisons to alternative supports and long-term sustainability remain underexplored [28, 27]. Equity and scalability considerations are actively investigated but still require more systematic study, especially regarding equitable access and nuanced impacts by student demographics [24, 27, 124].

Overall confidence in tutoring as an effective intervention to improve K-8 math outcomes is high, grounded in convergent findings from multiple meta-analyses and well-powered RCTs. The single most critical caveat is the heterogeneity of implementation fidelity, tutoring dosage, tutor training, and population characteristics, which influence effect magnitude and sustainability. Gaps remain in evidence on long-term follow-up, randomized evaluations of peer tutoring, cost-effectiveness across contexts, and comprehensive studies of hybrid human-AI models at scale. Addressing these gaps is essential to fully realize tutoring’s potential for equitable, scalable math education improvement.

## Introduction and Definitional Framing

Tutoring for K-8 mathematics encompasses a range of instructional modes typically characterized by one-to-one or small group direct support, often personalized and scaffolded. Models include peer tutoring, volunteer tutoring, paraprofessional-led tutoring, professional adult tutoring, and emerging hybrid human-AI tutoring systems. Mathematics achievement is commonly operationalized using standardized test scores, mastery of curriculum-aligned skills, or proficiency in exit assessments. Demographically, tutoring programs have been implemented in diverse settings including urban, rural, English learner-rich, and economically disadvantaged schools [24, 28, 26].

Standard K-8 math instruction frequently involves whole-class teaching with some differentiation. Alternative supports examined include intelligent tutoring systems (ITS), differentiated instruction (DI), and blended learning. These approaches are often studied as comparators or complements to tutoring [22, 18, 125].

## Experimental Evidence on Tutoring Effectiveness

The meta-analysis by Dietrichson et al. [24] synthesizes evidence on tutoring interventions primarily delivered by trained adults across K-8 grades, reporting effect sizes averaging 0.20 to 0.36 on math achievement outcomes in randomized controlled trials. This meta-analysis establishes tutoring’s overall efficacy relative to standard instruction.

The Tutor CoPilot randomized controlled trial involving 1,800 K-8 students and 900 tutors examined whether AI-generated real-time pedagogical guidance for novice tutors could improve student mastery. The trial found a 4 percentage point increase in exit ticket passing rates overall, rising to 9 points for students who began with lower-rated tutors [28]. This controlled trial demonstrates scalable, cost-effective enhancement of tutoring efficacy via AI support.

Similarly, a study in Ghana evaluated the AI tutor “Rori,” accessible on basic mobile phones via WhatsApp, with 477 students in grades 3-8 in a randomized controlled trial [26]. The intervention produced a moderate effect size of d=0.36 on math learning gains, confirming technology’s promise in diverse low-resource settings.

A quasi-experimental investigation across three U.S. middle schools with 125 to 385 students per site assessed hybrid human-AI tutoring integrated with adaptive learning software [27]. The study reported significant positive impacts on engagement metrics, including time spent using math software and lessons completed, with effects predominantly benefitting students below grade level. Although not a randomized trial, the design provides important insights into hybrid tutoring models and their implementation challenges.

Meta-analytic reviews of intelligent tutoring systems have aggregated 18 experimental studies involving elementary and middle school U.S. students, revealing pooled effect sizes near g=0.27 favoring ITS on math outcomes [22]. This evidence highlights ITS as a beneficial but moderate-impact alternative or supplement to human tutoring.

## Complementary Quasi-Experimental and Observational Findings

Observational research assessing tutor discourse strategies confirms that alignment between tutor instructional moves and student content mastery predicts improved student math achievement [19]. Such findings imply that tutor quality and instructional quality are mechanisms attentive to individual student needs.

Studies highlight the importance of dosage and fidelity. Quasi-experimental studies of hybrid tutoring show variable engagement and effect sizes influenced by the frequency and quality of tutoring sessions [27]. Observational data also suggest significant heterogeneity across students in response to tutoring, emphasizing the role of implementation context and student characteristics.

Peer tutoring research, though less represented in randomized controlled trials for K-8 math, indicates medium to large positive effects on academic achievement and motivation from programmatic peer tutoring interventions with trained tutors under structured models, but with less conclusive RCT evidence [1][2].

## Addressing Tiered Research Questions

The comprehensive meta-analytic and RCT literature fully answers Tier 1 questions concerning tutoring models, math achievement metrics, and target populations through robust experiment and review data [24, 26]. Tier 2 questions about baseline instruction and alternative supports receive partial answers, with systematic literature reviews and selected trials indicating moderate ITS effectiveness and partial equivalence of differentiated or blended instruction [22, 125, 18].

Tier 3 questions regarding implementation and mechanisms receive partial answers. Studies report dosage, tutor training, and discourse alignment as important moderators, but causal paths and comprehensive fidelity data remain limited [19, 27, 156]. Tier 4, comparative effectiveness evidence shows tutoring outperforms standard instruction with good consistency, but randomized comparison versus alternative interventions and long-term sustainability data remain scant [28, 27]. Equity and scalability considerations are emerging, especially via AI-human hybrid models, but systematic equity-focused and access data remain partial and call for further investigation [24, 124].

## Synthesis and Implications

Across evidence tiers, tutoring emerges as a consistently effective intervention improving K-8 math achievement with meaningful effect sizes supported by high-quality randomized data and meta-analytic syntheses. Hybrid human-AI tutoring systems represent scalable innovations that enhance student engagement and achievement while maintaining cost-effectiveness and operational efficiency, making tutoring more accessible and sustainable in resource-constrained settings [27, 28, 26]. Tutor training, dosage fidelity, and instructional match to learner needs stand out as critical mediators, suggesting that investment in tutor preparation and monitoring systems (including AI analytics) can amplify gains.

In practice, schools should consider integrating tutoring interventions tailored to student needs and contextual factors, leveraging AI supports where feasible to scale expert guidance. Emphasis on training tutors for effective discourse strategies aligned with student mastery may improve outcomes. Equity implications necessitate ensuring tutoring access for marginalized students, supported by subsidy, community partnerships, and culturally responsive models.

Intelligent tutoring systems can complement human tutors by providing individualized, adaptive content, but human support remains crucial for motivation and engagement, especially among lower-performing or underserved students. Differentiated classroom instruction and technology integration, while beneficial, generally produce smaller effects than tutoring, reinforcing tutoring’s role as a primary intervention to improve K-8 math outcomes.

---

## Limitations and Research Gaps

Key methodological limitations include a shortage of large-scale randomized controlled trials specifically evaluating peer tutoring and hybrid human-AI models with long-term follow-up measures. Implementation fidelity and dosage data are inconsistently reported, limiting understanding of dose-response relationships and scalability conditions. Heterogeneity in study designs, assessment tools, and populations impedes broad generalizations, and published research on equity-focused outcomes remains incomplete. Randomized experimental comparisons of tutoring versus alternative interventions such as differentiated instruction or computer-assisted instruction are limited, restricting conclusions about relative impact.

Additionally, evidence on long-term maintenance of tutoring benefits beyond immediate post-tests is sparse, as are cost-effectiveness analyses across diverse educational settings. While AI-enabled tutoring shows promise, rigorous RCT validation and external assessments are needed to substantiate causal claims and refine the operational deployment of such models. Equity dimensions and access barriers require targeted research to ensure that tutoring expansions reduce, rather than exacerbate, achievement gaps.
