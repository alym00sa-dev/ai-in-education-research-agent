## Executive Summary

Generative AI tools, particularly large language models (LLMs) and multimodal AI systems, present emerging opportunities to enhance K-8 mathematics education by providing real-time, adaptive, and personalized tutoring, problem-solving assistance, and instructional support. Evidence from a living meta-analysis synthesizing 21 studies indicates generative AI tutoring yields small to moderate positive effects on mathematics achievement with Hedges’ g approximately ranging from 0.31 to 0.42, though effects vary substantially by design and context [7]. Importantly, large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experiments support that AI tutors embedded in hybrid human-AI models can achieve learning gains comparable to human tutors in math when active cognitive engagement is scaffolded and pedagogical safeguards are in place [94; 6]. For example, the LearnLM Team (2025) conducted an exploratory RCT (n=165 secondary students) finding that AI tutoring matched human tutor efficacy, and Thomas et al. (2023) replicated similar results in a quasi-experimental design with 15,700 students [6; 36].

Complementary quasi-experimental evidence underlines mechanisms facilitating these gains, such as reducing teacher workload via automated lesson planning and assessment generation, personalized scaffolding for diverse learners—including students with disabilities and linguistic minorities—and iterative prompt engineering to promote student self-regulation and cognitive effort [15; 156; 86]. However, risks of overreliance on AI-generated answers threaten deep problem-solving, creativity, and long-term skill retention unless mitigated by teacher-led safeguards and thoughtful integration [10; 9].

For the tiered sub-questions: foundational framing of generative AI capabilities within K-8 math is well articulated, with evidence clarifying tool functionalities, pedagogical roles, and ethical considerations such as privacy and bias [7; 11; 120]. Baseline instructional models using traditional or non-generative digital tools are fully characterized through meta-analytic benchmarks (effect sizes 0.18–0.70 for non-AI adaptive tools), offering clear comparators [96]. Mechanistic insights regarding how AI fosters engagement and understanding through personalized, adaptive tutoring and teacher support are moderately supported by RCTs, qualitative studies, and observational data—though emotional and motivational dimensions of AI tutors remain emerging [6; 7; 156]. Comparative evidence on generative AI’s impacts over traditional instruction is partial, with small-to-moderate effect size consistency but persistent heterogeneity and notable gaps in longitudinal and equity-focused data [7; 6; 24].

Overall confidence in conclusions is moderate. The strongest findings derive from meta-analyses and rigorously conducted RCTs verifying small but consistent benefits of AI tutoring systems on math achievement and engagement. However, effectiveness varies by instructional design, student demographics, and implementation fidelity, indicating the necessity of human-AI collaboration and teacher professional development to optimize outcomes. The most critical gap is the paucity of large-scale, long-term RCTs focused on K-8 students that explore sustained impacts on conceptual understanding, creativity, skill retention, and socio-emotional dimensions. Additionally, empirical assessment of ethical, equity, and culturally responsive AI personalization frameworks remains limited.

## Generative AI Tools and Foundational Context in K-8 Math

Generative AI tools are defined primarily as advanced large language models (LLMs) capable of producing original human-like text, explanations, problem solutions, and personalized content through natural language interaction [7; 11]. In K-8 math education, these tools serve as dynamic tutors, offering adaptive feedback, scaffolding problem-solving processes, and generating tailored learning sequences [7]. The core targeted outcomes include math achievement, conceptual understanding, problem-solving skills, and engagement. Studies reflect diverse K-8 contexts—classrooms and remote learning environments—with heterogeneous populations inclusive of neurotypical students and some special education groups [7; 86].

Foundational framings emphasize the rapidly evolving AI capabilities and accompanying ethical concerns including algorithmic bias, transparency, student data privacy, and equitable access. Teacher digital and AI literacy are foundational for responsible integration, with professional development identified as critical for handling AI limitations and maximizing pedagogical benefits [7; 120]. The unpredictable nature of generative AI outputs necessitates instructional designs that actively monitor accuracy and content appropriateness [7].

## Baseline Instructional Practices and Non-Generative Digital Tool Comparators

Traditional K-8 math instruction employs teacher-led lessons, textbooks, physical manipulatives, and standardized formative and summative assessments such as TerraNova, CAASPP, PISA, and TIMSS [96]. Non-generative digital tools like DreamBox, Math Blaster, and ASSISTments provide adaptive pathways and formative feedback but lack the conversational and generative capabilities of modern AI [96].

These baseline tools have been shown to yield moderate gains in math achievement. Meta-analytic benchmarks in this field indicate effect sizes from approximately 0.18 to as high as 0.70 for procedural math skills depending on intervention fidelity and target skills [96]. Surveys report that of surveyed K-12 math teachers, about half have used generative AI tools—primarily for supporting lesson planning, assessment creation, and differentiated instruction—though direct student tutoring remains less frequent and experimental [7].

## Mechanisms of Action and Implementation Strategies for Generative AI in K-8 Math

Integrating generative AI tools into K-8 instruction requires careful attention to maintaining student cognitive engagement and promoting self-regulated learning. Pedagogical strategies often include hybrid human-AI tutoring models, where teachers provide relational and motivational support while AI delivers adaptive problem-solving assistance and feedback [6; 10]. Such models have demonstrated improved lesson completion rates and engagement outcomes relative to AI-only tutoring [6; 10].

AI’s adaptive feedback can scaffold conceptual understanding and reduce cognitive load, particularly benefiting students with special education needs or language barriers by tailoring tasks to individual profiles [86; 24]. However, emotion detection and AI-driven emotional feedback remain early-stage, pilot-tested technologies with mixed efficacy in reducing math anxiety or enhancing motivation [9].

Teacher factors—self-efficacy, AI knowledge, and beliefs—moderate adoption and effective classroom use of AI tutors. Professional development programs aimed at increasing AI literacy show statistically significant improvement in teachers’ readiness to integrate AI tools ethically and pedagogically [120; 7]. AI also contributes to operational efficiencies by automating lesson planning and formative assessment generation, though these outputs require iterative refinement and human oversight due to potential inaccuracies or biases [156].

Generative AI serves multifaceted roles in mathematics education: expert solver, tutor delivering adaptive feedback, peer learner modelling student reasoning, and co-creator supporting collaborative tasks—all supporting active cognitive engagement when combined with human instruction [7; 105].

## Comparative Effectiveness and Implications of Generative AI Relative to Traditional Methods

Experimental research, including RCTs and large quasi-experiments, indicates that generative AI tutoring can yield math achievement gains comparable to human tutoring when active cognitive engagement is promoted through pedagogical guardrails. The LearnLM Team (2025) conducted an exploratory RCT (n=165 secondary students) demonstrating AI tutor parity with human tutors [6]. Similarly, Thomas et al. (2023) quasi-experiment (n=15,700) showed hybrid human-AI models outperform AI-only tutors and standard instruction [36].

Safety mechanisms are critical because overreliance on AI-produced solutions can impair problem-solving skills, creativity, and long-term retention. Bastani et al. (2024) reported quasi-experimental evidence that lack of AI guardrails led to skill atrophy [10]. Prompt engineering and teacher mediation mitigate risks by discouraging shortcutting and encouraging conceptual reasoning [9].

AI-generated lesson plans tend to be rated lower than human plans at elementary levels but gain acceptance in secondary education and for structured tasks, suggesting generative AI is best employed in partnership with human educators [156]. Student attitudes toward AI tutors are predominantly positive and optimistic, contrasting with teacher caution largely concerned with ethical, accuracy, and usability issues [156; 7]. Equity considerations reveal AI’s potential to support marginalized learners via personalized scaffolds, though risks from digital divides and implicit bias require vigilant governance and culturally responsive design [86; 24].

### Synthesis and Implications

The body of evidence suggests that generative AI tools can enhance K-8 mathematics education through personalized, adaptive tutoring and teacher support functions. Gains in achievement and engagement across varied K-8 populations indicate promising utility when AI is embedded within pedagogically sound frameworks emphasizing human collaboration. The strongest signals emerge from hybrid tutoring models that combine AI-driven problem-solving assistance with teacher scaffolding, addressing cognitive and affective needs simultaneously.

Practical implications for schools include investing in teacher professional development to improve AI literacy and instructional design competence; establishing ethical, privacy, and equity governance structures; and adopting AI as an assistive tool rather than replacement for human instruction. Positive student engagement and motivation may be harnessed via carefully tailored AI prompts combined with emotional support strategies, although the latter are nascent and warrant further development.

Schools and districts must be mindful of potential digital access inequities and the need for culturally responsive AI content to avoid exacerbating existing disparities. Furthermore, AI-generated resources should undergo human validation and iterative improvement to ensure pedagogical appropriateness and accuracy.

---

### Limitations and Research Gaps

Despite encouraging findings, significant evidence gaps limit comprehensive conclusions. Large-scale, rigorous, longitudinal randomized controlled trials focusing exclusively on generative AI tutoring's sustained impact on K-8 math outcomes—especially conceptual understanding, creativity, and socio-emotional development—are lacking. Studies examining differential effects for special education students, linguistic minorities, and low socioeconomic status populations remain scarce. The emotive and motivational capacities of AI tutors await robust empirical validation.

Comparative studies directly contrasting generative AI interventions with established non-generative adaptive tools in diverse classroom settings are minimal, hampering clear comparative effectiveness judgments. Ethical, privacy, and equity frameworks for generative AI use in education require largescale real-world testing and validation. Publication bias and heterogeneity across studies diminish overall certainty and generalizability.
