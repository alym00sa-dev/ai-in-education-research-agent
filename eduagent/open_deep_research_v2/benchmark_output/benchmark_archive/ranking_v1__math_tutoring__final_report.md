## Executive Summary

The preponderance of high-quality evidence supports tutoring as an effective educational intervention to improve mathematics outcomes among K-8 students compared to standard classroom instruction without tutoring. Meta-analyses and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) consistently demonstrate that tutoring programs yield small to moderate positive effects on math achievement, typically with effect sizes ranging from approximately d=0.20 to d=0.38. These effects are robust across diverse learner populations, with indications of stronger benefits for students from low socioeconomic status (SES) backgrounds and those with lower prior achievement. Both one-to-one and small group tutoring are effective delivery models, with some meta-analytic evidence favoring small groups for scalability and comparable effectiveness. Furthermore, tutoring by trained paraprofessionals or teaching assistants often achieves outcomes similar to those of certified teachers when combined with structured protocols and professional development.

Among the strongest RCTs, [211] meta-analyzed 36 studies involving low-SES elementary and middle school students, reporting an effect size of d=0.36 for tutoring interventions, with teacher-led tutoring at d=0.59 and paraprofessional-led tutoring at d=0.46 (meta-analysis, 36 studies). [227] similarly found tutoring programs produce an average effect size of d=0.38 for one-to-one tutoring in PreK-12 populations (meta-analysis, numerous studies). [147] analyzed 23 elementary math tutoring studies and reported an overall effect size of d=0.20 (meta-analysis, 23 studies). [155] conducted an RCT of synchronous online tutoring for struggling 6th to 8th graders (n=119) finding large within-group gains (d=+0.95 to +1.47), although between-group effects did not reach statistical significance (RCT, n=119). [255] evaluated hybrid human-AI tutoring across three U.S. middle schools (n=over 500), demonstrating improved engagement, lesson completion, and skill proficiency with stronger gains in lower achieving students (QED, n=varied). [156] reported an RCT of Tutor CoPilot, a human-AI tutor assistant, found a 4 percentage point increase in mastery likelihood for students tutored by those using the system (RCT, n=1800), with larger 9-point gains for lower-performing tutors.

Corroborating these experimental findings are quasi-experimental and observational studies showing the importance of tutoring dosage, fidelity, tutor training, and social-emotional learning integration. For example, [156] dosage analyses indicated higher attendance correlates with improved learning outcomes (QED, n=188). [181] found peer tutoring increases student motivation and social relationships, though instructional quality varied (observational, n=multiple schools). Studies highlight positive impacts on reducing math anxiety, especially through peer tutoring and tailored one-on-one interventions, supporting the role of motivation and affect in tutoring success [193]. Implementation challenges, including inconsistent attendance and variable dosage, routinely moderate observed effects, emphasizing fidelity as a critical factor [201].

The available evidence answers foundational Tier 1 questions comprehensively by defining tutoring as supplemental instruction delivered primarily one-on-one or in small groups targeting mathematical numeracy and problem-solving from elementary through middle school grades. Tier 2 baseline conditions typically comprise standard classroom instruction without additional tutoring or alternative supplemental supports, a comparator commonly used in the included RCTs and meta-analyses. Tier 3 questions on mechanisms and implementation are partially addressed: evidence supports that individualized pacing, formative feedback, social-emotional support, and tutor training enhance effectiveness; however, disentangling the distinct contribution of these components remains limited due to bundled interventions in the literature. Tier 4 comparative evidence fully supports tutoring over no supplemental instruction, reveals moderate effect sizes with some heterogeneity across populations and delivery modes, but comparators to alternative support strategies like computer-assisted instruction or summer programs are less frequent and yield mixed findings.

Overall confidence in the effectiveness of tutoring for improving K-8 mathematics outcomes is high, grounded in multiple meta-analyses and well-designed RCTs. The most important caveat is the variability introduced by differences in implementation fidelity, dosage adherence, and tutor qualifications. Incomplete attendance and inconsistent application of tutoring protocols commonly reduce real-world effectiveness. Additionally, the evidence base on long-term durability of tutoring gains beyond immediate post-tests, detailed causal mechanisms of effectiveness, and targeted impacts on subpopulations such as English learners and students with math anxiety remain less well developed. Emerging hybrid human-AI tutoring models show promise but require further rigorous randomized trials to establish long-term impact and equity.

## Definitions and Target Outcomes in K-8 Tutoring

Tutoring in K-8 mathematics typically entails supplemental instruction delivered individually (one-to-one) or in small groups (up to five students), focusing on foundational mathematical knowledge, computational fluency, problem-solving, mathematical reasoning, and application skills. Programs target discrete areas such as number sense, basic operations, fractions, and early algebra skills [211][147]. Math proficiency is measured using standardized math tests, curriculum-aligned assessments, and operationalized constructs such as numeracy and mathematical reasoning.

Populations studied predominantly include elementary (grades K-5) and middle school (grades 6-8) students, with many trials conducted among low-SES or at-risk learners, including students with lower prior achievement [211]. A smaller number of studies focus explicitly on English learners or students with math anxiety. Emerging research integrates students with diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds but remains limited [256][193].

Comparison groups in tutoring evaluations most commonly are standard classroom instruction without additional tutoring or alternative supplemental interventions such as computer-assisted instruction, summer programs, or peer tutoring programs [155][201].

## Tutoring Models, Delivery, and Mechanisms

Tutoring models observed in research fall primarily into three categories: one-to-one tutoring, small-group tutoring (typically 2–5 students), and peer or cross-age tutoring. One-to-one tutoring is frequently regarded as the gold standard with larger effect sizes but limited scalability. Small-group tutoring often offers comparable outcomes with potentially better cost-effectiveness and reach [147][206]. Peer tutoring is shown to yield smaller but positive effects, especially when tutors are trained and reciprocal interaction is encouraged [213][181].

Delivery modes include in-person tutoring primarily within school settings, though online synchronous tutoring is increasingly studied. For example, [155] examined online synchronous math tutoring delivered twice weekly over 20 weeks to struggling middle school students, with large within-group gains (d=+0.95; d=+1.47) though between-group differences were not statistically significant (RCT, n=119). Hybrid human-AI tutoring models have recently emerged, combining AI software with human tutors who provide motivational and instructional support during school hours [255]. Such models increase engagement and skill mastery while reducing per-student costs.

Learning mechanisms identified include individualized pacing aligned with student skill level, frequent formative assessment with immediate feedback, metacognitive scaffolding, motivational and affective support, and progress monitoring [211][156]. High tutor quality, including training to provide effort-based praise and maintain productive struggle, is critical for promoting engagement and cognitive growth [156][156].

Attendance and dosage intensity are crucial moderators; higher session frequency and total tutoring time positively correlate with improved outcomes up to certain thresholds, beyond which returns diminish or plateau [211][156].

## Quantitative Evidence of Tutoring Impacts

Meta-analyses and RCTs provide strong evidence of tutoring's effectiveness in improving K-8 mathematical outcomes. [211] synthesized 36 studies of elementary and middle school tutoring programs for low-SES students, reporting an overall effect size of d=0.36 (meta-analysis, 36 studies). Teacher-led tutoring produced larger effects (d=0.59) than paraprofessional-led tutoring (d=0.46). Frequency of tutoring sessions showed a small positive association with effect size (d=0.02 per 10 sessions), while longer intervention duration showed a small negative association (d=-0.09 per 10 weeks) suggesting implementation duration impacts may be nonlinear.

[227] meta-analyzed dozens of tutoring interventions across preK-12 and found one-to-one tutoring had an average effect size of d=0.38 (meta-analysis). Teacher-led tutoring was more effective than paraprofessional- or parent-led. Pelligrini et al. (2021) analyzed 23 elementary math tutoring studies with an overall effect size of d=0.20 (meta-analysis), reinforcing the efficacy of small group and individual tutoring.

[155] implemented a synchronous online tutoring program for struggling middle school students (RCT, n=119) showing large within-group effect sizes of d=+0.95 and +1.47. However, between-group comparisons using propensity score matching were not statistically significant, likely due to sample size and study design.

[255] evaluated hybrid human-AI tutoring across three U.S. middle schools serving predominantly low-income students (QED, n=varied). Hybrid human-AI tutoring increased time on task, lessons completed, and number of skills mastered, with effects concentrated among students with lower baseline performance.

An RCT of Tutor CoPilot (Wang et al., 2024, n=1,800 students) demonstrated a 4 percentage point increase in mastery likelihood for students taught by tutors using the AI support system, and a 9 percentage point gain among students tutored by the lowest-rated tutors (RCT). This shows AI can augment tutor effectiveness and reduce instructional quality gaps.

## Quasi-Experimental and Observational Evidence

Dosage studies repeatedly highlight the association between attendance rates and outcome efficacy. [156] reported in their dosage analysis with 188 elementary struggling readers (QED) that students attending more tutoring sessions made significantly greater improvements in reading comprehension and decoding skills. Similarly, [201] showed that tutoring programs implemented after COVID-19 experienced lower-than-planned attendance, limiting overall math impact (QED).

Peer tutoring observational studies [181] reveal positive student engagement and social-emotional outcomes, yet instructional practices often lack scaffolds such as manipulatives, connections across content domains, and timely feedback, potentially constraining academic effect sizes.

Observational evidence also underlines the significance of tutor talk time ratios, with [156] demonstrating that providing real-time feedback to tutors to balance student-tutor talk time improved mathematical reasoning in virtual tutoring sessions.

## Evidence on Math Anxiety and English Learners

Math anxiety is a pervasive barrier to math achievement in K-8 students and is effectively mitigated by tutoring interventions. [193] conducted a quasi-experimental study with 420 middle school students (QED) showing peer tutoring reduced math learning anxiety with large effect sizes (Hedges’ g=0.84 for learning anxiety; 0.42 for evaluation anxiety). This effect appeared invariant to gender and grade, reinforcing peer tutoring as a promising intervention.

Targeted one-on-one interventions including cognitive tutoring and digital anxiety remediation have been shown to normalize neural correlates of math anxiety and associate reductions in anxiety with improved performance (RCTs and systematic reviews)[258][236].

Tutoring for English learners (ELLs) improves academic progress when tutors employ strategies including translation, modeling, and partnership building (observational and quasi-experimental reports)[256][316]. However, rigorous RCTs evaluating tutoring specifically designed for ELL populations in mathematics remain limited.

## Comparative Effectiveness and Alternative Interventions

Tutoring achieves larger effect sizes on math outcomes than many alternative supplemental interventions such as computer-assisted instruction, summer learning programs, or expanded instructional time [211][227].

Computer-assisted math programs and Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) produce smaller but significant positive effects (d=0.09 to 0.27) often less than in-person tutoring, though hybrid human-AI tutoring models show promise for enhancing engagement and learning at lower cost [228][255].

Peer tutoring offers a lower-cost alternative with modest effect sizes (Hedges g ~0.33), but effectiveness varies by tutor training quality and program structure [213].

---

## Synthesis and Implications

The comprehensive body of evidence affirms that tutoring is a powerful intervention to improve K-8 mathematics outcomes, outperforming many alternative supplemental supports. Best effects are achieved through structured, manualized programs delivered by trained teachers or paraprofessionals in small groups or one-to-one formats. Hybrid human-AI tutoring systems could expand scalability and reduce costs while maintaining or improving student engagement and mastery.

Implementation fidelity, including tutor training, session frequency, and dosage adherence, is critical for tapping tutoring’s potential. Social-emotional supports integrated in tutoring, including feedback and anxiety reduction, enhance student motivation likely contributing to improved math learning. Peer tutoring offers a promising, scalable approach with affective and academic benefits but depends on tutor preparation and instructional quality.

Schools and policymakers should prioritize investments in tutoring infrastructure that ensures adequate dosage and tutor quality. Combining human expertise with AI assistance represents a promising innovation deserving rigorous evaluation for long-term effectiveness and equity implications.

---

## Limitations and Research Gaps

The evidence base shows several limitations. Longitudinal follow-ups assessing the durability of tutoring gains post-intervention are scarce. Most studies bundle components (tutor training, instructional materials, dosage) preventing isolation of key active ingredients. The quality and consistency of tutor training and implementation fidelity varies across settings and directly affects outcomes but lacks systematic study.

There is insufficient rigorous randomized evidence on tutoring for English learners and students with math anxiety, particularly in middle school. While preliminary data affirm benefits, targeted RCTs are needed. Emerging hybrid human-AI models show promise but lack extensive large-scale randomized evaluations and strongly diverse population representation.

Comparisons between tutoring and other popular supplemental supports, especially computer-assisted instruction, reveal tutoring’s superiority but are limited by heterogenous intervention designs and outcome measures. Economic analyses of cost-effectiveness across tutoring models, including hybrid AI delivery, are limited and demand further research.
