## Executive Summary

The current body of evidence indicates that generative AI tools show promising potential to enhance K-8 mathematics education by providing adaptive, personalized support that complements traditional instruction and conventional educational technologies. A recent living meta-analysis synthesizing 21 studies with 38 effect sizes reports a small positive average effect size of g = 0.42 for generative AI interventions on mathematics learning outcomes, albeit with considerable heterogeneity depending on context, instructional design, and learner characteristics [5]. These tools' affordances, including natural language interaction, adaptive feedback, and personalized lesson planning, align well with the developmental and cognitive needs of K-8 students, particularly benefiting foundational concept comprehension and motivation.

Among the strongest experimental evidence, the living meta-analysis by Strohmaier, Bödefeld, and Reinhold (2026) provides robust meta-analytic evidence demonstrating small but consistent positive effects of generative AI on mathematics achievement across educational levels, though mostly inferred from studies including older students beyond K-8 [5]. Bastani et al. (2025) conducted a large-scale randomized controlled trial with high school students using GPT-4 as an AI tutor in mathematics. The trial found that while AI support improved student performance during practice, students who became reliant on generative AI showed diminished skill acquisition once AI assistance was withdrawn. The study highlighted that configuring AI tutors to deliver teacher-designed hints rather than direct answers mitigated this harmful effect [9]. Another quasi-experimental case study by Rizos, Foykas, and Georgakopoulos (2024) reported engagement and positive math outcomes for 8th-grade students with special educational needs using ChatGPT-generated worksheets, illustrating generative AI’s utility in tailored instruction and inclusivity [29]. Surveys of K-12 teachers indicate substantial adoption of generative AI tools primarily for lesson planning and assessment development, with professional development and teacher attitudes critically influencing usage frequency and effectiveness [85].

Observational and survey studies corroborate and provide nuance to the experimental results. Liu et al. (2025) analyzed nearly 1,000 math and science educators and found 50% reported generative AI use, predominantly for upstream tasks rather than direct student instruction, with uptake moderated by technological readiness and pedagogical beliefs [25]. Studies find generative AI fosters self-regulated learning, critical thinking, and higher-order cognitive skills such as synthesis when integrated with pedagogical scaffolding, though sample sizes are generally small and findings preliminary [25]. Additional research reveals ethical and implementation challenges – including AI inaccuracies, risk of overreliance, digital divides, and inequities – with wide variation in teacher readiness and infrastructural support across diverse K-8 settings [173]. The literature also highlights the importance of hybrid human-AI models, combining teacher oversight and AI assistance to maximize engagement and durable learning outcomes [27].

Addressing the four tiers of inquiry: Tier 1 (Foundational Framing) is partially answered; definitions of generative AI tools and relevant math outcomes are documented in meta-analyses and qualitative reports, with recognition of developmental appropriateness for K-8 learners [5][173]. Tier 2 (Baseline and Existing Approaches) is partially resolved; traditional instruction and other educational technologies are well understood, but direct comparative evidence explicitly contrasting generative AI tools with conventional EdTech in K-8 math remains scarce [5][27]. Tier 3 (Mechanisms and Implementation) is partially addressed; studies begin to unpack generative AI integration modes (e.g., teacher-led vs student-driven), cognitive and motivational mechanisms, and professional development needs [85][25]. Tier 4 (Comparative Evidence and Implications) remains largely unresolved at scale; while some rigorous RCTs exist at high school level, large-scale randomized trials in authentic K-8 settings comparing generative AI with traditional instruction or other technologies are limited, as are comprehensive moderator analyses for diverse populations [5][9].

Overall, confidence is moderate due to the nascent and heterogeneous evidence base with small sample sizes and limited longitudinal data specific to K-8 populations. The single most critical caveat is that without careful pedagogical integration and teacher-guided safeguards, generative AI tools risk undermining durable learning outcomes by promoting overreliance or exposing students to inaccurate information. Effective deployment depends on thoughtful instructional design, ongoing teacher professional development, equitable access to technology, and further rigorous research with diverse, representative K-8 populations.

## Findings and Analysis

### Definitions and Characteristics of Generative AI in K-8 Mathematics Education (Tier 1)

Generative AI tools used in K-8 math education commonly involve large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT and GPT-4 variants capable of generating text explanations, adaptive feedback, and customized instructional content [5]. The generative AI affordances extend beyond traditional AI tutoring systems by enabling open-ended natural language interaction, multimodal content generation, and dynamic problem-solving support [5]. Relevant math skills emphasized include computational fluency, conceptual understanding, problem-solving, and engagement metrics, with an emphasis on higher-order skills such as synthesis and creativity when scaffolded appropriately [25].

Populations largely studied include upper elementary and middle school students, but many primary studies in broader age ranges (including high school) inform the K-8 context due to limited exclusive K-8 trials [5][29]. Students with special educational needs, English language learners, and underserved populations are beginning to be included, though systematic inclusion remains limited [29][173].

### Traditional Instruction and Other Educational Technologies (Tier 2)

Baseline instruction in K-8 mathematics commonly uses direct teaching, textbooks, and conventional educational technologies such as interactive math software, intelligent tutoring systems (ITS), and adaptive curriculum platforms [5]. Comparative evidence on traditional methods indicates moderate effectiveness but lacks personalization and dynamic feedback capacities now afforded by AI tools [27]. Traditional ITS have shown positive impacts on mathematics achievement but generally provide structured, rule-based guidance rather than generative content [5]. Generative AI’s novel capacity for open-ended interaction and adaptive problem generation distinguishes it from these predecessors but necessitates empirical comparison [5].

### Instructional Integration and Delivery Models (Tier 3)

Generative AI implementation in K-8 settings varies from teacher-led integrations—where educators use AI to generate lesson plans, assessments, and tailored instructional materials—to student-driven models where learners interact directly with AI tutors for problem-solving and feedback [25][85]. Survey data indicate most K-12 teachers using AI employ it primarily for lesson planning and administrative tasks, with less frequent direct student interaction [25]. Evidence supports hybrid human-AI models, combining teacher oversight with AI tutoring, as promising for engagement and durable learning outcomes [27].

Cognitive mechanisms engaged by generative AI include scaffolding, immediate adaptive feedback, amplification of self-regulated learning strategies, and promotion of higher-order thinking skills (analysis, synthesis, creativity), particularly when instructional design aligns AI tasks with educational goals [25]. Motivational mechanisms involve increased engagement and perceived learner autonomy, though risks of reduced cognitive effort exist without appropriate scaffolding [9][25]. Professional development focusing on AI literacy and pedagogy is crucial to optimize these mechanisms [85].

### Comparative Effectiveness and Contextual Factors (Tier 4)

The living meta-analysis by Strohmaier et al. (2026) is the most comprehensive source of comparative evidence, indicating a small positive effect size (g = 0.42) for generative AI on mathematics learning outcomes with moderate heterogeneity attributable to contextual and learner differences [5]. The high school RCT by Bastani et al. (2025) indicates that generative AI support enhances interactive practice but causes lower durable learning if students over-rely on AI without safeguards; configuring AI to present teacher-designed hints mitigates this [9]. No similarly large-scale RCTs focused exclusively on K-8 populations were found, highlighting a significant gap.

Case studies with 8th-grade students with special educational needs report generative AI contributions to engagement and achievement gains [29]. Teacher surveys underscore the importance of technological access, infrastructure, professional development, and ethical concerns in moderating effectiveness and uptake [85][25]. Overall, the evidence for generative AI’s superiority or equivalence relative to traditional or other digital educational technologies remains inconclusive due to insufficient direct comparative studies [5][27].

### Equity, Ethics, and Practical Challenges

Multiple studies identify significant challenges in equitable access to generative AI technologies due to digital divides and disparities in infrastructure and teacher readiness [173][85]. Ethical issues around data privacy, bias, academic integrity, misinformation, and AI transparency are prominent and insufficiently addressed in current empirical work [173]. Teacher professional development emerges as a key lever to support ethical and effective AI use, yet systemic infrastructural and policy supports remain uneven [85]. The risk of generative AI undermining creativity and independent problem-solving without thoughtful instructional design is a recurrent theme [9][25].

### Synthesis and Implications

Generative AI tools exhibit noteworthy potential to enhance K-8 mathematics education by enabling adaptive, personalized learning environments that better meet individual student needs compared to traditional and prior educational technologies. The ability to generate customized problem sets, provide natural language feedback, and scaffold higher-order thinking skills aligns well with cognitive developmental stages of K-8 learners, especially in foundational conceptual mathematics.

However, evidence reveals that generative AI tools must be thoughtfully integrated within pedagogically sound frameworks that involve consistent teacher oversight and safeguards to prevent cognitive offloading and misinformation dependence. Hybrid instructional models that combine human teachers and AI tutors appear most promising in promoting engagement and durable learning gains. Professional development designed to boost teacher AI literacy and pedagogical competency is critical for successful implementation.

Equity considerations must be central to deployment strategies as disparities in digital access and teacher readiness risk further entrenching educational inequalities. Ethical oversight and curriculum adaptations are necessary to ensure responsible AI use, transparency, and sustained development of critical thinking and creativity among students. Researchers and practitioners should prioritize longitudinal and rigorously controlled studies that compare generative AI tools directly to other educational technologies and traditional instruction, with a focus on diverse and underserved K-8 populations to fully understand generative AI’s benefits and limitations.

The necessity of continuously updated evidence syntheses such as living meta-analyses or registries of AI educational impacts is clear due to the rapidly evolving technology landscape. Policymakers must also engage with thoughtful regulatory frameworks that promote equitable access, data privacy, and educational excellence.

---

### Limitations and Research Gaps

The evidence base is limited by the following methodological and contextual weaknesses:

- Most rigorous experimental studies focus on high school or mixed-age populations, with few large-scale, randomized controlled trials specifically targeting K-8 students [5][9].
- Longitudinal evidence on the durability of learning gains and transfer of problem-solving skills post generative AI intervention is scant [5].
- Direct comparative trials contrasting generative AI tools with traditional or other educational technology interventions in K-8 math are lacking, limiting causal inferences about relative effectiveness [5][27].
- Studies on diverse subpopulations, including students with disabilities, English language learners, and low socioeconomic status cohorts, remain small and mostly qualitative [29][173].
- Many studies rely on self-reported adoption and perceive usage surveys with limited performance or outcome data [25].
- Ethical and equity issues related to bias, privacy, access asymmetries, and AI content accuracy are underexplored in empirical research [173].
- Professional development and infrastructural readiness as moderators of generative AI effectiveness need detailed study and large-scale data [85].
- There is limited evidence on AI’s impact on socio-emotional learning, creativity, and student motivation beyond brief or exploratory analyses [25].

Addressing these gaps requires large-scale, longitudinal, controlled trials explicitly designed around K-8 mathematics education, integrated with qualitative investigations of teacher and student experiences and systemic policy analyses.

### Body of Evidence Maturity: EMERGING  
Justification: The evidence base on generative AI application in K-8 mathematics education is emerging, featuring rigorous meta-analytic and randomized controlled trial data predominantly from secondary and mixed-age populations, with limited studies focused exclusively on K-8. The evidence consistently shows small positive effects but remains heterogeneous and context-dependent. Large-scale, longitudinal, and population-representative randomized trials specific to K-8 learners and thorough empirical investigations into equity, ethics, and instructional mechanisms are still needed to advance confidence and practical implementation at scale.
