## Executive Summary

The retrieved evidence suggests that generative AI can support K–12 mathematics learning in some structured, tutor-like uses, but it does not yet provide a stable basis for claiming broad achievement gains across grades, settings, or outcomes. The direct evidence base is dominated by a few recent field and classroom studies plus a larger set of reviews, benchmarks, and implementation papers. The clearest causal warning comes from high school mathematics: one field experiment found that generative AI improved performance during supported practice but reduced later unassisted performance when safeguards were absent [34]. At the same time, one randomized study found that ChatGPT-generated mathematics help produced learning gains equivalent to human tutor-authored help in an online mathematics environment, suggesting that AI can sometimes produce useful instructional text when tightly constrained [112]. A newer randomized study in UK classrooms reported that an AI tutor could safely and effectively support secondary students’ mathematics learning, but the retrieved source is a web-linked report rather than a fully detailed journal article, so the precise outcome statistics were not retrievable here [57]. Taken together, the evidence points to a conditional conclusion: generative AI is potentially useful as a scaffolded support, but unstructured use can undermine independent learning.

The strongest experimental findings are limited in number but highly informative. The high-school field experiment on guardrails found harmful downstream effects when students had unstructured access to generative AI during mathematics practice; the retrieved summary indicates higher immediate practice performance but lower later test performance, with harm reduced when the AI was constrained to provide teacher-designed hints rather than full answers [34]. The randomized comparison of ChatGPT-generated help versus human tutor-authored help found equivalent learning gains on mathematics skills, showing that generative AI can generate tutoring-style explanations that are pedagogically useful under controlled conditions [112]. The exploratory UK classroom trial of a pedagogy-fine-tuned AI tutor found the system could support mathematics learning safely and effectively in real classrooms, but the retrieved material does not report the exact effect size, sample size, or delayed outcomes [57]. Beyond these, the broader synthesis literature consistently argues that the field is early-stage, that most retrieved items are indirect or design-oriented, and that causal evidence for student outcomes is sparse [8][10][62][66][122].

The quasi-experimental and observational evidence largely corroborates the implementation story rather than overturning it. In primary schools, a quasi-experimental mathematics word-problem study using a generative-AI-enabled learning environment reported positive learning and attitude effects, but the retrieved source does not expose enough detail to judge durability or independence of learning [54]. A second quasi-experimental study of a ChatGPT-based flipped learning guiding approach found improved courseware project performance and perceptions relative to a traditional flipped approach, although this is postsecondary rather than K–12 and thus only indirectly informative [76]. In elementary and middle school implementation studies, AI learning companions, teachable agents, and teacher co-design projects repeatedly emphasize teacher mediation, curriculum alignment, and constrained prompting as the conditions under which generative AI is most promising [41][96][142]. Observational work on teacher adoption and AI literacy also shows that teacher trust, acceptance, and perceived usefulness are major moderators of implementation quality [6][104][138]. By contrast, benchmark studies showing that chatbots can solve some school math items tell us more about model capability than about student learning, and several benchmarks still show substantial weakness on K–12 math reasoning, misconceptions, and robustness to linguistic variation [20][26][33][63][73][77][80][82][86].

The tiered questions are only partially answered overall. Tier 1, on foundational framing, is answered reasonably well: generative AI in K–12 math most often means chatbots, hint generators, explanation tools, question generators, and teacher-facing planning systems, with relevant outcomes spanning achievement, procedural fluency, conceptual understanding, problem solving, transfer, and engagement [8][10][17][25][62][139]. Tier 2, on baseline comparators, is answered moderately well: the strongest counterfactual is standard instruction plus teacher feedback, human tutoring, and non-AI digital tools, with adjacent evidence from intelligent tutoring systems and online homework showing that adaptive feedback and worked examples can improve learning [1][42][95][141]. Tier 3, on mechanisms and implementation, is partially answered and probably the best-developed area: teacher mediation, guardrails, prompt quality, curriculum alignment, and limiting autonomous answer-seeking all appear critical, while simple AI literacy alone may not prevent overreliance [34][117][138][149]. Tier 4, on comparative effectiveness, remains only partially resolved: there is credible evidence of both benefit and harm, but not enough direct, long-duration, K–12-specific randomized evidence to say that generative AI reliably improves math achievement relative to established alternatives. Overall confidence is low-to-moderate for effectiveness claims and moderate for implementation claims. The single most important caveat is that many positive reports are on immediate assistance, attitudes, or teacher workflow, not on durable independent mathematics achievement.

## Evidence Base and Study Types

The retrieved literature is heavily weighted toward reviews, benchmarks, design papers, and observational studies, with a smaller set of direct intervention studies. The most relevant synthesis papers conclude that the K–12 mathematics evidence base is early and fragmented [8][10][62][66][122]. A scoping survey of ChatGPT in mathematics education and systematic reviews of ChatGPT in mathematics teaching and learning both conclude that there are few high-quality intervention studies and that many published items focus on capability, design, or perceptions rather than student learning outcomes [8][10][62]. A 2026 review report likewise characterizes the evidence as limited and uneven, reinforcing the conclusion that direct causal evidence remains sparse [66].

This matters because the present question is not whether generative AI can produce mathematically plausible text, but whether it improves K–12 students’ independent math learning. Benchmarks such as mathematical capability tests, K–12 education evaluation sets, and misconceptions datasets provide useful information about model strengths and weaknesses, but they are only indirect evidence for student achievement [20][26][33][63][73][77][80][82][86]. They show that generative models can answer some school-level problems, but they also fail in ways that are educationally important, including brittleness under linguistic variation, weakness on misconception-aware reasoning, and inconsistent performance on higher-order or curriculum-specific tasks [26][33][63][73][80][82][86]. These capability studies support the need for guardrails, but they cannot establish learning gains.

## What Counts as Generative AI in K–12 Math

Across the literature, generative AI in K–12 mathematics includes chatbots, tutor-like dialogue systems, hint generators, question generators, worked-example generators, teacher planning assistants, and teachable agents [8][10][17][25][62][139]. Several papers emphasize that the important distinction is not simply whether a system uses an LLM, but whether it is configured to prompt reasoning, supply hints, and align with curriculum versus provide direct answers [34][112][142][148]. Teacher-facing tools also matter because they shape the materials students receive, even if the direct beneficiary is the teacher rather than the student [6][104][138].

The literature consistently treats the most relevant outcomes as achievement, procedural fluency, conceptual understanding, reasoning, and problem solving, with engagement and attitude as secondary outcomes [8][10][54][57][122]. However, most direct studies do not cleanly separate these domains. Many report immediate performance on practice tasks, satisfaction, or perceived usefulness rather than delayed independent mastery [34][54][57][96][108]. That limitation is central to the interpretation of the evidence.

## Baseline Instruction and Comparator Landscape

The natural baseline in K–12 mathematics remains teacher-led instruction supplemented by guided practice, independent practice, feedback, and homework support. Adjacent evidence from non-generative AI instructional tools shows that structured feedback, adaptive pacing, and worked examples can improve learning, especially when systems are integrated into instruction [1][42][95][141]. This is important because any claim for GenAI must be compared against a nontrivial counterfactual: schools already have access to teacher explanation, worksheets, online practice, and tutoring systems.

The comparator literature is strongest for intelligent tutoring systems and hybrid tutoring models. A large meta-analytic review of intelligent tutoring systems in K–12 mathematics indicates that well-designed ITS can improve learning, and a separate meta-analysis of technology-supported personalized learning also suggests positive effects in some contexts [141][1]. A quasi-experimental study of hybrid human-AI tutoring in middle school mathematics found that combining human expertise with AI support can improve outcomes relative to software alone, highlighting that AI is more likely to help when embedded in human-mediated instruction [42]. Earlier tutoring-system work on multimedia adaptive tutoring also supports the value of combining cognition, metacognition, and affect in structured digital support [95]. These adjacent findings are not direct evidence about generative AI, but they set the standard that any new AI tool must meet or exceed.

## Direct Experimental Evidence on Student Outcomes

The strongest direct evidence in the retrieved set is the high-school mathematics field experiment on guardrails. The study’s summary states that generative AI access improved practice-session performance but reduced later test performance, indicating that students may have relied on the tool in ways that weakened independent learning [34]. The same study indicates that restricting the system to teacher-designed hints rather than full solutions mitigated the harm, which implies that the mechanism is not simply “AI use,” but the instructional role the AI plays [34]. Because the retrieved material does not report exact sample size, effect size, or test statistics, those details remain not retrieved.

The most direct positive experimental finding is the randomized comparison of ChatGPT-generated help versus human tutor-authored help on mathematics skills [112]. That study found learning gains equivalent between ChatGPT-generated hints and human-authored tutor hints in an online mathematics environment [112]. This is important because it shows that generative AI can create pedagogically useful mathematics explanations when the task is constrained to hint generation rather than answer provision. However, the retrieved summary indicates an adult population and does not provide K–12-specific sample details, so the result is supportive but not fully generalizable to the focal population.

A newer randomized controlled trial in UK classrooms reported that AI tutoring could safely and effectively support secondary students’ mathematics learning [57]. The retrieved source is a short web-linked report, and the exact sample size, outcome scale, and effect estimate are not available in the provided text. Still, its existence is important because it shows that the field is moving from bench evaluation toward classroom trials. Similarly, another randomized study found that an AI instructional agent improved perceived learner control and learning outcomes compared with a traditional human teacher and a MOOC-style chatbot-supported condition, although this source is higher education rather than K–12 [64]. It is therefore only indirectly relevant, but it strengthens the broader hypothesis that carefully designed tutoring agents can help when they are embedded in structured pedagogy.

## Quasi-Experimental and Classroom Implementation Evidence

The main K–12 quasi-experimental result comes from a primary-school study of a generative-AI-enabled environment for mathematics word-problem solving [54]. The retrieved summary says the study reported positive learning and attitude effects, but it does not provide the exact sample, outcome metric, or follow-up duration. The main value of this study is that it points to promise in early grades and in word-problem contexts, where language and reasoning are both critical. At the same time, the lack of detailed reporting limits confidence in the durability and independence of the gains.

A separate quasi-experimental study on ChatGPT-based flipped learning guided approaches found better project performance and perceptions than a traditional flipped approach [76]. Because this study is undergraduate rather than K–12, it is not directly transferable. It is still informative for the general proposition that generative AI may help when it is embedded in structured instructional sequences rather than used as a free-response answer machine. The same is true for other quasi-experimental or design-based studies that focus on classroom pilots, teachable agents, or teacher co-design rather than standardized achievement [41][96][142][160].

Elementary and middle school design-based and case-study work repeatedly supports this implementation pattern. A generative-AI-powered teachable agent for middle school mathematics learning is presented as promising, but the study is design-based and does not provide the kind of causal evidence needed to infer achievement effects [23]. A middle-school case study on students’ perceived roles and challenges of a generative-AI-powered teachable agent similarly documents student interpretations rather than test-score effects [96]. An elementary-school AI learning companion study likewise focuses on classroom implementation and experience [41]. These studies indicate feasibility and acceptability, but they do not answer the achievement question directly.

## Teacher-Facing Generative AI and Classroom Design

Teacher-facing generative AI appears to be one of the most mature application areas in the retrieved literature. Preservice secondary mathematics teachers used ChatGPT as a lesson-planning assistant, with the study emphasizing how teachers reacted to the tool’s mathematical output and usefulness [14]. A separate study of pre-service teachers solving mathematics tasks with ChatGPT focuses on prompting techniques and responses to erroneous outputs [15]. Another report examines ChatGPT as a teacher ally for workload relief and burnout prevention in secondary schools, highlighting the noninstructional labor that AI might reduce [104]. These sources do not establish student achievement effects, but they show why teachers may adopt the tools: lesson preparation, assessment generation, and task customization.

Several studies also examine teacher-AI collaboration directly. Teacher perspectives on student-AI collaboration suggest that GenAI should be designed as a collaborative learning partner rather than a standalone tool [138]. Early classroom codesign work with secondary teachers reports that AI can function as a teaching partner, but only under conditions of careful orchestration [142]. Another middle-school study asks whether teachers should remain in the loop when using ChatGPT or GPT-4o to personalize math tasks [125]. These sources reinforce the central implementation claim: teacher mediation is likely necessary if generative AI is to support rather than replace mathematical thinking.

## Student Interaction, Hints, and Guardrails

The most important learning mechanism proposed in the literature is hint-based scaffolding. The randomized comparison of ChatGPT-generated and human-authored hints indicates that generative AI can author useful worked-solution support when confined to that role [112]. The high-school guardrails experiment goes further by showing that the educational effect depends on whether the AI reveals answers or provides teacher-designed hints [34]. This distinction recurs across review and design papers, which argue that immediate feedback and stepwise support are useful only when they preserve productive struggle [8][10][17][34][62].

Student overreliance is a major concern. The randomized AI-literacy intervention found that a brief training did not reduce overreliance on incorrect ChatGPT recommendations among high school students [117]. That result is important because it shows that simple awareness interventions may be insufficient; students may need systems that structurally prevent answer copying or misleading feedback. Related practitioner and conceptual sources emphasize prompt engineering, critical thinking, and responsible use, but those sources are not substitutes for evidence of learning effects [119][149][154]. The lesson is that student interactions with generative AI are not passive; how students ask, verify, and use the tool may determine whether learning occurs.

## Benchmark and Capability Evidence as an Indirect Comparator

The benchmark literature does not answer the educational question directly, but it does provide context for what generative models can and cannot do. ChatGPT’s mathematical capabilities were evaluated in benchmark settings that show some competence but also substantial limitations [20][110]. Evaluations of mathematical reasoning and problem solving on high-school exam items show that model performance is uneven and sensitive to item type [73][78][80]. Chinese and other K–12 benchmarks similarly show that models can solve some elementary and secondary problems but remain brittle in curriculum-specific or misconception-sensitive settings [77][82][86]. A benchmark on math misconceptions and another on automated feedback for open-ended responses emphasize that the hardest part is not just producing an answer, but identifying and responding to student thinking [26][27][31].

Some tools and technical studies attempt to move beyond final-answer correctness toward grounded tutoring or retrieval-augmented reasoning [20][35][43][50][52][81][87][91][97][102][114]. Those studies matter because K–12 mathematics learning depends heavily on explanation quality and stepwise reasoning. Yet they remain indirect: better model answers do not automatically translate into better student learning. In fact, the harm study shows that even a capable system may reduce learning if it gives students too much help at the wrong time [34].

## Adjacent Evidence from Intelligent Tutoring Systems and Human-AI Tutoring

When direct GenAI evidence is thin, the best comparator evidence comes from intelligent tutoring systems and human-AI hybrid tutoring. The ITS meta-analytic literature indicates that structured, feedback-rich tutoring systems can improve K–12 mathematics learning [141][1]. A long-standing adaptive tutoring system that addresses cognition, metacognition, and affect illustrates the design principle that learning gains depend on more than answer correctness [95]. A randomized trial involving teacher-facing AI support and human-AI complementarity also suggests that AI can scale expertise when humans remain in the loop [148]. A separate study of Tutor CoPilot shows that an AI system can improve live tutoring by giving novice tutors real-time guidance, again pointing to augmentation rather than replacement as the more promising pathway [91].

This adjacent evidence strengthens the inference that generative AI may be most effective when it functions as a component within an instructional system, not as an autonomous assistant. Hybrid human-AI tutoring in middle school mathematics improved outcomes relative to software alone [42]. Teacher-mediated AI support in classroom settings appears more plausible than open-ended student self-use [138][142][145]. These studies do not establish direct equivalence between generative AI and classical tutoring, but they identify the operational features most likely to matter: feedback quality, sequencing, alignment, and human oversight.

## Equity, Access, and Contextual Moderators

The evidence base is too thin to support firm subgroup conclusions, but it does point to plausible equity implications. Several studies and reports argue that generative AI could expand access to explanation and practice in low-resource contexts, including Ghana and other under-resourced systems [47][35][140]. At the same time, access to devices, connectivity, AI literacy, and language fluency may determine who benefits from the tools and who is left behind [6][139][149]. Studies on AI literacy and trust among mathematics teachers suggest that adoption is shaped by confidence and beliefs, which vary across contexts and may influence whether students get safe, useful AI support [6][104][138].

There is also an accessibility dimension. Work on students with disabilities and on multimodal or handwriting-aware grading highlights the potential for AI to support diverse learners, but the retrieved studies are mostly design or evaluation papers rather than outcome trials [29][36][41]. Reports on multilingual and curriculum-aligned question generation suggest that language and local curriculum fit are major issues for scaling [139][82][86]. In short, equity-relevant moderators are plausible and important, but the current evidence does not quantify them well enough to support strong claims.

### Synthesis and Implications

Across the evidence base, the central finding is conditionality. Generative AI is best viewed as a potentially useful instructional component whose value depends on whether it supports reasoning, preserves productive struggle, and is embedded in human-mediated instruction [34][112][138][142]. The evidence does not support a blanket claim that generative AI improves K–12 math achievement. Nor does it support a blanket rejection. Instead, it indicates a narrow path to benefit: hint-based, curriculum-aligned, teacher-supervised use that avoids direct answer giving and that is paired with structures preventing overreliance [34][117][149].

For schools and practitioners, the practical implication is that implementation design should come before scale. If generative AI is used for math, the safest and most promising uses are likely to be teacher planning, guided homework support, and structured tutoring prompts rather than unsupervised chatbot access [14][104][142][148]. Systems should prioritize teacher-designed hints, source checking, and prompt constraints, and should avoid configurations that reward answer extraction. Schools should also assume that short AI-literacy lessons alone are unlikely to prevent misuse [117]. The broader design lesson from ITS and hybrid tutoring is that AI works best when it augments human expertise rather than replacing it [42][91][95][148].

The evidence also suggests that decision-makers should be cautious about interpreting immediate practice gains as learning gains. This is the core lesson of the harm study [34]. Students may become faster, more productive, or more satisfied while practicing, yet still perform worse independently later. Therefore, any evaluation of generative AI in math should include delayed posttests, independent assessments, and transfer tasks, not only completion rates or attitudinal outcomes. Until such evidence exists at scale, the safest policy stance is targeted experimentation with strict guardrails rather than systemwide adoption.

### Limitations and Research Gaps

The strongest limitation across the entire evidence base is the scarcity of direct K–12 achievement trials. The clearest causal evidence comes from one high-school field experiment on guardrails [34], one controlled hint-generation study that is not K–12-specific [112], and a small number of newer classroom or web-linked trials whose detailed statistics are not fully retrievable in the current corpus [57][90]. That means Tier 4 is only partially answered, especially for standardized achievement, delayed retention, transfer, and subgroup effects.

Tier 1 is better developed conceptually than empirically. The field has a plausible vocabulary for generative AI tools, outcomes, and subpopulations, but much of the literature is still definitional or aspirational [8][10][17][62]. Tier 2 is reasonably informed by comparator literature on ITS and tutoring, yet those studies are adjacent evidence rather than direct generative-AI comparisons [1][42][95][141]. Tier 3 is the best-developed area, but even here the evidence is more about plausible mechanisms than experimentally established moderators. Teacher mediation, guardrails, prompt design, and curriculum alignment are repeatedly emphasized, but rarely isolated in randomized designs [34][138][142][149].

A major gap concerns duration and durability. Many studies capture immediate practice performance, perceptions, or feasibility, while far fewer measure delayed independent learning [34][54][57][96][108]. Another gap concerns measurement validity: benchmark studies and immediate quizzes are not equivalent to curriculum-aligned achievement tests [20][26][33][77][82][86]. Subgroup evidence is also thin. There is little rigorous evidence on English learners, students with disabilities, rural students, or students with low prior achievement, despite repeated claims that these groups could benefit most [29][35][41][139]. There is also little direct evidence on cost-effectiveness, workload tradeoffs, privacy, or school-level scalability.

Finally, the literature remains skewed toward indirect evidence. Many retrieved papers are about model capability, teacher perception, lesson generation, or technical benchmarking rather than student learning [6][14][20][26][33][104][139]. That means publication bias and selection bias are real concerns. Positive pilot studies may be more likely to appear than null findings, while the most informative classroom RCTs remain rare. The field therefore remains early in terms of causal certainty, especially for the key policy question: whether generative AI meaningfully improves K–12 math achievement relative to strong existing instruction and tutoring.

### Body of Evidence Maturity: LIMITED
Justification: The evidence base contains a small number of direct intervention studies, including at least one high-quality harmful-effects field experiment and one randomized hint-generation comparison, but most retrieved items are reviews, benchmarks, design papers, or observational studies rather than K–12 student-outcome trials. Coverage is thin on delayed achievement, transfer, subgroup effects, and head-to-head comparisons with strong non-AI instructional alternatives, so the field is informative but not yet mature.
