## Executive Summary

The retrieved evidence suggests that AI writing assistants can improve some high school literacy outcomes, but the overall evidence base for grades 9–12 is still limited, heterogeneous, and often indirect. The most defensible conclusion is that AI tools are most likely to help with revision, mechanics, and immediate writing performance when they are embedded in structured instruction and teacher-guided feedback cycles, rather than used as stand-alone supports [165][69][121][121]. For the exact target question, the evidence is stronger for automated writing evaluation and hybrid feedback systems than for generative chatbot use alone, and much of the direct high-school evidence remains observational, case-based, or small-scale [69][73][83][116][181].

The strongest synthesized evidence comes from the multi-level meta-analysis of automated feedback and writing, which included primary through post-secondary students and reported that automated feedback improves student writing performance overall, with heterogeneity across contexts and implementations [165]. A second synthesis, the systematic review of automated writing evaluation in school settings, concluded that AWE can be effective but that outcomes depend heavily on feedback design, instructional integration, and classroom conditions [121]. A broader systematic review of AI in higher education and a systematic mapping review of AI and self-regulated learning reinforce the same implementation message: AI is most useful when it supports feedback, revision, and self-regulation, but evidence is fragmented and not yet tightly mapped to secondary school literacy outcomes [71][166]. Because the retrieved source records do not report the effect sizes or pooled coefficients in the metadata provided, the exact magnitude of these synthesized effects is evidence not retrieved, even where the direction of pooled evidence is favorable [165][121][166].

The strongest direct high-school studies are few. The RCT titled Artificial Intelligence, Teacher Tasks and Individualized Pedagogy studied automated writing evaluation in high school and whether adding human graders on top of AI feedback improves results further, but the retrieved record does not provide the exact sample size or effect estimate, so the precise magnitude is evidence not retrieved [121]. The quasi-experimental study The Role of AI Assisted Writing Feedback in Developing Secondary Students Writing Skills compared AI-assisted feedback with traditional teacher feedback in secondary students; again, the retrieved record confirms a comparison design but does not provide the exact outcome statistics in the supplied metadata, so effect magnitude is evidence not retrieved [83]. The high-school case study on the CGScholar AI Helper Project reports that customized, rubric-aligned AI feedback may support struggling writers in 11th-grade ELA, but it is qualitative and therefore useful mainly for implementation insight, not causal claims [83]. The pilot study Learning to Prompt in the Classroom to Understand AI Limits indicates that a short classroom AI-literacy workshop can help secondary students understand ChatGPT limitations and prompting basics, but the exact outcome statistics are not retrieved [87]. These studies generally corroborate the meta-analytic pattern: AI support appears promising, but the effects depend on scaffolding and teacher mediation [165][121][87][83].

The observational and qualitative high-school evidence complicates the picture in important ways. Studies of secondary students’ prompt engineering pathways with ChatGPT show that productive use requires students to learn how to ask for help effectively, which means the intervention is not simply access to a chatbot but access plus literacy in prompting and revision [73]. The study exploring EFL secondary students’ editing of AI-generated text suggests that students often spend their effort revising or editing machine-generated drafts rather than independently composing, raising questions about whether polished output reflects durable writing growth [79]. The study on AI-generated feedback and ELL writers’ coherence and cohesion focused on a higher-order outcome but reported a cautionary signal in the retrieved title-level evidence: ChatGPT’s help for coherence/cohesion is not yet well established [116]. The study on diverse learners composing with generative AI similarly indicates that benefits are likely uneven across achievement levels, which aligns with concerns about inequitable gains [128][59]. Taken together, the observational evidence supports the possibility of short-term assistance while also underscoring risks of shallow revision, overreliance, and uneven benefit [57][59][79][116].

Answering the tiered sub-questions directly: Tier 1 is only partially answered. The literature clearly distinguishes generative AI chatbots, AWE/AES systems, intelligent tutoring systems, and grammar-support tools, but the studies rarely isolate them cleanly in causal comparisons [165][121][181][70]. Tier 2 is partially answered as well: high school writing without AI is typically supported by teacher feedback, peer review, conferencing, rubric-based instruction, and process writing, while non-AI digital tools and older AWE systems serve as the main comparators [69][74][121][165]. Tier 3 is partially answered; the evidence consistently points to feedback specificity, rubric alignment, teacher oversight, prompt training, and iterative drafting as the key implementation features, but precise dosage, fidelity, and the optimal sequencing of teacher versus AI feedback remain unresolved [73][87][118][167]. Tier 4 is also only partially answered: the evidence suggests AI writing assistants can improve some writing outcomes relative to standard instruction, but superiority over strong human feedback is not established, subgroup evidence for multilingual learners and students with disabilities is thin, and durability, transfer, and content-area writing remain largely unanswered [83][116][128][149][181]. Overall confidence is moderate for the claim that AI assistants can help secondary writers under structured, teacher-mediated conditions, but low for strong claims about durable skill transfer, subgroup equity effects, or generalized superiority over skilled human instruction [165][121][166]. The single most important caveat is that the current record is still dominated by small studies, short interventions, case studies, and indirect evidence, so polished writing outputs should not be mistaken for established literacy growth [69][79][83][116].

## ## Evidence on intervention types and what counts as an AI writing assistant

The retrieved literature treats AI writing assistants as a family of distinct tools rather than a single intervention. The highest-level syntheses define the category broadly: automated writing evaluation and automated feedback systems, automated essay scoring, intelligent tutoring systems, and generative AI/chatbot-based writing support all appear in the evidence base, but they are not interchangeable in mechanism or likely impact [165][121][71][166]. The AI-helper case study in 11th-grade ELA is especially explicit that customized, rubric-aligned feedback is intended to help struggling writers, which places that tool in the “instructional feedback assistant” class rather than a simple editing aid [83]. The high-school prompt-engineering study and the classroom AI-literacy pilot show that generative AI in secondary writing is being used as an interactive partner, where student prompting and model limitation awareness are part of the intervention itself [73][87].

The literature also shows that researchers often operationalize outcomes unevenly. Some studies focus on writing quality, revision quality, or grammar and mechanics, while others emphasize higher-order constructs such as coherence, cohesion, organization, or evidence use [116][79][70]. The multi-level meta-analysis and the school-setting systematic review both emphasize that automated feedback is more likely to influence the writing product when it is tied to multiple drafts and revision opportunities [165][121]. That is consistent with the K-12 CyberScholar and AI-teaching-partner studies, which frame AI as a formative, iterative support in classroom workflows rather than an autonomous writing replacement [181][118]. Content-area writing is still underrepresented, although the evidence base includes disciplinary and expository writing analogues that imply similar concerns about source use, organization, and revision [77][79][70].

The population coverage remains uneven. The most direct secondary evidence includes a high-school RCT of AWE and an 8-week high-school comparison of AI feedback versus teacher feedback, but the majority of retrieved generative-AI studies are either EFL-focused, mixed secondary samples, or non-U.S. contexts [121][83][69][73][128]. Special education evidence is especially thin. The closest relevant evidence is older strategy-instruction work for students with disabilities and at-risk writers, which is useful as a baseline comparator but not as AI-specific causal evidence [149][121]. Multilingual learner evidence is somewhat more visible in the secondary EFL and migrant-background studies, yet the literature still lacks strong subgroup-specific intervention trials [54][116][128].

## ## Baseline instruction and non-AI comparators

High school literacy instruction before and alongside AI assistants is dominated by teacher feedback, peer review, rubric-based revision, conferencing, and process writing [69][74][121][165]. These are the most relevant comparators because they represent established classroom practice rather than “no support.” Older AWE and writing-platform studies provide additional comparison conditions, including automated feedback integrated into writing software and hybrid systems where teachers and technology each provide different forms of support [69][121][165]. The evidence base suggests that these baseline supports are not trivial: if a classroom already provides structured conferencing and timely feedback, AI has to demonstrate added value rather than simple convenience.

The comparative literature also shows that “human-only” feedback is not monolithic. Some studies compare AI to teacher feedback, others to peer feedback, and others to hybrid or rubric-aligned human mediation [83][118][165]. The distinction matters because teacher feedback can be more contextual and diagnostically rich, while peer review can be effective when structured well [69][74]. The peer-feedback and teacher-feedback comparators embedded in the high-school AI studies therefore set a relatively strong baseline. In that setting, AI is best understood as a tool that may scale feedback access, not as a universal substitute for skilled instruction [121][118][165].

The baseline research also suggests that older digital tools can help writing, but often on narrower dimensions. Spellcheck and grammar tools typically improve mechanics and editing; older AWE systems can improve scoreable writing features, but their instructional usefulness depends on how students engage with the feedback [69][121][165]. The school-setting review and the meta-analysis both conclude that the pedagogical use of AWE matters more than its mere availability [121][165].

## ## Implementation patterns in secondary classrooms

Across the secondary studies, AI writing tools are embedded in writing instruction in several recurring ways. Some are used during drafting and revision, where students consult ChatGPT or an AI helper to improve a text after an initial draft [73][79][83]. Others are used as formative feedback systems that offer comments between drafts or during the writing process [69][121][181]. The AI-teaching-partner pilot with secondary teachers is especially important because it shows an explicit model of AI as a third agent in classroom conversation, not as a replacement for teacher feedback [118]. Similarly, the K-12 AI literacy module and the classroom prompt-training pilot show that AI use is increasingly paired with instruction on responsible use and model limitations [182][87].

The implementation findings converge on a few practical conditions. First, feedback specificity matters: rubric-aligned, task-specific feedback is more likely to be useful than generic praise or vague correction [83][118][167]. Second, prompt design matters for generative AI; students need to know how to ask for targeted help and how to judge the relevance of responses [73][87][161]. Third, teacher oversight matters because teachers can calibrate the AI’s use, reduce misuse, and connect feedback to curriculum goals [121][118][83]. Fourth, AI appears most effective in iterative writing cycles rather than one-shot tasks; the evidence repeatedly points to revision as the key pathway [165][69][79][181]. Fifth, there are clear signals that AI use should be constrained by classroom norms and policy, because the literature on academic integrity, ownership, and hallucination is not just theoretical [88][51][53][60][91].

Several studies also suggest that implementation is not simply about “allowing” AI. The process of using AI itself becomes an instructional objective. In the prompt-engineering study, secondary students’ prompts and revisions are treated as observable literacy behaviors [73]. In the AI literacy pilot, students are taught to understand what AI can and cannot do [87]. In the study of secondary students’ editing of AI-generated text, the composition process itself becomes a measure of how AI support changes writing behavior [79]. These patterns strongly imply that effective implementation requires explicit teaching of AI use as a literacy practice, not passive tool access [73][87][182].

## ## Comparative effectiveness in high school writing outcomes

The overall comparative picture is promising but not decisive. The meta-analysis of automated feedback and writing reports positive pooled effects on writing performance, though heterogeneous [165]. The school-setting systematic review reaches a broadly similar conclusion and emphasizes that implementation design shapes whether those benefits appear in classrooms [121]. These syntheses support the claim that AI feedback can improve writing outcomes, but they do not establish that AI is universally superior to strong human instruction.

The direct high-school studies are more mixed. The RCT on Artificial Intelligence, Teacher Tasks and Individualized Pedagogy examined AWE in high school and the potential added value of human graders layered on top of AI feedback, but the retrieved record does not provide exact statistics, so the precise causal magnitude is evidence not retrieved [121]. The quasi-experimental study on AI-assisted writing feedback in secondary students directly compares AI-assisted feedback with traditional teacher feedback, which is highly relevant to the brief, but the retrieved record again does not include the exact outcome values [83]. The qualitative high-school AI-helper case study supports the feasibility of rubric-aligned, customized AI feedback for struggling writers, but because it is qualitative, it cannot answer whether AI caused stronger writing gains [83]. The pilot prompt-training study suggests that short AI-literacy instruction can improve students’ understanding of AI limits and prompts, which is a plausible prerequisite for effective use, but exact effect magnitudes were not retrieved [87].

The broader observational record helps explain why the effect pattern is mixed. One study of AI-generated feedback for ELL writers highlights coherence and cohesion as targeted dimensions, but the available evidence is not strong enough to claim that AI reliably improves higher-order organization [116]. The study of secondary students editing AI-generated text shows that students may accept, modify, or rework machine-produced text in different ways, making final product quality an ambiguous indicator of learning [79]. Another study of diverse learners composing with generative AI points to variability across student profiles and achievement levels, which is consistent with heterogeneity in outcomes [128]. This means that gains in polished text or revision frequency do not automatically imply deeper writing development.

The evidence for specific literacy outcomes is strongest for revision and mechanics. The meta-analysis and school review both support the view that automated feedback improves writing performance partly by increasing revision opportunities [165][121]. The human-versus-ChatGPT feedback study and the secondary AI-feedback comparison show that the central question is not whether AI can generate comments, but whether those comments are used productively [73][83]. By contrast, evidence for organization, content development, transfer, and long-term retention remains weak [116][79][136]. Evidence on writing fluency and reading-to-write performance is also sparse in the retrieved high-school record [128][140].

## ## Subgroup evidence: multilingual learners, students with disabilities, and lower-proficiency writers

The subgroup evidence is one of the weakest parts of the literature. Multilingual learners appear in the secondary EFL, migrant-background, and non-native speaker studies, where AI may help with sentence-level support, vocabulary, and feedback access [54][105][116][128]. However, these studies do not yet provide a coherent causal estimate for high-school multilingual learners, and they sometimes focus on foreign-language contexts that differ from U.S. secondary ELA or content-area writing [54][69][73]. The evidence therefore suggests promise but does not yet show that AI assistants reliably narrow writing gaps for multilingual learners.

Students with disabilities are even less directly represented in the AI-specific secondary evidence. The relevant baseline literature on strategy instruction for students with disabilities and at-risk writers is strong as a comparator, but it is not AI-assisted writing evidence [149][121]. As a result, the current record cannot tell decision-makers whether AI writing assistants reduce transcription burden, improve accessibility, or create new barriers for students with disabilities in grades 9–12 [149][131]. The closest adjacent evidence comes from accessibility and assistive-technology discussions, which highlight potential benefits but do not establish outcomes in secondary writing classrooms [131][149].

For lower-proficiency writers, the evidence is suggestive that more scaffolded support may be especially useful, but also that these students may be more vulnerable to shallow revision and overreliance. The AI-helper case study explicitly frames the tool as support for struggling writers [83]. The diverse-learners and achievement-level studies imply that benefit may depend on prior proficiency [128][57]. The prompt-training and AI-literacy studies reinforce that students with weaker self-regulation or prompting skill may need more explicit teaching before AI can help rather than hinder [73][87][182]. In short, the subgroup literature supports targeted promise but not subgroup-specific efficacy claims.

## ## Risks, tradeoffs, and unintended consequences

The literature is unusually clear that AI writing assistants create tradeoffs. Overreliance is the dominant concern: if students use AI to generate or repair text without learning the underlying processes, they may produce better-looking writing without improving as writers [60][79][57]. This concern appears across conceptual papers, policy work, and empirical studies of prompt use and editing behaviors [60][88][91]. Academic integrity is another recurring issue, including plagiarism detection, authorship ambiguity, and the possibility that teachers cannot easily distinguish AI-assisted from human writing [51][53][56][89]. Studies of AI-generated essays and detection systems reinforce that assessment validity is now a serious concern in secondary and postsecondary writing alike [56][89][203][145].

Accuracy is also a practical problem. Several studies in the record focus on feedback quality, error correction accuracy, or the limitations of AI-generated suggestions [116][105][62][203]. That matters for secondary students because inaccurate feedback may mislead writers, especially novice writers and multilingual learners [105][116]. Equity concerns run through the literature as well. The digital-divide study on admissions essays suggests that benefits from AI writing support may not be distributed evenly across socioeconomic groups, and the same logic likely applies to high school classrooms where device access, home support, and AI literacy vary [59][88][182]. For school systems, this means implementation should be treated as a governance issue, not just a pedagogical one [88][147].

## Synthesis and Implications

Across the whole evidence base, the most credible conclusion is that AI writing assistants are best viewed as instructionally mediated feedback and scaffolding tools, not as substitutes for teacher-led writing instruction [165][121][118]. The pattern is consistent across the meta-analysis, school review, high-school case studies, prompt-training work, and classroom pilots: AI is most useful when it expands feedback access, supports revision, and is constrained by rubric-aligned teacher guidance [165][121][83][87][118]. The evidence is least convincing for claims that AI alone produces durable writing development, transfer across tasks, or equitable gains for all student groups [116][79][128][149][136].

For schools and practitioners, this implies a cautious implementation model. AI writing assistants should be introduced as part of a multi-draft writing cycle, with explicit instruction on how to prompt, evaluate, and revise in response to AI feedback [73][87][182]. Teachers should retain control over writing goals, rubric criteria, and final evaluation, while using AI to increase feedback frequency and support revision workflow [121][118][167]. The strongest practical use cases are likely to be sentence-level editing, revision prompts, and formative feedback in short-cycle assignments; the weakest are autonomous essay generation and unsupervised use on high-stakes tasks [79][51][53][60]. Schools should also plan for integrity and governance from the start, because policies, detection limits, and access inequities materially affect whether AI can be used responsibly [88][89][56][59].

## Limitations and Research Gaps

The most important limitation is that the direct grades 9–12 intervention literature is thin. The evidence base contains one clear high-school RCT record and at least one direct high-school comparison of AI-assisted feedback versus teacher feedback, but the retrieved metadata do not provide the exact sample sizes or effect statistics needed for a fully quantitative conclusion [121][83]. Many other studies are qualitative, observational, or mixed-secondary/EFL rather than high-school-only [73][79][116][128][181]. That means Tier 4, which asks whether AI writing assistants outperform standard instruction in high school, remains only partially answered.

The biggest subgroup gaps are multilingual learners and students with disabilities. Multilingual learner studies are present, but often in EFL or migrant-background contexts that do not cleanly generalize to secondary ELA or content-area writing [54][105][116]. Students with disabilities are represented more strongly in baseline strategy-instruction research than in AI-specific writing trials, leaving a major evidence gap for special education [149][131]. Content-area writing is also underdeveloped. There are some adjacent expository and disciplinary writing studies, but few direct secondary science, social studies, or history writing interventions with AI support [77][140][79].

Durability and transfer remain largely unresolved. Most retrieved evidence appears short-term and product-focused, with limited follow-up after AI support is removed [136][59][79]. That is a major concern because a tool can improve output quality without improving the underlying writing skill. In addition, implementation details are often underreported: dosage, teacher training intensity, exact prompt structures, fidelity of use, and the sequencing of teacher versus AI feedback are rarely specified well enough to support strong moderation claims [118][121][167]. Finally, the evidence on harms is more conceptual than causal. Dependency, plagiarism, hallucinated feedback, and equity gaps are widely discussed, but high-quality direct evidence on the frequency and educational impact of these harms in secondary classrooms is still lacking [51][53][88][91][203].

### Body of Evidence Maturity: LIMITED
Justification: The evidence base contains some relevant secondary-school studies and one strong meta-analytic synthesis, but the directly causal high-school literature is still small, heterogeneous, and often missing the exact statistics needed for firm inference. Subgroup evidence for multilingual learners and students with disabilities is especially thin, and content-area writing, durability, and transfer remain underdeveloped.
