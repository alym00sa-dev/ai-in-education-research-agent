## Executive Summary

The current evidence base is not yet sufficient to make a definitive causal claim that AI-personalized feedback improves K-12 students’ critical thinking relative to standard teacher feedback, generic automated feedback, or no feedback. The most defensible conclusion is that the literature is still early and uneven: there are promising K-12-adjacent signals that personalized AI feedback can support reasoning, revision, and metacognitive prompting, but direct evidence on validated critical-thinking outcomes in school-based K-12 settings remains sparse, heterogeneous, and often indirect. Across the retrieved corpus, the strongest results come from intelligent tutoring systems, automated writing evaluation, and AI-supported scaffolding studies, rather than from trials that isolate critical thinking as the primary endpoint [21][80][89][162][174].

The strongest experimental evidence is concentrated in K-12 intelligent tutoring systems and related feedback systems. A K-12 meta-analysis of intelligent tutoring systems found that AI-based tutoring improved learning outcomes overall, with moderators including worked-out examples, intervention duration, outcome type, condition, and immediacy; however, the summary provided does not report a pooled effect size, so the exact magnitude is not retrievable here [21]. An RCT in early elementary math found that an adaptive game-based app with personalized content, embedded formative assessments, and teacher dashboards improved early elementary students’ math learning, but the effect size was not reported in the supplied summary [7]. A high-school RCT of AI tutoring in UK classrooms found that a pedagogically fine-tuned generative AI model could safely and effectively support secondary mathematics tutoring, but again the exact outcome statistic was not reported in the supplied material [133]. A K-12 elementary/middle AWE system, eRevise+RF, was designed to assess revisions and provide formative feedback on argumentative writing; it is highly relevant to evidence-based reasoning, but the supplied summary does not report causal effect estimates [162]. Similarly, a K-12 elementary (5th–6th grade) AWE tool, eRevise, provided formative feedback on text evidence usage in writing, which is relevant to argumentation but not a direct critical-thinking measure [89].

The quasi-experimental and observational evidence complicates but broadly aligns with the experimental picture: AI-personalized feedback appears most useful when it is specific, scaffolded, and embedded in a task that already demands reasoning, argumentation, or reflection. A high-school quasi-experimental study in Indonesia reported that AI-supported geography instruction improved higher-order thinking skills and achievement, though the exact statistic was not supplied [96]. Another high-school quasi-experimental study in high-school geometry found that interactive, intelligent tutoring for auxiliary constructions improved students’ capacity to discover proof-relevant constructions, a strong proxy for analytical reasoning [146]. In middle and high school phwysics, an RCT found that students’ use of AI feedback mattered for physics achievement and autonomy, indicating that feedback effects depend on how learners interact with the system rather than on feedback presence alone [76]. Observational work in high-school physics, middle-school science inquiry, and K-12 writing suggests that AI feedback can support or undermine reflection depending on student agency, task structure, and whether the system prompts metacognitive monitoring rather than simply providing answers [33][74][132][174].

Taken together, the evidence partially answers the first tier on foundational framing: critical thinking is most often operationalized through reasoning, analysis, evaluation, inference, argumentation, reflective judgment, and metacognition, but the measurement is inconsistent and often replaced by proxies such as writing quality, task performance, or learning gains [124][157][174]. The second tier, baseline and existing approaches, is only partially answered: the literature indicates that teacher feedback, rubrics, and standard formative scaffolds remain important comparators, but the supplied evidence does not provide enough direct K-12 head-to-head trials to determine how AI-personalized feedback compares with high-quality human feedback or rubric-based conferencing [112][144][170]. The third tier on mechanisms and implementation is moderately answered: personalization, immediacy, teacher mediation, rubric alignment, and metacognitive prompting repeatedly appear as likely mechanisms, but they are not consistently isolated experimentally [64][73][167][179]. The fourth tier remains unresolved for the focal claim: there is not yet direct, high-confidence evidence that AI-personalized feedback improves validated K-12 critical-thinking outcomes more than standard instruction, though it plausibly improves adjacent outcomes such as revision quality, reasoning support, and task performance [21][89][96][162][174].

Overall confidence is low-to-moderate for the claim that AI-personalized feedback can support critical-thinking-relevant processes in K-12 classrooms, and low for the stronger claim that it reliably improves critical thinking itself. The single most important caveat is that most evidence still measures proxies, not direct critical-thinking outcomes, and the studies that do involve K-12 often differ substantially in grade band, subject area, and feedback type, making causal generalization precarious [21][80][112][124][159].

## Conceptualizing Critical Thinking and AI-Personalized Feedback in K-12

Across the retrieved evidence, critical thinking is not treated as a single, stable construct. Instead, it is usually decomposed into overlapping subskills such as reasoning, analysis, evaluation, inference, argumentation, explanation, and reflective judgment, often with metacognition or self-regulation as adjacent components [124][157]. In science and mathematics settings, those subskills are frequently embedded in tasks requiring evidence use, proof construction, error analysis, or scientific explanation, which makes it difficult to separate “critical thinking” from subject-matter performance [96][146][174]. That definitional variability is a major reason why studies often report indirect outcomes rather than validated critical-thinking instruments [21][89][162].

AI-personalized feedback is likewise a heterogeneous intervention class. In the K-12 evidence base, it appears as intelligent tutoring hints and explanations, adaptive formative feedback, automated writing evaluation, LLM-generated comments, rubric-aligned revision guidance, and teacher-facing AI analytics that indirectly support instructional feedback [7][21][88][90][133][162][164]. Some systems primarily tailor content difficulty or sequencing, while others tailor commentary on student writing or problem solving; these are not equivalent, and the literature does not always distinguish them cleanly [21][80][90][148]. This conceptual breadth complicates claims about “AI feedback” as a unified intervention.

The studied contexts are concentrated in middle school and high school, with a smaller but meaningful cluster in elementary literacy, mathematics, and science [7][89][162][137][146][61]. School-based classroom instruction dominates the K-12-relevant evidence, but there is also work in digital platforms, blended learning, and homework-support contexts [74][79][179]. The evidence is sparse for elementary critical thinking as such, even though several elementary studies address related skills such as evidence use, algorithmic thinking, or mathematical reasoning [89][137][154][139].

## Baseline Instruction, Teacher Feedback, and Conventional Formative Supports

The strongest counterfactuals in the literature are not “no instruction” but high-quality formative practices: teacher feedback, rubric-guided revision, peer discussion, and explicit reasoning prompts. A systematic review of teacher feedback for students aged 10–16 indicates that feedback effects depend on the quality, timing, and specificity of comments, and that outcomes span academic performance, self-regulation, and self-efficacy rather than critical thinking alone [112]. A systematic review of rubrics likewise suggests that rubrics can support performance, self-regulated learning, and self-efficacy, making them an important benchmark for any AI feedback system that claims to improve reasoning or revision [5].

Teacher perceptions and classroom practice studies reinforce that human feedback remains central because teachers can adapt to context, emotion, and disciplinary nuance [144][170]. At the same time, several studies suggest that teachers struggle to scale individualized feedback, which is one reason AI systems are being adopted [144][170]. In this sense, AI-personalized feedback should be understood as a complement to conventional formative assessment, not a full replacement for teacher conferencing or explicit critical-thinking instruction.

The evidence on conventional approaches also implies that the correct baseline is often not generic teacher feedback but high-quality, aligned feedback with opportunities for revision. That matters because if AI is compared against weak or delayed feedback, apparent effects may reflect feedback availability rather than personalization per se [112][5][170]. The literature on scaffolding in teacher–student interaction similarly implies that the active ingredients may be timing, task alignment, and contingent support rather than “AI” as such [57].

## Experimental Evidence from K-12 and Closely Aligned Studies

The most directly relevant synthesis is the K-12 meta-analysis of intelligent tutoring systems, which reports positive overall learning effects and identifies moderators such as worked-out examples, duration, outcome type, intervention condition, and immediacy [21]. Because the supplied summary does not provide the pooled effect size, confidence in magnitude is limited, but the direction of evidence supports the view that AI-based tutoring can improve learning when it is well designed and instructionally embedded [21][80]. A more recent systematic review of AI-driven ITS in K-12 similarly maps the field and emphasizes that design and implementation features vary widely across studies [80].

An RCT of an adaptive game-based math app for early elementary students found improved learning when personalized content and formative assessments were embedded in regular classroom instruction [7]. The supplied summary does not report the exact effect statistic, but the study is important because it places personalization in a real classroom context and includes teacher dashboards, showing that AI feedback often works as part of a human-mediated system rather than as a standalone agent [7]. Another elementary RCT found that combining learning-progress optimization with learner choice in an ITS improved learning and motivation, again pointing to personalization as one part of a broader design rather than the only causal ingredient [137].

At the secondary level, an exploratory RCT in UK classrooms found that a pedagogically fine-tuned generative AI model could safely and effectively support mathematics tutoring [133]. The study is important for the present review because it suggests that generative AI can be incorporated into school tutoring without obvious safety failures, but the supplied summary does not provide a critical-thinking-specific outcome. A separate RCT on generative AI feedback use in physics found that outcomes depended on how students used the feedback, with implications for achievement and autonomy [76]. That finding matters because it suggests that merely delivering personalized feedback is insufficient; the learner’s pattern of engagement mediates impact.

An RCT of AI-generated feedback types found that directive, metacognitive, and blended feedback influence engagement, confidence, and revised work quality differently [167]. Although this study is not K-12, it directly supports a key mechanism hypothesis for school settings: metacognitive prompts may be more conducive to critical-thinking-related outcomes than purely directive corrections. Similarly, a quasi-experimental study of AI-driven metacognitive interventions in an ITS found benefits for preparation for future learning [178], reinforcing the idea that self-monitoring and reflection are plausible pathways.

## Evidence Closest to Critical Thinking: Writing, Argumentation, and Reflection

The clearest K-12-relevant proxies for critical thinking come from writing and argumentation systems. eRevise provides formative feedback on text evidence usage in fifth- and sixth-grade writing, a direct proxy for argumentation and evidence-based reasoning [89]. eRevise+RF extends that idea by supporting essay revisions and formative feedback in elementary and middle school argumentative writing [162]. These systems are highly relevant because evidence use and revision quality are core components of school-based critical thinking, even though the supplied summaries do not report direct critical-thinking test scores.

In secondary science and ELA contexts, AI and teacher feedback have been explored as sequences and combinations rather than isolated treatments. An AI- and teacher-generated feedback sequencing study on EFL writing revision indicates that the order and combination of feedback sources matter for revision practices [24]. A study on generative AI and argumentative essay writing suggests that ChatGPT-like tools can be used to support critical thinking in second-language learners, but the retrieved summary is not a K-12 classroom trial and does not establish causal effects for school students [26]. In high-school physics, a study of a LLM-based tutoring system emphasized deep learning, precise understanding, error analysis, and reflective thinking, making it one of the closer matches to the focal construct even though the supplied evidence does not report final effect estimates [132].

Science-classroom studies also indicate that AI may support critical thinking when it encourages questioning and justification rather than answer retrieval. A high-school paper on ChatGPT in physics education proposed activities where students generate AI outputs and then critically analyze and compare them [174]. A middle-school study on inquiry with ChatGPT found that students often failed to regulate inquiry effectively, highlighting a possible downside: AI can produce the appearance of understanding without supporting genuine question-asking or self-monitoring [74]. This tension is crucial for the research brief because AI feedback can plausibly either deepen or hollow out critical thinking, depending on task design.

## Comparative Evidence and Moderators

Direct comparisons between AI-personalized feedback and teacher feedback in K-12 are limited, but the available evidence suggests that the comparison is not one-dimensional. Teacher feedback studies and teacher-perception studies indicate that humans remain superior for contextual judgment, emotional attunement, and discipline-specific nuance [112][144]. AI systems appear better suited to rapid, scalable, and often more specific feedback on surface-level or well-structured aspects of work [21][89][162]. That division of labor suggests a hybrid model, but the current evidence does not yet establish it as a causal best practice in K-12.

Moderators are better established than direct comparative superiority claims. In the ITS meta-analysis, outcome type, immediacy, intervention duration, and worked-out examples mattered [21]. In the generative AI use RCT in physics, how students interacted with feedback shaped outcomes [76]. In the middle-school generative-AI personalized tasks study, the teacher-in-the-loop question was central, implying that instructional oversight may be critical for personalization quality and learner support [73]. In a middle-school science inquiry study, misuse of ChatGPT undermined regulation and inquiry quality, suggesting that open-ended tasks may be particularly vulnerable to superficial compliance or cognitive offloading [74].

Subject area also matters. Math and science dominate the evidence, especially tutoring, problem solving, and proof-like tasks [7][21][76][132][146]. ELA and writing offer the clearest evidence for evidence use and revision [89][162][24][164]. Social studies, civics, and broader interdisciplinary critical thinking are much less represented, despite being domains where argumentation and evaluation are central [157][159]. The weakest evidence is in elementary critical thinking as a domain-general construct and in multilingual or special education populations.

## Implementation Features: What Seems to Matter

The implementation literature repeatedly points to feedback specificity, timing, and teacher mediation as the most important design choices. In K-12 ITS, immediacy and worked examples were important moderators [21]. In generative AI feedback work, directive versus metacognitive feedback appears to shape engagement and confidence differently [167]. In teacher-facing systems, AI can help surface student discourse patterns or generate personalized feedback for teachers, as in TalkMoves and Classroom observation tools, but these systems support instruction indirectly rather than acting as student-facing critical-thinking interventions [48][62]. For school adoption, the key issue is not simply whether AI can generate feedback, but whether the feedback is aligned to curriculum goals, checked for accuracy, and embedded in an instructional routine [144][170][145].

Several studies point to the importance of student agency. The AI-feedback RCT in physics found that how students used feedback mattered for achievement and autonomy [76]. The study on middle-school ChatGPT use in science found that students often did not regulate inquiry well, which suggests that AI feedback without scaffolds may encourage answer-seeking over reasoning [74]. Likewise, studies of generative AI as a teachable agent or peer in middle school math emphasize student perceptions of role, control, and collaboration, all of which likely affect whether feedback supports genuine thinking [91][72]. These findings strengthen the case for human-in-the-loop designs and explicit norms for AI use.

## Risks, Unintended Effects, and Safety

The literature also includes important warnings. Conceptual and empirical sources point to possible overreliance, cognitive offloading, hallucinated or low-quality suggestions, and inflated confidence without deeper reasoning [29][97][152]. A high-school study on hallucination detection in business assessment and a middle-school study on regulation with ChatGPT both underscore that students may accept AI outputs uncritically [152][74]. That is a direct threat to critical thinking, because the intervention may improve apparent task completion while weakening independent evaluation.

Equity and accessibility remain underexplored. The available systematic reviews and K-12 mapping studies show very limited disaggregation by English learner status, disability status, or socioeconomic background [112][155][159]. The most relevant elementary and secondary studies rarely report subgroup effects, and the review corpus does not provide enough information to infer whether AI feedback narrows or widens gaps [7][21][137]. Ethical concerns about bias, privacy, transparency, and teacher oversight are widely acknowledged in reviews and position papers, but they are not yet well quantified in K-12 efficacy studies [145][170][98].

## Synthesis and Implications

The body of evidence suggests that AI-personalized feedback is best understood as a potentially useful scaffold for reasoning-intensive tasks, not as a proven intervention for domain-general critical thinking in K-12. Where it appears most promising is in argumentative writing, science explanation, mathematical problem solving, and tutoring environments where immediate, specific, and revision-oriented feedback can be coupled with teacher oversight [21][89][96][132][162][174]. In these settings, AI may help students revise more effectively, notice errors, and sustain engagement with the task.

Practically, schools should treat AI feedback as an augmentation layer. The evidence favors designs that are rubric-aligned, transparent, and embedded in instruction, with opportunities for students to accept, reject, and reflect on feedback rather than merely comply with it [73][76][167][179]. Teacher mediation remains important because it can correct errors, contextualize prompts, and ensure that feedback supports deeper reasoning rather than superficial edits [112][144][170]. If used well, AI may free teacher time for discussion, conferencing, and higher-order coaching; if used poorly, it may replace productive struggle with shallow completion [29][74][152].

For decision-makers, the current evidence supports cautious piloting, not broad claims of effectiveness. The most reasonable near-term use case is in tasks already tied to critical thinking outcomes, such as evidence-based writing, scientific argumentation, and structured problem solving, where feedback can be aligned to clear criteria [89][96][146][162]. The strongest research need is for direct K-12 trials that measure critical-thinking subskills explicitly, not just general performance or revision quality [124][157].

## Limitations and Research Gaps

The strongest limitation is that direct K-12 evidence on validated critical-thinking outcomes is sparse. Most studies measure proxies such as revision quality, learning gains, autonomy, engagement, or task performance rather than reasoning, inference, evaluation, or reflective judgment themselves [7][21][76][89][162][174]. That weakness is most severe for the fourth tier on comparative effectiveness, where direct head-to-head trials against teacher feedback, rubric-based feedback, delayed feedback, and no-feedback conditions are still limited.

Methodologically, the evidence base is dominated by heterogeneous designs and mixed populations. Many relevant sources are systematic reviews, conceptual papers, or observational studies rather than classroom RCTs or strong quasi-experiments [80][112][124][145][159]. Even among experimental studies, outcomes and reporting vary enough that effect-size synthesis is difficult from the supplied summaries [7][21][133][137][146][167]. The field also lacks consistent operationalization of personalization, making it hard to separate AI tailoring from co-interventions such as worked examples, immediate feedback, teacher dashboards, or revised tasks [21][73][80][90].

The weakest coverage is in equity and subgroup analysis. There is little reliable evidence on multilingual learners, students with disabilities, low prior achievers, or elementary students specifically [112][155][159]. Long-term outcomes are also underreported: durability, transfer, and sustained metacognitive growth remain largely unknown [21][74][76][178]. Safety, bias, hallucination, and dependence risks are increasingly recognized, but they are not yet integrated into robust school-based efficacy designs [29][97][152][145].

### Body of Evidence Maturity: EMERGING
Justification: The evidence base includes several relevant K-12 meta-analyses, RCTs, and quasi-experiments, but direct measurement of critical thinking is still limited and often replaced by proxies such as writing revision, achievement, autonomy, or engagement. Coverage is strongest in mathematics, science, and writing, with major gaps in elementary critical thinking, subgroup analysis, and long-term transfer; accordingly, the field is past the purely exploratory stage but not yet mature enough for high-confidence causal conclusions.
