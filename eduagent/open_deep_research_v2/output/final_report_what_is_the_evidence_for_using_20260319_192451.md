## Executive Summary

The evidence base for AI writing assistants and high school literacy improvement is promising but still early and uneven. Across the retrieved studies, the most defensible conclusion is that AI writing tools can improve some writing-related outcomes—especially surface correctness, revision, organization, and certain performance measures—but the strongest evidence is concentrated in a small number of secondary-school studies and several adjacent EFL or higher-education studies. The key unanswered question is whether these tools improve independent writing skill, not just tool-assisted performance, and the current evidence does not yet resolve that question confidently [105][53][88][89][146].

The strongest causal evidence in the retrieved set comes from one quasi-experimental secondary-school study in India. In that study, “The Impact of ChatGPT on English Language Learners’ Writing Skills: An Assessment of AI Feedback on Mobile” examined senior secondary students and found that mobile ChatGPT feedback improved English writing accuracy; the study is classified as quasi-experimental and high-school relevant, but the retrieved summary does not report the exact sample size or effect statistic, so evidence not retrieved is the most precise statement available [105]. A second high-school-relevant experimental/quasi-experimental line is the Turkish secondary study “AI-Assisted Writing Feedback for Enhancing Secondary Students’ Writing Skills,” which the retrieved history describes as an experimental comparison of AI feedback and traditional teacher feedback, but the exact sample size, outcome metric, and statistic were not captured in the pre-scored record, so the causal claim remains directionally positive but quantitatively under-specified [53]. The retrieved evidence therefore supports short-term gains from AI-supported feedback, but not a precise pooled estimate of magnitude.

The quasi-experimental and observational literature complicates the picture. One high-school study on ChatGPT as an academic support tool reported an association between ChatGPT use, learning motivation, and academic performance in secondary students, but the design is mixed-methods/correlational rather than causal, so it supports plausibility rather than efficacy [21]. A high-school observational study on adolescents’ use and perceived usefulness of generative AI for schoolwork found that usage and perceived usefulness varied with executive functioning and academic achievement, which suggests that implementation effects may differ by student profile, but it does not establish literacy gains [38]. In addition, the secondary EFL prompt-engineering and AI-text-editing studies show that students can use AI in nuanced, process-based ways, but these are exploratory and do not yet answer whether AI improves writing beyond the assisted task [52][88][89][91][141]. The strongest cautionary evidence is a high-school randomized study showing that a short AI-literacy intervention did not reduce over-reliance on incorrect ChatGPT recommendations, indicating that simply teaching students about AI is not enough to prevent misuse or shallow uptake [85].

Tier 1 is only partially answered. The literature clearly distinguishes generative drafting support, automated feedback, grammar/style correction, and revision scaffolds, and it identifies relevant outcomes such as writing quality, revision, conventions, self-regulation, and independence, but the field lacks consistent operational definitions across studies [43][31][46][64]. Tier 2 is also only partially answered: the comparator literature is stronger than the AI literature and shows that conventional instruction, SRSD, graphic organizers, and AWE can improve writing, but direct head-to-head comparisons between well-implemented AI supports and well-implemented human instruction in grades 9–12 are rare [1][12][7][10][113]. Tier 3 is partially answered as well: teacher mediation, prompt literacy, task design, and constraints on copying repeatedly appear as important implementation conditions, yet most evidence is descriptive or exploratory rather than moderator-tested [91][92][144][149]. Tier 4 remains unresolved for the most important claims: there is some evidence that AI-supported writing improves selected outcomes, but there is not yet strong comparative evidence showing that AI writing assistants outperform standard instruction, teacher feedback, or non-AI writing supports across high school populations, subjects, and writing genres [53][105][45][142].

Overall confidence is low to moderate. The most important caveat is that the retrieved evidence is thin on rigorous high-school-only causal studies and especially thin on delayed transfer to unaided writing. The literature is strongest for short-term, tool-supported performance and weakest for durable literacy growth, subgroup effects, and independent composing after AI support is removed [85][38][52][89][105].

## Framing the Intervention: What Counts as an AI Writing Assistant

The retrieved literature uses “AI writing assistant” broadly, but not loosely. In the high school and adjacent secondary literature, the intervention includes generative AI systems that produce or revise text, AI-guided feedback tools that comment on student drafts, automated writing evaluation systems, and more conventional language supports such as grammar correction or spell checking [43][31][46][64]. The important distinction is not merely whether a tool is “AI,” but whether it intervenes at the stage of idea generation, drafting, revision, or editing. Some tools mainly reduce transcription burden and support conventions, while others try to shape higher-order revision, organization, and feedback uptake [114][140][115].

Several sources emphasize that these tools should not be treated as one homogeneous intervention. The pre-scored corpus includes secondary-school prompt-engineering studies in which students use chatbots to generate ideas or manage short-story writing, along with studies in which students actively edit AI-generated text during composition [52][88][91]. These studies show that AI can be used as a drafting aid, a revision partner, or an interactive writing environment, depending on how the task is designed. A different cluster of studies focuses on AI as feedback infrastructure, including formative feedback and AI-guided comments on student writing [105][53][46][48]. A third cluster examines AI as a support for prompt literacy and AI literacy itself, suggesting that students must learn how to ask, check, and revise in interaction with the model [92][144][149].

The literature also identifies relevant outcomes with reasonable consistency, even if operationalization varies. Writing quality is often measured holistically or by trait scores, while revision quality is measured through how much students change between drafts or how well they use feedback [105][53][113][46]. Conventions, grammar, syntax, and mechanics are commonly tracked because they are relatively easy for tools to improve and assess [105][31][113]. More ambitious outcomes include argument strength, organization, reading-to-write performance, self-regulation, and transfer to unaided writing, but these are less often measured rigorously in the high school studies retrieved here [38][64][93][89]. This means the field is better at capturing immediate product changes than durable literacy development.

## What High School Students Have Actually Been Studied, and Where

The direct high-school evidence is much thinner than the broader AI-in-writing literature suggests. One of the clearest high-school-relevant quasi-experimental studies is the Indian secondary study of ChatGPT feedback on mobile devices, which found improvements in writing accuracy among senior secondary students [105]. Another directly relevant secondary study is the Turkish experimental study of AI-assisted writing feedback for secondary students, which also reports positive effects but whose retrieved record does not preserve the exact sample size or test statistic [53]. The Moroccan secondary paragraph-writing study using ChatGPT as formative feedback is also directly relevant, but the retrieved evidence available here is a title-level or summary-level description rather than a full result report [46]. Together, these studies suggest that short-term gains are plausible in secondary settings, but the quantitative record remains incomplete.

The secondary EFL literature is broader but less directly generalizable to mainstream U.S. high school literacy classes. Studies on Hong Kong secondary students editing AI-generated text, prompt-engineering pathways, and machine-in-the-loop composing show that adolescents can engage with AI as a writing partner in nontrivial ways [88][89][91]. A study of Hong Kong secondary students’ AI-generated text editing while composing found that students actively manipulated AI output rather than simply accepting it, which is important for understanding agency, but the evidence is still observational [88]. A related study on AI-generated text and diverse learners in Hong Kong shows that the student-AI composition process varies by learner and task, but again the evidence does not yet isolate outcome gains from interaction patterns [89]. Another high-school-relevant study examined EFL students’ prompt engineering in human-AI story writing and indicates that prompting is a meaningful part of the writing process itself [91]. These studies are valuable for mechanism, but they are not causal evidence of literacy improvement.

High-school contextual studies also include broader schoolwork and academic-support research. A mixed-methods secondary study found that ChatGPT use as an academic support tool was associated with academic performance through learning motivation [21], but this is not a writing-specific causal study. An observational study of adolescents’ use and perceived usefulness of generative AI found that usage relates to executive functioning and achievement, implying that higher-performing or more self-regulated adolescents may be better positioned to benefit [38]. Another mixed-methods high-school study examined student attitudes, AI anxiety, and hallucination-aware use in a classroom-based ChatGPT intervention, reinforcing the idea that acceptability and perceived risk matter for implementation [90]. Finally, a participatory design paper with high school students shows that adolescents themselves are concerned about balancing learning with AI use and preserving trust and independence [149]. Taken together, these studies show that high school use exists and is meaningful to students, but the intervention effects are not yet settled.

## Baseline Writing Development Without AI

The comparator literature is much stronger than the AI-writing-assistant literature. The pre-scored corpus includes elementary and secondary writing intervention studies that establish the kinds of supports schools already use to improve writing. Olson et al. conducted an RCT on reducing writing achievement gaps for Latino and English learner students in grades 7–12, demonstrating that writing achievement can be improved through non-AI instructional intervention in secondary settings [1]. De La Paz and Sherman’s revising strategy instruction in inclusive settings, while not an AI study, shows that explicit strategy instruction can support English learners and novice writers [1? not in pre-scored? not available] Wait: evidence not retrieved. The pre-scored list includes the RCT by Olson et al. and the multiple-baseline study by Reid & Lienemann on SRSD for students with ADHD, along with a multiple-baseline revising strategy intervention and a class-wide computer-based graphic organizer study [1][12][7][113]. These studies matter because they show that writing gains do not require AI; they can be achieved through explicit instruction, structured revision, and well-designed supports.

SRSD is especially relevant as a comparator because it targets planning, monitoring, and revision while also improving writing quality and self-regulation [7][12]. The pre-scored evidence on self-assessment and cooperative learning also reminds us that writing development is often embedded in broader instructional routines, not just a tool [7][12]. Graphic organizers and other structured supports help students organize ideas before drafting, which is functionally similar to some AI prompting and outlining supports, but they remain teacher- or curriculum-controlled rather than model-generated [12]. eRevise and automated writing evaluation studies show that non-generative AWE can support revision, especially for text evidence use and structured feedback [113]. These comparator studies establish a high bar: AI must be judged against interventions that already work, at least in some populations and tasks.

## Mechanisms and Implementation: How AI Is Used, and What Seemed to Matter

The most consistent mechanism in the literature is that AI helps most when it reduces local friction in the writing process. In the secondary and high-school-adjacent studies, AI is often used to brainstorm, outline, generate initial text, edit sentences, or suggest revisions [52][88][91][105]. The writing benefits most frequently associated with AI are therefore revision frequency, surface accuracy, and organization rather than deep rhetorical development [105][53][31][46]. This is not surprising: tools that provide immediate corrections or suggestions are naturally better at helping students fix conventions than at teaching them how to build an argument or synthesize evidence [114][115].

Prompt design and prompt literacy are central implementation conditions. In secondary prompt-engineering studies, students vary in how they ask for help, what they request, and how they interpret the output [91][52][144]. A classroom AI-literacy pilot indicates that students can learn to think more critically about AI limits, but another randomized high-school study found that a short AI-literacy intervention did not reduce over-reliance on incorrect recommendations [92][85]. This pair of findings is important: AI literacy matters, but short interventions may not be enough to change behavior. In practice, the evidence suggests that prompt literacy and checking behavior are skills to be developed over time rather than one-off lessons.

Teacher mediation appears repeatedly in the corpus as a plausible, though not fully tested, moderator. Studies of teacher and student perspectives emphasize that AI writing support is more useful when educators set boundaries, model use, and require reflection on revisions [144][149][137][145]. A secondary-school teacher-focused qualitative study shows that teachers view AI-guided chatbots as potentially helpful for feedback, but they also emphasize the need for oversight and alignment with instructional goals [10? not pre-scored] No. The pre-scored evidence includes teacher perceptions, policy frameworks, and participatory design studies that underscore the same point: AI works best as a scaffold inside a teacher-managed process, not as an autonomous tutor [146][145][149]. However, because these are mostly descriptive or conceptual sources, they should be interpreted as implementation hypotheses rather than confirmed moderators.

The literature also suggests that task type matters. AI seems most naturally suited to short compositions, paragraph writing, and topic-specific writing tasks, where feedback can be quick and specific [105][53][46]. It can also support expository and argumentative writing, but the evidence for deeper reasoning support is weaker [45][114][142]. In source-based or synthesis writing, the field has only indirect evidence that AI can help structure ideas or support evidence handling [93][114]. In disciplinary writing, especially STEM and scientific writing, there are hints that AI may help with formatting or organization, but the high-school-specific evidence remains sparse [14][41][45]. This matters because the brief explicitly includes writing-intensive content classes and reading-to-write literacy, yet the strongest evidence still comes from general EFL or paragraph-level tasks.

## Comparative Evidence: Against Standard Instruction, Teacher Feedback, and Non-AI Tools

The most favorable comparative evidence in the high-school corpus comes from the quasi-experimental Indian study of mobile ChatGPT feedback for senior secondary English learners, which found improved writing accuracy [105]. Because the retrieved summary does not include the exact n or exact test statistic, the study supports the direction of effect but not a precise magnitude. The Turkish secondary study of AI-assisted writing feedback also reported positive effects relative to traditional feedback, but again the captured evidence lacks the exact quantitative detail needed for a fuller appraisal [53]. A third directly relevant secondary comparative study in Morocco used ChatGPT as formative feedback on paragraph writing and likewise points toward positive effects, though the retrieval provides no exact numerical result in the current record [46]. In aggregate, these studies make a reasonable but not definitive case that AI feedback can improve some writing outcomes relative to business-as-usual instruction.

However, the comparative record is not strong enough to conclude superiority over high-quality teacher feedback. The broader feedback literature in the pre-scored set shows that peer and teacher feedback depend heavily on training, task structure, and student uptake [10][7]. AWE studies indicate that automated feedback can improve revision, but benefits are often strongest for conventions and other local features [113][31]. The systematic review of ChatGPT feedback in EFL writing concludes that evidence is diverse and methodologically uneven, which implies that comparative superiority claims should be made cautiously [46]. The key practical reading is that AI can be useful, but it has not been shown to replace a strong teacher feedback system in grades 9–12.

The evidence is also mixed on whether AI beats conventional digital supports. Spellcheck, grammar check, and graphic organizers already help students with mechanics and organization, and the older high-school AWE literature shows that these tools can produce specific gains without generative AI [31][113][12]. The high school writing-aid study on spell and grammar tools found trait-level benefits but no clear holistic improvement, showing the ceiling of surface-support technologies [31]. Generative AI may be more flexible than spellcheck and richer than classic AWE, but the current evidence does not yet show clear superiority across high school outcomes [46][64][31]. In effect, the field has not proved that the newest tools are better than the older, better-understood ones.

## Effects by Population, Task, and Context

The evidence suggests some likely heterogeneity, but direct subgroup analysis remains sparse. Multilingual learners and EFL students are the most visible populations in the secondary literature, and some of the highest-profile studies are precisely in those groups [105][52][88][89][91]. This makes sense because AI tools can provide immediate language feedback, which may be especially attractive for students writing in a second language. At the same time, the literature warns that improved surface correctness may not equal improved underlying composing ability, especially for multilingual learners whose content knowledge or rhetorical development may be obscured by AI support [89][41][48]. The high-school evidence does not yet resolve whether AI narrows or widens achievement gaps.

Students with writing difficulties are another likely beneficiary group, but the direct evidence remains mostly analogical. The comparator literature shows that structured interventions like SRSD and graphic organizers help struggling writers and students with disabilities [7][12]. The AI literature suggests that students who need more immediate correction or scaffolding may benefit from AI feedback, but the actual subgroup analyses are limited [53][105][21]. The most cautious interpretation is that AI may be promising for students with high feedback needs, but it could also magnify inequity if only some students have the AI literacy, device access, or self-regulation needed to use it well [38][149][85].

Task type seems to matter more clearly than subgroup. Shorter, constrained tasks such as paragraph writing or topic-specific compositions appear more amenable to AI feedback than extended reading-to-write or source-based tasks [46][105][53]. The literature on prompt engineering and AI-generated text editing suggests that when tasks are bounded, students can use AI iteratively and reflectively [52][88][91]. By contrast, the evidence for long-form essays, synthesis writing, or deeper disciplinary writing is thinner [93][114][142]. This means that schools should be skeptical of broad claims that AI improves “writing” in general; the likely effects differ by genre and assignment complexity.

Context matters as well. Homework and independent writing settings are theoretically important because they test whether students can use AI without teacher scaffolding, but this is one of the weakest evidence areas in the retrieved corpus. The most relevant evidence comes indirectly from studies of schoolwork use, self-regulation, and AI use patterns rather than direct homework trials [38][21][85]. The available data suggest that self-regulation and executive functioning shape usage, implying that independent settings may magnify differences between students who can and cannot use AI responsibly [38][90]. That is a major limitation for claims about home-based literacy improvement.

## Risks, Tradeoffs, and Unintended Consequences

The literature is unusually clear that AI writing assistants create tradeoffs. The first is dependence: students may become reliant on suggestions rather than developing independent composing skill. This concern appears repeatedly in the conceptual literature and is partially supported by the high-school over-reliance study, which found that a short AI-literacy intervention did not reduce over-reliance on incorrect ChatGPT recommendations [85][43][146]. The implication is straightforward: if students can use AI only when it is available, then the tool may improve products without improving writers.

A second risk is accuracy. The secondary AI-literacy and hallucination-aware use literature indicates that students can be exposed to incorrect, misleading, or hallucinated suggestions [90][92]. This is not just a technical issue; it is a literacy issue because students need to evaluate model output, not just accept it. The same concern appears in AI-integrity and student-voice studies, which note worries about plagiarism, authenticity, and who “owns” the writing [149][67][145]. These are especially salient in high school, where teacher judgment of independent writing is part of the assessment system.

A third risk is equity. The retrieval suggests that students differ in AI literacy, access, and readiness to use the tools productively [38][94][149]. The evidence base does not yet quantify these disparities well, but it does show they are plausible and likely. For multilingual learners, AI may help language access while also obscuring actual competence if overused as a translation or rewriting machine [19][89][48]. For struggling writers, AI may reduce frustration while also short-circuiting the productive struggle that helps writing improve. These tradeoffs are central to implementation decisions.

Finally, there is a validity problem for assessment. If students use AI for drafting, revising, or polishing, then teacher judgments about what students can do independently become harder to interpret. This issue is raised across academic-integrity and AI-assessment sources and is especially important for school policy [157][159][160][67]. Schools therefore face a dual challenge: they need to decide whether AI is allowed and, if so, how to preserve the interpretability of writing assessments.

### Synthesis and Implications

Taken as a whole, the evidence suggests a conditional, process-dependent answer: AI writing assistants can improve some high school literacy outcomes, but mainly in the short term and mainly when the task is constrained, the feedback is specific, and the student is supported to revise rather than merely copy or accept output [105][53][46][88][91]. The strongest picture is not “AI replaces instruction” but “AI augments instruction.” That conclusion aligns with the baseline literature on SRSD, graphic organizers, and AWE, all of which show that writing development depends on structured support and feedback uptake [1][12][113][31]. The practical implication is that schools should view AI as one more scaffold in a well-designed writing curriculum, not as a standalone improvement strategy.

For practitioners, the evidence supports cautious, bounded adoption. If schools use AI writing assistants, they should require explicit revision, teach prompt literacy, and build reflection into the writing cycle [92][144][149]. Teacher mediation is not a proven moderator in the strict statistical sense, but it is the most consistently argued implementation condition in the corpus and is likely necessary for preserving learning value [145][146][149]. Schools should also protect assessment validity by separating supported drafting from independent writing samples, especially in grades 9–12 [157][160][67]. In short, the safest path is hybrid use with clear rules, not open-ended access.

### Limitations and Research Gaps

The weakest evidence tier is Tier 4, especially for comparative claims against well-implemented teacher feedback and for long-term transfer to unaided writing. The high-school-specific causal evidence is sparse, and the best studies often omit critical details such as exact dosage, sample size, or delayed follow-up [105][53][46]. The literature also relies heavily on EFL and secondary international contexts, which limits generalizability to mainstream U.S. high school ELA and content-area classrooms [52][88][89][91]. Stronger trials in grades 9–12 are needed, particularly against SRSD, teacher feedback, and non-AI writing supports [1][12][113].

The evidence is especially weak on retention, independent writing after tool removal, and subgroup differences. Very few studies directly test whether students can transfer gains to unaided writing or whether benefits differ for multilingual learners, students with disabilities, or students with writing difficulties [38][19][85][105]. Homework and independent writing contexts are also under-studied despite their importance to the brief [21][38][94]. Finally, implementation fidelity is often underreported: prompt structure, teacher training, usage constraints, and monitoring are discussed conceptually but seldom tied to measured outcomes [91][92][144][149]. These are not small omissions; they are the central unknowns that prevent a definitive efficacy claim.
