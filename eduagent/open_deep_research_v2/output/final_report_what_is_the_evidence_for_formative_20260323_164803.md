## Executive Summary

The literature supports a clear but qualified conclusion: formative assessment is most consistently defined as an ongoing process of eliciting evidence of student understanding during instruction and using that evidence to adapt teaching, feedback, and learner actions, and it is generally associated with better student learning outcomes than instruction that relies mainly on summative testing or untargeted practice (Black and Wiliam, 2009; Andrade et al., 2021; Rashid and Jaidin, 2014; McCarthy et al., 2025). The direction of evidence is positive across K-12 and postsecondary settings, but the construct remains operationally unstable because many studies label adjacent practices—feedback, quizzes, self-assessment, peer assessment, progress monitoring, and adaptive tutoring—as “formative assessment” even when the full evidence-to-adaptation cycle is not demonstrated (Andrade et al., 2021; McCarthy et al., 2025; Karaman, 2021).

The strongest experimental evidence is concentrated in meta-analyses and a smaller number of randomized or quasi-experimental studies. Karaman (2021) meta-analyzed 32 studies with 47 effect sizes and reported an overall effect of formative assessment on student learning of g = .72, SE = .07, 95% CI = .59, .85, p < .05; subgroup effects were d = 1.16 for student-initiated formative feedback, d = .83 for mixed feedback, d = .69 for adult-initiated formative feedback, and d = .42 for computer-initiated formative feedback, with substantial heterogeneity (Q = 188.91, df = 46, p < .05; I² = 75.65%) (Karaman, 2021). Xuan et al. (2022) meta-analyzed K-12 reading studies and reported an average weighted effect of 0.19, with no meaningful moderation by grade band or mainstream versus at-risk status, a nonsignificant digital-technology effect (d = 0.001, p = .978), and stronger effects when teachers used differentiated instruction and when formative assessment was jointly enacted by teachers and students (Xuan et al., 2022). Yao et al. (2024) synthesized 258 effect sizes from 118 primary studies and concluded that formative assessment has positive effects on K-12 learning, but the pooled estimate was not reported in the available notes (Yao et al., 2024). Sortwell et al. (2024) reviewed 13 meta-analyses and found effects ranging from trivial to large with no negative effects identified, but certainty was low to very low (Sortwell et al., 2024).

Among primary causal studies, the clearest K-12 trial evidence is the secondary-school EFL study by Zhang et al. (2025), a quasi-experimental intervention with 89 middle-school students in China over eight weeks. The formative-assessment group received formative-assessment-based instruction, while the comparison group received conventional teaching; the study reported that formative assessment most strongly enhanced relatedness and that autonomy was the strongest predictor of English achievement, but the exact achievement statistic was not reported in the retrieved text (Zhang et al., 2025). In postsecondary education, Nguyen et al. (2023) conducted a quasi-experimental blended-learning study with 271 participants, reduced to 78 students per matched group, and reported that a formative assessment model significantly improved students’ motivation and engagement, with effect sizes ranging from 0.38 to 0.56 (Nguyen et al., 2023). Beekman et al. (2021) followed 695 Dutch sixth graders from 17 schools longitudinally and found that self-regulation and motivation developed positively in association with formative self- and peer-assessment and remained positively associated after the transition to secondary school, but the exact coefficients were not reported in the retrieved notes (Beekman et al., 2021). Ismail et al. (2022) compared formative and summative assessment in 72 Iranian EFL learners and reported significantly improved self-regulatory skills in the formative groups, but the exact statistics were not reported in the available material (Ismail et al., 2022).

The tiered questions are answered unevenly. Tier 1 is partially answered: the literature provides a reasonably coherent definition of formative assessment as evidence elicitation plus instructional adaptation, and it repeatedly distinguishes formative from summative assessment by timing and use, but the boundary with feedback, classroom assessment, peer/self-assessment, and diagnostic assessment remains blurred (Black and Wiliam, 2009; Andrade et al., 2021; McCarthy et al., 2025). Tier 2 is only partially answered: common baselines include standard instruction, summative-only assessment, teacher judgment, quizzes, feedback-only interventions, and progress monitoring, but many studies do not define the counterfactual cleanly enough to support strict comparison (Rashid and Jaidin, 2014; Chen, 2020; Palabıyık and Daloglu, 2025). Tier 3 is partially answered: implementation commonly uses exit tickets, quizzes, rubrics, peer and self-assessment, and teacher feedback cycles, and the proposed mechanisms are timely feedback, instructional adaptation, metacognition, motivation, and self-regulation, but fidelity and dosage are often underreported (Andrade et al., 2021; Wenzel et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2023). Tier 4 is partially answered: formative assessment tends to improve outcomes, but effect sizes are heterogeneous and often modest, the strongest syntheses are concentrated in K-12 literacy and broad classroom assessment, and evidence remains limited for retention, far transfer, and subgroup equity effects across postsecondary, STEM, multilingual, disability, and socioeconomic strata (Xuan et al., 2022; Sortwell et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2024).

Overall confidence is moderate. The single most important caveat is that many studies do not isolate formative assessment as a clean intervention: they evaluate feedback, quizzes, peer assessment, self-regulation supports, or digital tools that may or may not function formatively depending on whether evidence is used to adapt instruction, which weakens causal attribution and limits comparability across studies (Andrade et al., 2021; McCarthy et al., 2025; Karaman, 2021).

### Synthesis and Implications

Across the corpus, formative assessment is best understood as a process rather than a single tool. The defining feature is not whether an activity is a quiz, peer review, rubric, or teacher question; rather, it is whether evidence of student understanding is elicited and then used to modify teaching and learning in real time (Black and Wiliam, 2009; Andrade et al., 2021; Rashid and Jaidin, 2014). That distinction matters because many ostensibly “formative” studies are actually about feedback, low-stakes testing, or self-regulated learning supports, and the same instrument can be formative or summative depending on how the information is used (McCarthy et al., 2025; Chen, 2020; Palabıyık and Daloglu, 2025).

The outcome literature is broader than the intervention literature. Achievement is the most commonly measured endpoint, but the studies retrieved here repeatedly extend to motivation, self-regulation, metacognition, engagement, and relatedness, especially in EFL and blended-learning settings (Ismail et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). Where the evidence is strongest, formative assessment appears to improve learning most when it is embedded in an instructional cycle that includes actionable feedback and visible pedagogical response, rather than being used as a standalone scoring mechanism (Karaman, 2021; Xuan et al., 2022; Beekman et al., 2021).

The practical implication for schools and instructors is that simply adding more quizzes, rubrics, or digital tools is unlikely to reproduce the effect unless the assessment information is actively used to change instruction. Several studies and reviews emphasize teacher capacity, differentiated instruction, and student participation as important conditions for success, which suggests that professional development and feedback literacy are central implementation levers (Wenzel et al., 2023; Xuan et al., 2022; Andrade et al., 2021). The evidence also suggests that formative assessment is most promising when it supports learner agency, because the strongest subgroup effects in the meta-analysis were linked to student-initiated or jointly enacted feedback processes (Karaman, 2021; Xuan et al., 2022).

The evidence base is not equally mature across sectors. K-12 reading and broad K-12 meta-analyses are the best synthesized areas, while postsecondary evidence is thinner, more heterogeneous, and more likely to be dominated by quasi-experimental, qualitative, or tool-specific studies rather than large randomized trials (Xuan et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2024; Sortwell et al., 2024). Subject coverage is also uneven: literacy, EFL/writing, mathematics, and science are better represented than social studies, arts, vocational education, and many graduate or professional domains. For policy and practice, this means formative assessment should be treated as a design principle that must be adapted locally rather than as a universal package with a stable effect size.

### Limitations and Research Gaps

The largest limitation is construct instability. The literature repeatedly uses formative assessment to refer to feedback, quizzes, peer assessment, self-assessment, progress monitoring, adaptive instruction, and classroom assessment more broadly, often without demonstrating that evidence was actually used to adapt teaching (Andrade et al., 2021; McCarthy et al., 2025; Chen, 2020). This makes it difficult to determine whether positive findings reflect formative assessment proper or adjacent practices that are only partially formative.

The second limitation is baseline ambiguity. Many studies compare formative assessment to “conventional teaching,” “standard instruction,” or “summative assessment,” but the comparator is often not described with enough precision to know whether the control condition already contained informal formative elements (Rashid and Jaidin, 2014; Palabıyık and Daloglu, 2025). That contamination likely attenuates estimated effects and makes cross-study comparison difficult.

A third gap is uneven causal rigor. Although there are useful meta-analyses and some quasi-experiments, the number of clean, well-powered randomized trials remains limited, especially in postsecondary education and in subjects outside literacy and EFL (Karaman, 2021; Xuan et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2023). Many studies also use short follow-up windows, weak reporting of implementation fidelity, or self-report outcomes that are more vulnerable to expectancy and social desirability bias than achievement tests.

Equity and subgroup evidence remain underdeveloped. The best-synthesized K-12 reading meta-analysis found no significant differences by grade band or mainstream versus at-risk status and no digital-technology moderation, but the overall literature still provides little direct evidence for effects by disability status, multilingual learner status, socioeconomic background, race/ethnicity, or prior attainment across subjects (Xuan et al., 2022; Sortwell et al., 2024). The field therefore cannot yet support strong claims about who benefits most, under what conditions, or whether formative assessment reduces or reproduces existing inequities.

Finally, durability is still a weak point. Retention, transfer, and long-term self-regulation are frequently invoked as important outcomes, but only a minority of studies measure them directly, and fewer still do so with delayed post-tests or longitudinal designs (Beekman et al., 2021; Ryan et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2025). The clearest next step is more rigorous trials that specify the comparator, document instructional adaptation, and track not only immediate achievement but also retention, transfer, motivation, and self-regulated learning over meaningful time spans.

## Body of Evidence Maturity: LIMITED
Justification: The evidence base is conceptually well developed and includes several meta-analyses, systematic reviews, and a smaller set of quasi-experimental studies, but clean randomized evidence is limited and the construct is inconsistently operationalized across studies. Coverage is strongest in K-12 literacy and broad classroom assessment, while postsecondary, equity-relevant subgroup, and long-term outcome evidence remains comparatively thin.
