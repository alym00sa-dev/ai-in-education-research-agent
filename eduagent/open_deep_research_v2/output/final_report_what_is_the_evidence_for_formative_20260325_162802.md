## Executive Summary

The evidence indicates that formative assessment in K-12 education is generally associated with improved academic achievement relative to business-as-usual instruction, but the average benefits are modest to moderate and highly contingent on implementation quality, subject area, and instructional responsiveness. The strongest pooled evidence comes from the K-12 reading meta-analysis, which found an overall effect of ES = +0.19 across 48 studies and 116,051 students, with larger effects in smaller studies, in interventions that embedded differentiated instruction, and in designs that integrated teacher and student assessment rather than teacher-only assessment [212]. A broader umbrella review of 13 meta-analyses likewise found trivial to large positive effects with no negative effects identified, but judged the underlying certainty low to very low overall [211]. The evidence base is therefore directionally positive, but not strong enough to support a “one-size-fits-all” claim about any single formative assessment model [212][211].

The strongest direct experimental findings are mostly subject-specific rather than K-12-wide. In secondary physics, a randomized experiment with 139 students found that repeated clicker-based formative assessments improved post-test performance by 0.34 SD relative to traditional teaching and reduced anxiety by 0.28 SD (Toward Reducing Anxiety and Increasing Performance in Physics Education, 2021). In secondary chemistry, technology-integrated formative assessment produced higher conceptual scores and procedural knowledge than formative assessment alone and conventional instruction, with large group effects reported as ηp² = 0.21 for conceptual knowledge and ηp² = 0.27 for procedural knowledge [346]. In high school mathematics, a 12-week quasi-experimental study with 51 tenth graders found formative assessment practices significantly improved mathematics achievement and attitudes, but the exact effect size was not reported in the extracted text (Kültür & Kutlu, 2021). In writing, the meta-analysis by Graham, Hebert, and [301] found a pooled effect of d = .63 across grades 1–8, with teacher feedback strongest at d = .89, self-assessment and peer feedback both at d = .62, and computer feedback at d = .38. In reading, [212] found ES = +0.19 and identified substantial moderation by sample size, differentiated instruction, and integrated teacher-student assessment. In a broader K-12 meta-analysis, [309] reported 258 effect sizes from 118 studies and an overall Hedges’ g = 0.25, with larger effects in high school and in quasi-experimental studies, though subgroup details for vulnerable student populations were not provided in the extracted text.

The quasi-experimental and observational evidence broadly corroborates the positive pattern while clarifying implementation and equity conditions. In primary-grade mathematics, [218] found that formative assessment and summative assessment were positively related, that the relationship was stronger in first grade than kindergarten, and that lower-performing classrooms benefited more, though grade level was not ultimately a significant moderator. Boström and [317] reported that a formative assessment practice improved student achievement in mathematics in a secondary-school study, but the extracted text did not provide the exact effect statistic. In primary science, embedded formative assessment studies showed positive conceptual learning and classroom-process effects, but several of these studies are implementation-oriented and do not report fully extractable effect sizes in the available snippets. In ELL-focused reading synthesis, [300] found medium effects for interventions targeting basic reading skills for English learners and reported that upper-elementary students benefited more than secondary students, with strategy-embedded and medium-sized group interventions particularly useful; however, this is adjacent evidence about reading intervention generally rather than formative assessment alone.

Across the tiered questions, the evidence answers Tier 1 partially to well: formative assessment is consistently defined as the elicitation and use of evidence during instruction to adapt teaching, and the most studied outcomes are reading, mathematics, writing, and science achievement, usually via standardized tests or teacher-developed posttests [212][301][346]. Tier 2 is answered only partially: comparison conditions are often described as traditional teaching, conventional instruction, or usual classroom practice, but many studies do not fully specify the counterfactual and several reviews note conceptual overlap with feedback, quizzes, and diagnostic assessment [211][284]. Tier 3 is also partial: the literature clearly identifies core implementation features such as questioning, feedback, self/peer assessment, success criteria, and instructional adjustment, but direct causal tests of specific implementation components remain limited [223][212][213]. Tier 4 is partially answered for reading, writing, mathematics, and some science contexts, but remains weak for English learners, students with disabilities, low-income students, and long-term retention/transfer; subgroup evidence is sparse and often indirect. Overall confidence is moderate. The single most important caveat is that “formative assessment” is not a single intervention: its effects depend on whether evidence is actually used to change instruction, and the current literature does not yet provide robust, cross-subgroup causal estimates.

## Defining formative assessment in K-12 education

Across the retrieved literature, formative assessment is consistently defined as an in-process instructional practice that elicits evidence of student learning and uses that evidence to adapt teaching and learning. [206] define it as a systematic process that continuously gathers evidence and provides feedback while instruction is under way, and they emphasize that evidence must be used to adapt teaching to meet learner needs [206]. [371] describe formative assessment as teachers continuously collecting evidence of understanding and skill and providing specific, relevant feedback to move learning forward, while noting that the evidence must be used to adapt teaching to students’ needs [371]. Black and Wiliam’s definition recurs across multiple sources as the benchmark formulation: formative assessment is the use of evidence about student achievement to make better decisions about next instructional steps, and it is formative only when that evidence is actually used to modify teaching and learning [212][223][387].

The literature also repeatedly distinguishes formative assessment from summative, diagnostic, and interim assessment. Summative assessment is described as end-of-unit or end-of-course grading, certification, or accountability; diagnostic assessment is framed as prior-knowledge or needs identification; and interim assessment is described as occurring between formative and summative assessment in frequency and scope [217][206][392]. Several authors explicitly caution that not all quizzes, tests, or feedback are formative. [387] argues that a quiz becomes formative only when the elicited evidence is interpreted, relayed back to students, and used by both teacher and student to improve learning. Similarly, [384] list nongraded quizzes, teacher observation, oral questioning, essay drafting, and self- and peer-assessment as formative when they are used to guide teaching and learning rather than merely to assign scores [384].

### What outcomes and populations are most studied?

The dominant outcomes are academic achievement measures, especially standardized or teacher-developed reading, mathematics, writing, and science tests. The reading meta-analysis by [212] centered on standardized reading achievement. The writing meta-analysis by [301] focused on writing quality across grades 1–8. The mathematics studies most often used posttests of mathematical principles, curriculum-based mathematics measures, or statewide assessments. Science studies measured conceptual understanding, procedural knowledge, inquiry skills, or achievement in physics, chemistry, and basic science [301][212][346][324][218].

The populations are heavily concentrated in elementary and secondary classrooms, with fewer studies at the early elementary and high school ends of the K-12 continuum. The reading meta-analysis included kindergarten, elementary, and secondary education [212]. Writing evidence spans grades 1–8 [301]. Mathematics evidence is spread across primary, middle, and secondary school, with substantial representation in grade 1–2, grade 7–8, and grade 10 studies [218][317][324]. Science evidence is thinner and more fragmented, with a mix of elementary, middle, and secondary studies [346][319][233].

The literature also shows that formative assessment is often studied alongside teacher feedback, self-assessment, peer assessment, and differentiated instruction. [212] explicitly treated teacher-directed, student-directed, and integrated teacher-student assessment as different intervention forms. [301] found distinct effect sizes by feedback source. [213] similarly showed differences among student-, mixed-, adult-, and computer-initiated formative feedback. This means the term “formative assessment” in the evidence base often refers to a family of practices rather than a single standardized intervention.

## Baseline and comparison conditions

The counterfactual in this literature is usually some version of conventional teaching, traditional instruction, or business-as-usual classroom practice. In the randomized physics experiment, the comparison group received traditional teaching rather than clicker-based formative assessment (Toward Reducing Anxiety and Increasing Performance in Physics Education, 2021). In Hagos and Andargie’s chemistry study, the comparison conditions were formative assessment alone and conventional instruction, against which technology-integrated formative assessment performed better [346]. In Kültür and Kutlu’s mathematics quasi-experiment, the control group did not receive formative assessment practices and the treatment group did (Kültür & Kutlu, 2021). [218] compared formative and summative assessment data in primary classrooms rather than testing a clean treatment-control contrast, so it is better interpreted as evidence about assessment relationships than as a direct causal comparison.

Several reviews note that comparison conditions are often under-specified. [211] emphasize heterogeneity in what counts as formative assessment across the 13 meta-analyses they reviewed, which limits comparability across studies. [284] similarly note that the literature uses multiple overlapping terms and that classroom assessment is sometimes invoked as formative even when the assessment information is not clearly used to change instruction. This means the counterfactual is often “usual practice,” but usual practice varies substantially by teacher, subject, school, and accountability context [284][211].

## Implementation features and mechanisms

The mechanisms most often proposed are immediate feedback, clearer learning goals, instructional adjustment, student self-regulation, and earlier identification of misconceptions. [223] describe classroom assessment as co-regulated learning: teachers and students jointly gather and interpret evidence, then use it to revise teaching and learning processes. [212] show that reading effects are stronger when formative assessment is coupled with differentiated instruction and integrated teacher-student participation, which supports the idea that formative assessment works by changing subsequent instruction rather than by assessment alone. [213] found larger effects for student-initiated and mixed feedback than for adult-initiated or computer-initiated feedback, suggesting that learner involvement matters [223][212][213].

Peer and self-assessment are important implementation modes, especially when students are expected to monitor their own work. [431] found a significant positive effect of peer assessment on academic performance for primary and secondary students, with g = .29, which supports the plausibility of peer-mediated formative work. [301] found that self-assessment and peer feedback both improved writing quality, though not as much as teacher feedback. These findings suggest that student participation in formative processes can be beneficial, but the evidence also implies that teacher guidance remains essential, especially for younger learners and potentially for learners with special educational needs [301][431][212].

Professional development appears to be a recurring enabling condition, not just an optional add-on. [212] report that 94% of differentiated-instruction interventions in their reading synthesis were coupled with teacher professional development. This is consistent with broader review evidence that effective formative assessment requires teacher assessment literacy and systematic use of evidence. However, teacher development does not automatically translate into student gains: Schneider and Meyer’s PD study in middle school English language arts and mathematics improved teacher assessment skill, but the extracted abstract text notes that students of participating teachers tended to demonstrate lower achievement than matched comparison students [228]. That finding cautions against assuming that better teacher practice in the short run will always produce higher student achievement in the same time frame.

Technology is another major implementation theme. In reading, [212] found no significant moderation by digital technology, suggesting that digital delivery alone is not the key mechanism. In science and mathematics, however, technology-integrated formative assessment, clicker-based checks for understanding, automated feedback, and online adaptive systems often show positive results [7][346][221]. The implication is not that technology is inherently effective, but that it can make evidence collection, feedback, and rapid response more feasible when well aligned with instruction.

## Effects on academic achievement

The clearest pooled effect for K-12 achievement comes from reading. [212] found an overall effect of ES = +0.19 across 48 studies and 116,051 students. They also report that smaller studies produced larger effects, differentiated instruction strengthened effects, and integrated teacher-student formative assessment outperformed teacher-only assessment. This is the most directly relevant K-12 meta-analysis for the research brief, but it is reading-specific rather than fully cross-subject [212].

Writing shows a larger pooled estimate than reading in the retrieved evidence. [301] found d = .63 across grades 1–8, with teacher feedback at d = .89, self-assessment and peer feedback at d = .62, and computer feedback at d = .38. This suggests that formative writing assessment can be a substantial lever when feedback is direct and revision-oriented. The higher effect for teacher feedback implies that formative assessment is especially effective when feedback is specific, expert, and linked to revision opportunities [301].

Mathematics effects are consistently positive but more heterogeneous. [213] reported an overall mean effect size of .72 across 32 studies and 47 effect sizes, with student-initiated formative feedback d = 1.16, mixed feedback d = .83, adult-initiated feedback d = .69, and computer-initiated feedback d = .42. In the primary-grade mathematics classroom study, formative and summative assessment were positively related, and the relationship was stronger in first grade than kindergarten and in lower-performing classrooms, though grade level was not a significant moderator overall [218]. Boström and [317] found that a formative assessment practice improved student achievement in mathematics in a secondary-school study, but the extracted text does not report the exact effect size. Together, these findings indicate that mathematics benefits are plausible and often substantial, but not uniform across grade bands or implementations [213][218][317].

Science evidence is promising but more fragmented. The randomized physics experiment reported a 0.34 SD gain in post-test performance and a 0.28 SD reduction in anxiety, suggesting that frequent formative checks with immediate feedback can improve both cognition and affect in high school science (Toward Reducing Anxiety and Increasing Performance in Physics Education, 2021). [346] found positive effects in chemistry for conceptual and procedural knowledge, with large partial eta-squared values for group effects. Other science studies, including primary science, inquiry skills, and concept-mapping-based formative assessment, largely support positive effects but often do not provide extractable effect estimates in the available snippets [346][319][233].

The broader K-12 meta-analysis by [309] is especially important because it is not limited to one subject. It synthesized 258 effect sizes from 118 studies and reported an overall Hedges’ g = 0.25. The extracted web summary indicates larger effects in high school and quasi-experimental studies, and larger effects outside North America and Western Europe, but the retrieved metadata did not provide the exact subgroup coefficients. Even so, the overall pooled estimate reinforces the conclusion that formative assessment is beneficial on average across K-12 settings, though the effect is not large enough to ignore implementation quality or context [309].

## Moderators, subgroup differences, and equity considerations

The strongest moderator evidence is for implementation features and grade band, not for equity subgroups. [212] found that reading effects were larger in smaller studies, in interventions with differentiated instruction, and in integrated teacher-student assessment. They also reported non-significant differences by mainstream versus at-risk student status, digital technology use, research design, program duration, and publication type. This is useful because it suggests the effects are not solely an artifact of one study design or delivery format, but it also underscores that the meta-analysis did not identify strong evidence that at-risk students responded differently from mainstream students in the available reading studies [212].

Primary-grade mathematics evidence suggests lower-performing classrooms may benefit more. [218] reported that the relationship between formative and summative assessment was stronger in lower-performing classrooms, and stronger in first grade than kindergarten. However, they also cautioned that grade level was not a significant moderator overall, so this should be read as suggestive rather than definitive. [213] found no moderation by education level or publication type, though the effect sizes were numerically largest at the primary level. These results point to developmental and instructional complexity as plausible moderators, but not yet as robust, replicated subgroup findings [218][213].

Direct evidence for English learners, students with disabilities, and low-income students is much thinner. [300] provide an adjacent multilevel meta-analysis of ELL reading interventions, not formative assessment alone, and found medium effects for basic reading skills with stronger effects in upper elementary than secondary grades. The Bookworms literacy curriculum study explicitly included ELL, FRL, special education, and baseline achievement as moderators, but it is not a formative-assessment-only intervention and its moderation analyses were described as preliminary. [212] did report mainstream versus at-risk comparisons, but not ELL, disability, or low-income subgroup estimates. Thus, the current evidence base does not support precise subgroup claims for these equity groups in formative assessment specifically [300][212][336].

The evidence on retention and transfer is especially limited. A technology-integrated chemistry study explicitly addressed retention of conceptual and procedural knowledge, and a primary science implementation study named subsequent transfer, but the available snippets do not provide enough detail for a strong synthesis of durability effects. Similarly, the physics experiment and some mathematics studies appear to assess short-term posttests rather than delayed maintenance. As a result, durable learning outcomes remain an important but under-specified part of the literature [346][325][7].

## Specific study findings

The randomized physics study by Toward Reducing Anxiety and Increasing Performance in Physics [7] is one of the clearest direct causal findings in the retrieved set. In a randomized experiment with 139 secondary physics students, repeated clicker-based formative assessments improved post-test physics performance relative to traditional teaching by 0.34 SD and reduced anxiety by 0.28 SD. The study therefore supports the claim that frequent evidence gathering plus immediate feedback can improve both achievement and affect in high school science (Toward Reducing Anxiety and Increasing Performance in Physics Education, 2021).

The chemistry study by [346] is another strong direct study. It examined technology-integrated formative assessment and found significantly higher conceptual chemistry scores than formative assessment alone and conventional instruction, with a large group effect of ηp² = 0.21, and similarly strong effects for procedural chemistry knowledge with ηp² = 0.27. This suggests that formative assessment may be particularly effective when combined with technology and when both conceptual and procedural learning are targeted [346].

The high school mathematics quasi-experiment by Kültür and [324] found that formative assessment practices significantly improved mathematics achievement and attitudes in a 12-week intervention with 51 tenth graders. Because the extracted text does not report a standardized effect size, the study should be treated as supportive but not quantitatively comparable to the meta-analytic evidence (Kültür & Kutlu, 2021).

The primary-grade mathematics study by [218] found a positive relationship between formative assessment data and summative assessment data. The relationship was stronger in first grade than kindergarten and stronger in lower-performing classrooms, though grade level was not a significant moderator overall. This makes the study especially useful for understanding how early grades and lower-performing classrooms may respond differently, but the evidence is correlational rather than a direct treatment effect estimate [218].

The writing meta-analysis by [301] is perhaps the clearest subject-specific synthesis after reading. It shows that formative writing assessment meaningfully improves writing quality, and that teacher feedback is the most effective source. The consistent ordering of teacher feedback above self, peer, and computer feedback suggests that formative assessment is strongest when teachers can interpret student writing, supply actionable guidance, and support revision cycles [301].

## Synthesis and Implications

Across subjects, the best-supported conclusion is that formative assessment improves K-12 academic achievement when it is more than routine testing. The intervention must elicit evidence during instruction, interpret that evidence, and lead to real instructional or learner adjustment. Where this chain is strongest, the outcomes are most consistently positive: reading gains are modest on average but reliable, writing gains are larger, mathematics gains are often substantial, and science findings are promising but less complete. The most persuasive implementation features are timely feedback, differentiated or responsive instruction, teacher-student collaboration, and teacher assessment literacy [212][301][213][223].

For schools and practitioners, the practical implication is that formative assessment should be designed as a cycle, not a one-off event. Teachers need tools to collect evidence efficiently, interpret it quickly, and adjust instruction immediately. Professional development is therefore not optional: the evidence suggests it is often bundled with stronger formative assessment interventions, and the success of the practice depends on whether teachers can translate evidence into differentiated instruction. In mathematics and writing especially, the literature suggests that teacher feedback and meaningful revision or reteaching are the most likely pathways to achievement gains [212][301][228].

## Limitations and Research Gaps

The evidence base remains uneven in several important ways. First, the strongest pooled evidence is concentrated in reading and writing, with mathematics and science more fragmented and high-school evidence thinner than elementary or middle school evidence in some areas. Second, direct subgroup estimates for English learners, students with disabilities, and low-income students are sparse or absent in the core formative-assessment intervention literature, even though these populations are frequently named in theory and policy sources. Third, many studies do not report sufficiently clear comparator conditions, implementation fidelity, or delayed follow-up outcomes, making it difficult to isolate the effect of formative assessment from feedback, diagnostic testing, or general classroom improvement [211][212][309].

Methodologically, the literature is also limited by heterogeneity in what counts as formative assessment, frequent dependence on small or single-site studies, and a heavy reliance on short-term posttests rather than retention or transfer measures. Several promising studies are quasi-experimental or observational rather than randomized, and some PD studies improve teacher practice without clearly improving student outcomes in the same time frame. Equity gaps remain central: the field still lacks a robust causal evidence base showing when formative assessment is especially beneficial for English learners, students with disabilities, or low-income students, and whether those groups need different implementation supports. The most important next step is a set of larger, clearly specified K-12 trials that compare well-defined formative assessment models against usual instruction and report subgroup, durability, and workload outcomes with sufficient precision.

## Body of Evidence Maturity: LIMITED
Justification: The evidence base is substantial and mostly positive, with multiple meta-analyses and several direct experiments/QEDs supporting achievement gains, but it is highly heterogeneous in intervention definition, outcome measurement, and comparators. Coverage is strongest for reading, writing, and mathematics, while equity subgroup evidence, long-term outcomes, and clear causal estimates in science and high school remain limited.
