**Query:** What is the evidence for formative assessment improving student learning outcomes?

**Date/Time:** 2026-03-23 18:24:18

## Executive Summary

The evidence indicates that formative assessment can improve student learning relative to typical instruction, but the average effect is usually modest and highly contingent on implementation quality, subject area, and whether assessment evidence is actually used to adapt teaching. The clearest causal pattern is not that “more testing” helps, but that eliciting evidence of understanding during instruction, interpreting it, and changing instruction or learning activity in response is associated with better outcomes, especially when feedback is timely, specific, and paired with reteaching, differentiation, or student self-regulation supports (Black and Wiliam, 2009)[190][206]. The strongest pooled K-12 evidence comes from reading: Xuan, Cheung, and [192] synthesized 48 studies with 116,051 K-12 students and found a positive but modest average effect on reading achievement, ES = +0.19. A broader umbrella review of 13 meta-analyses likewise concluded that formative assessment produced trivial-to-large positive effects with no negative effects identified, but the certainty of the evidence was low to very low, preventing robust prescriptions about the best strategy [191]. In short, formative assessment is promising and often beneficial, but its effects are not automatic and are rarely large in routine practice.

The strongest experimental studies reinforce that conclusion. In a cluster-randomized controlled intervention in secondary physics classes, [265] studied 29 teachers and 604 students (mean age 15.6 years). Ten teachers were trained to implement formative assessment through multiple-choice concept questions, monitoring tools, clicker sessions, and reflective lessons; 10 teachers used the same concept questions without formative-assessment training; and 9 teachers taught traditionally. Students in the formative-assessment group outperformed both comparison groups on conceptual understanding immediately after the intervention and again 3 months later, while the frequent-testing and traditional-teaching groups did not differ; the exact effect size was not reported in the extracted record. In the K-12 reading meta-analysis, [192] reported that effects were stronger when differentiated instruction was built in and when teacher- and student-directed assessment were integrated, whereas digital technology alone did not outperform paper-pen conditions; the pooled effect remained ES = +0.19. [197] meta-analyzed 32 experimental and quasi-experimental studies with 47 effect sizes and reported an overall mean effect size of .72 (SE = .07, p < .05), with subgroup effects favoring student-initiated formative feedback (d = 1.16), mixed feedback (d = .83), adult-initiated feedback (d = .69), and computer-initiated feedback (d = .42). Double, McGrane, and [227] meta-analyzed control-group studies of peer assessment and found that peer assessment improved academic performance relative to no assessment or teacher assessment, though the exact pooled effect was not provided in the extracted notes. In higher education, the evidence base is thinner, and the best available systematic review explicitly notes that there is very little causal evidence supporting formative assessment and feedback practices at that level (Morris, Perry, and Wardle, 2021).

The quasi-experimental and observational evidence generally corroborates the experimental findings while also clarifying the importance of implementation and baseline practice. [217] used a quasi-experimental non-equivalent control-group design in elementary mathematics and concluded that Assessment for Learning improved student learning outcomes, with effectiveness shaped by assessment preparation, student involvement, and follow-up assessment; the exact effect statistic was not reported. van den Berg, Bosker, and [318] reported that a classroom formative assessment model in Dutch primary mathematics used four daily formative cycles and a weekly quiz cycle, whereas the comparison condition relied on half-yearly standardized tests and pre-teaching after those tests; the study notes describe the control condition as “low-impact” relative to the intervention, but the exact effect size was not extracted. [193] ran a randomized trial in 140 English secondary schools embedding formative assessment as standard practice; the study was identified as a large-scale causal test, but the extracted record does not provide the outcome statistic. In higher education, [245] found that computer-based feedback and formative assessment via a student response system improved posttest performance in physics (n = 148; experimental n = 75, control n = 73), but the effect size was not reported in the notes. Papajorgji et al. (2020) reported significant pre-post achievement gains and improved motivation across four cohorts of university science students (n = 311) using an online formative assessment tool, again without a fully extracted effect statistic. Observational and qualitative studies repeatedly show that the active ingredients are not the label “formative assessment” itself but the concrete practices: eliciting evidence, interpreting it, and using it for adaptation, often with feedback, peer/self-assessment, and teacher judgment [194][190][206].

The tiered questions are answered unevenly. Tier 1, on definition and scope, is answered fairly well: formative assessment is consistently defined as an ongoing process in which teachers and/or students elicit evidence of understanding, interpret it, and use it to adapt instruction or learning tactics (Black and Wiliam, 2009)[190][206]. Tier 2, on baseline instruction and comparators, is only partially answered: control conditions commonly involve business-as-usual instruction, regular curricula, textbook-led teaching, homework, periodic quizzes, and summative or benchmark tests, but these baselines vary widely and often already include informal feedback, making “standard instruction” a moving target [224][318][227]. Tier 3, on mechanisms and implementation, is also partial: the literature strongly implicates feedback, reteaching, differentiation, and student self-regulation, but direct mechanistic tests are rare, and fidelity, dosage, and teacher assessment literacy are often underreported [190][206]. Tier 4, on comparative effects and boundary conditions, is the strongest but still incomplete: effects are generally positive, often modest, and larger when formative assessment is integrated with differentiated instruction, student involvement, or well-structured teacher training; however, postsecondary evidence remains sparse, subject coverage is uneven, and subgroup evidence for multilingual learners, students with disabilities, and low-SES learners remains limited [192][191][196].

Overall confidence is moderate. The single most important caveat is that “formative assessment” is not a single standardized intervention; the evidence base mixes feedback, peer assessment, self-assessment, mastery learning, technology-supported quizzes, diagnostic tools, and teacher PD, so effects depend heavily on what exactly is implemented, how well it is implemented, and whether the evidence actually changes instruction or learner behavior.

## Synthesis and Implications

Formative assessment is best understood as a decision cycle, not a single tool. Across the literature, the core mechanism is consistent: evidence of student understanding is elicited during instruction, interpreted against a learning goal, and used to change what happens next for teaching or learning (Black and Wiliam, 2009)[190][206]. This is why the strongest studies do not merely add quizzes; they change the instructional response. In the physics cluster-RCT by [265], the intervention combined concept questions, monitoring tools, clicker sessions, and reflective lessons, and students outperformed comparison groups on conceptual understanding immediately and 3 months later. In the K-12 reading meta-analysis, Xuan, Cheung, and [192] found that effects were stronger when formative assessment was paired with differentiated instruction and integrated teacher- and student-directed activity. These findings imply that formative assessment is most effective when it functions as part of a responsive teaching system.

Baseline conditions matter a great deal, because “standard instruction” is often not passive or empty. In the studies reviewed, control groups commonly received regular curriculum instruction, homework, textbook-based teaching, periodic quizzes, teacher feedback based on ordinary rubrics, benchmark tests, or summative assessments used for grading or monitoring [224][318][227]. In some cases, the control condition already included ordinary teacher judgment or casual feedback, which means the estimated incremental impact of a formative intervention is necessarily modest. This helps explain why large positive effects are not the norm in meta-analysis. It also means that the practical question is not whether to “use formative assessment,” but how to improve from informal, uneven, or end-of-unit monitoring to a more systematic and instructionally actionable process.

Mechanistically, the literature points most often to feedback, reteaching, scaffolding, peer/self-assessment, and student self-regulation. However, these mechanisms are more often asserted than isolated experimentally. The evidence is strongest when the formative process gives learners or teachers something actionable before the next learning opportunity. That is why peer assessment can help when it produces usable information for revision, and why feedback-only systems are weaker when students simply receive comments without structured opportunity to act on them [227][196]. The evidence also suggests that student agency matters: student-initiated or mixed feedback appears stronger than computer-initiated feedback in the [197] synthesis, and the K-12 reading meta-analysis found better outcomes when teacher and student roles were combined rather than kept separate.

For schools and practitioners, the most defensible implication is that formative assessment should be implemented as a coherent classroom routine with clear learning targets, frequent evidence collection, actionable feedback, and a planned instructional response. The evidence does not support the idea that simply increasing the number of quizzes or collecting more data will improve learning. It does support professional development that helps teachers interpret evidence, select the right next step, and differentiate instruction. This is especially important in mathematics, science, and reading, where the strongest evidence clusters. In digital contexts, the lesson is similar: technology can make evidence collection more efficient, but it does not substitute for pedagogical judgment or instructional adaptation [194][206]. In postsecondary education, the practical guidance is more tentative because the causal evidence base is much thinner (Morris, Perry, and Wardle, 2021).

## Limitations and Research Gaps

The main limitation of the evidence base is heterogeneity. “Formative assessment” covers teacher questioning, diagnostic quizzes, student self-assessment, peer assessment, mastery learning, adaptive digital systems, clicker-based questioning, feedback cycles, and curriculum-embedded checks, which are related but not interchangeable interventions [238][206]. This makes pooled estimates difficult to interpret and limits the portability of any average effect. The strongest meta-analytic reading estimate, ES = +0.19, is modest and subject-specific, while broader syntheses report very low certainty despite positive directionality [192][191].

A second limitation is that many studies do not cleanly characterize the baseline comparator. Control groups often receive ordinary teacher feedback, benchmark tests, homework, and routine classroom monitoring, so treatment effects are incremental relative to already-active instruction rather than relative to a truly inert baseline [224][318][227]. That makes causal interpretation harder and suggests that some “formative assessment effects” are actually effects of adding structure, specificity, or instructional follow-through to practices already in place.

A third limitation is the thin postsecondary evidence base. The higher-education systematic review by Morris, Perry, and [196] explicitly concluded that causal evidence is scarce, and much of the postsecondary literature is descriptive, bibliometric, or design-oriented rather than experimental. As a result, confidence is much higher for K-12 than for college and university settings. This is especially problematic because postsecondary classes often have larger enrollments, weaker teacher-student continuity, and greater reliance on digital systems, all of which may alter implementation and effects.

A fourth limitation is that implementation fidelity, dosage, and teacher assessment literacy are often underreported. Many studies describe what should happen in an ideal formative cycle, but fewer measure how often teachers actually elicit evidence, whether they interpret it accurately, whether they change instruction, and whether students subsequently act on the feedback [190][206]. This matters because fidelity is likely a major moderator of effect. The literature repeatedly points to training, workload, class size, and curriculum pressure as barriers, but these factors are not yet synthesized in a way that allows strong causal moderator claims.

Equity and subgroup evidence remains incomplete. Some studies suggest stronger benefits for lower-achieving learners, and some reviews mention students with special educational needs, but there is little direct comparative evidence for multilingual learners, specific disability categories, rural/urban differences, or intersectional subgroups. Subject coverage is also uneven: reading, mathematics, and science dominate, while humanities beyond ELA, vocational education, and many postsecondary domains remain underrepresented. Long-term outcomes such as retention, transfer, metacognition, and self-regulation are frequently named as intended benefits, but comparatively few rigorous studies isolate them as primary outcomes.

## Body of Evidence Maturity: LIMITED

Justification: The evidence base is substantial and directionally consistent, with several meta-analyses and at least one strong cluster-RCT showing positive effects, but the literature is highly heterogeneous and the certainty of the pooled evidence is often low. Coverage is strongest in K-12 reading, mathematics, and science, while postsecondary, equity subgroups, and mechanism-level causal tests remain comparatively underdeveloped.
