**Query:** What is the evidence for formative assessment improving student learning outcomes?

**Date/Time:** 2026-03-25 15:01:06

## Executive Summary

The overall evidence indicates that formative assessment can improve student learning, but the size and reliability of benefits depend strongly on implementation quality, subject area, and whether assessment evidence is actually used to adapt teaching. The best-supported definition across the literature is not “more testing,” but a recursive instructional process in which teachers or learners elicit evidence of understanding, interpret it, and use it to decide next steps in instruction (Black & Wiliam, 2009)[238][245]. Across K–12, the evidence is more convincing than in postsecondary education: K–12 meta-analyses show small-to-moderate average effects with substantial heterogeneity, whereas higher-education sources are dominated by reviews, conceptual papers, and implementation studies rather than robust causal tests [214][185][243][245][246].

The strongest experimental findings come from K–12 and secondary-school studies. The umbrella review of 13 meta-analyses found that formative assessment produced “trivial to large positive effects” on K–12 learning, with no negative effects identified, but certainty was low to very low [214]. In K–12 reading, the meta-analysis of 48 studies and 116,051 students found an average weighted effect of 0.19 on reading achievement, with larger effects in small studies, stronger effects when formative assessment was embedded in differentiated instruction, and better outcomes when teacher and student assessment were integrated rather than teacher-only [185]. In the Turkish meta-analysis of 32 studies and 47 effect sizes, the overall mean effect size was .72 (SE = .07, p < .05), and student-initiated formative feedback had the largest effect (d = 1.16), followed by mixed feedback (d = .83), adult-initiated feedback (d = .69), and computer-initiated feedback (d = .42) [243]. In a cluster-randomized controlled intervention in secondary physics with 29 teachers and 604 students, formative assessment training plus multiple-choice concept questions, clicker sessions, and reflective lessons improved conceptual understanding immediately and 3 months later; the frequent-testing and traditional-teaching groups did not differ, and the report gives no effect size in the extracted text (Lichtenberger et al., year not reported in snippet). In a randomized study in junior secondary basic science with 80 students, formative classroom assessment significantly improved achievement relative to control, again with no exact effect size reported in the extracted text (Matilda & Helen, year not reported in snippet).

The strongest quasi-experimental and observational findings generally corroborate the experimental pattern, while also clarifying moderators. A quasi-experimental study in primary-grade mathematics found that formative and summative assessment scores were positively related, with stronger relations in lower-performing classrooms and in first grade than kindergarten, suggesting stronger utility when teachers use evidence to adjust instruction for weaker classrooms [186]. A study of formative and summative assessment in primary mathematics found that low-performing classrooms benefited more, and that the relationship between formative and summative assessment was stronger among first graders than kindergarteners [186]. In a state-wide mathematics support program, formative assessment had positive impacts overall, but prior work suggested that higher pretest students may benefit especially in some settings, indicating that prior achievement moderates effects rather than yielding a uniform pattern (van den Ham & Heinze, 2022). Observational and review evidence also suggests that teacher-directed assessment alone is weaker than teacher-plus-student assessment, and that differentiated instruction and professional development are often bundled with stronger effects, especially in reading [185][238]. In higher education, the evidence is much less causal, but several studies suggest that formative assessment, self-assessment, and peer assessment are associated with improved motivation, self-regulation, engagement, and sometimes achievement, while direct learning evidence remains thinner [184][221][250][245].

The tiered questions are answered unevenly. Tier 1 is partially answered: the intervention is well defined in principle, and the most studied outcomes are achievement, reading and mathematics performance, self-regulation, motivation, and engagement, but retention and transfer are rarely measured directly (Black & Wiliam, 2009)[214]. Tier 2 is partially answered: baseline conditions are typically usual instruction, summative-only assessment, or loosely defined business-as-usual practice, but comparator definitions vary and are often vague [185][186][245]. Tier 3 is partly answered: common implementations include questioning, quizzes, exit tickets, self- and peer-assessment, and feedback cycles, but many studies bundle components, making causal attribution to any single ingredient difficult [193][238][244]. Tier 4 is partially answered for K–12 and still largely unresolved for postsecondary education: effects vary by sample size, subject, culture, prior achievement, and whether formative assessment is paired with differentiation or student participation, but evidence on retention, transfer, English learners, disability status, and direct K–12 versus postsecondary comparisons remains limited [185][214][321][245].

Overall confidence is moderate for the claim that formative assessment improves learning in K–12 classrooms, especially when it triggers feedback and instructional adaptation, but low for precise claims about optimal components, long-term retention/transfer, and higher-education causal effects. The single most important caveat is that the strongest benefits are associated with formative assessment as an active instructional cycle, not as routine testing or score reporting; superficial implementation, weak feedback, and failure to use results for instruction likely explain many null or inconsistent findings [238][245][214].

## Conceptualizing Formative Assessment

Across the retrieved literature, formative assessment is consistently defined as a process during instruction in which evidence of student understanding is elicited, interpreted, and used to improve teaching and learning. Black and Wiliam’s classroom-assessment framing remains central: assessment is formative when evidence about achievement is used to make better-founded next-step decisions than would otherwise have been made (Black & Wiliam, 2009)[238]. Reviews in K–12, higher education, and subject-specific contexts repeatedly stress that formative assessment is not equivalent to frequent testing or score reporting; rather, it is a feedback-and-adjustment cycle that may include questioning, short quizzes, observations, self-assessment, peer assessment, and teacher feedback [193][245][207][185].

In K–12 settings, formative assessment is often presented as a recursive loop of instruction, evidence gathering, interpretation, and instructional adaptation. In secondary-school reviews, teacher assessment, peer assessment, and self-assessment recur as the core agent roles, with feedback and follow-up action being the critical bridge from diagnosis to learning improvement [193]. In higher education, the concept is similar but often embedded in online, blended, or course-architecture discussions, with stronger emphasis on feedback literacy, self-regulation, and assessment alignment than on direct causal evidence of student achievement [245][246][250].

### Outcomes Most Commonly Studied

The most frequently measured outcomes are academic achievement and subject-specific performance, especially reading and mathematics. The K–12 reading meta-analysis focused on standardized reading achievement and reported a pooled effect of 0.19 across 48 studies and 116,051 students [185]. The Turkish meta-analysis reported learning outcomes across 32 studies and 47 effect sizes, with an overall mean effect size of .72 (SE = .07, p < .05), but the extracted text does not specify the common instruments used across all included studies [243]. In primary mathematics, multiple studies use formative-assessment data alongside summative achievement measures or classroom performance data, often using hierarchical or multilevel models to connect formative and summative outcomes [186][217].

Beyond achievement, many studies target self-regulation, motivation, engagement, anxiety, and metacognitive skills. For example, a quasi-experimental study comparing formative and summative assessment found greater improvements in motivation, lower test anxiety, and stronger self-regulation in the formative group than in the summative group, though the extracted snippet does not provide complete inferential statistics [184]. A longitudinal study of early adolescents found that formative-assessment interventions improved self-regulation and intrinsic motivation over time, with no clear effect on external regulation and no strong condition-by-time effect for self-efficacy [221]. In higher education, reviews and observational studies frequently emphasize self-regulation, engagement, and feedback literacy, but direct outcome evidence is thinner than in K–12 [245][244][246].

Retention and transfer are conspicuously undermeasured. Several sources discuss long-term learning, sustainable self-regulation, or “learning over time,” but few retrieved studies provide direct delayed-transfer or delayed-retention outcomes in a way that can be compared across contexts [221][245]. This is a major gap relative to the brief’s stated priorities.

### Comparator Conditions and the Counterfactual

The most common comparator is usual instruction or business-as-usual classroom practice, often without systematic formative assessment. In the K–12 literature, this is sometimes operationalized as traditional teaching, control classes, or no special formative-assessment training (Xuan et al., 2022; Lichtenberger et al., year not reported in snippet). In the physics RCT, the comparison conditions were frequent testing without formative-assessment training and traditional teaching, allowing the authors to show that training and instructional use of evidence mattered beyond simply using the same concept questions (Lichtenberger et al., year not reported in snippet).

Other comparators include summative-only assessment, routine feedback without instructional change, or alternative feedback systems. In the primary mathematics literature, studies compare formative and summative assessment data or examine whether formative-assessment indicators relate to summative performance, rather than using a pure no-formative-assessment control [186]. In higher education, much of the literature compares formative approaches with conventional lecture-based instruction, final exams, or standard course assessment regimes, but the counterfactual is often poorly specified and uneven across studies [245][246]. A key methodological issue is that “formative assessment” is often bundled with differentiated instruction, coaching, or feedback interventions, making the true comparator less a single condition than a package of regular instruction plus variable improvement efforts [185][238].

### How Formative Assessment Is Implemented

The literature identifies several recurring implementation forms: questioning, short quizzes, exit tickets, self-assessment, peer assessment, feedback cycles, and technology-mediated diagnostic tools. In secondary-school reviews, teachers are described as using classroom questioning, group work, periodic testing, follow-up feedback, and student self-assessment instruments as part of the formative process [193]. In science education, formative assessment practices include questioning strategies, feedback loops, peer assessment, individualized descriptive feedback, and using evidence to inform instruction [228]. In EFL and language contexts, quizzes, self-assessment, peer assessment, portfolios, strategic questioning, and descriptive feedback are common [219][252][184].

Technology expands these practices rather than replacing them. Computerized formative-assessment systems can schedule tasks, deliver feedback quickly, and support adaptive testing or progress monitoring, but the evidence base does not show that technology alone produces the benefit. In K–12 reading, digital technology involvement was not associated with significant differences in effect size [185]. In other work, the effect of computer-initiated formative feedback was smaller than student-initiated or mixed feedback, suggesting that technology is most useful as a delivery mechanism for good pedagogy rather than as a substitute for it [243].

### What Seems to Matter Most

The clearest moderator pattern is that formative assessment works best when evidence is acted on. In the K–12 reading meta-analysis, differentiated instruction added 0.13 SD, teacher-plus-student assessment was more effective than teacher-only assessment, and small-sample studies yielded larger effects than large-sample studies [185]. These findings align with the broader review literature arguing that mere collection of evidence is insufficient; teachers must interpret results and change instruction, pacing, grouping, or feedback accordingly [238][193]. Secondary-school and primary-mathematics studies similarly emphasize that teachers who use formative evidence to reteach, adjust tasks, or tailor support tend to see stronger outcomes than those who only report scores [186][217].

Feedback quality also appears to matter. Studies repeatedly distinguish specific, descriptive feedback from generic praise or scoring. In higher education and language-learning reviews, feedback that is immediate, concrete, and tied to learning targets is presented as more useful than evaluative or delayed feedback [245][219][207]. The Turkish meta-analysis provides the strongest pooled evidence in this set that feedback source matters: student-initiated formative feedback showed the largest effect (d = 1.16), mixed feedback also performed strongly (d = .83), adult-initiated feedback was smaller (d = .69), and computer-initiated feedback was smallest (d = .42) [243].

Professional development is another recurring ingredient. The K–12 reading meta-analysis reports that 94% of differentiated-instruction interventions were coupled with teacher professional development, implying that training and implementation support are often necessary to realize benefits [185]. The physics RCT likewise suggests that a short but structured teacher training can unlock the benefits of concept questions, clicker sessions, and reflective lessons (Lichtenberger et al., year not reported in snippet). In contrast, studies and reviews repeatedly caution that low fidelity, weak criteria, poor feedback literacy, and time pressure can undermine formative assessment in practice [238][245][193].

### Experimental Evidence in Detail

The umbrella review by Sortwell et al. synthesized 13 meta-analyses of K–12 formative assessment and concluded that formative assessment produced trivial to large positive effects on student learning with no negative effects identified, but evidence certainty was low to very low [214]. This is the most defensible “state of the field” conclusion for school settings.

The K–12 reading meta-analysis by Xuan et al. is the clearest subject-specific pooled estimate. It included 48 studies and 116,051 students and found a weighted average effect of 0.19 for reading achievement [185]. Moderator analyses showed that small-sample studies yielded larger effects (d = 0.33, p < .001), integrating differentiated instruction increased reading achievement by 0.13 SD, and teacher-plus-student assessment outperformed teacher-only assessment (d = -0.12, p < .001), while student-directed assessment alone was not significant (d = 0.03, p = .769) [185]. These results imply that the “formative assessment package” is more effective when students are active agents and teachers adapt instruction accordingly.

The Turkish meta-analysis by Karaman included 32 studies and 47 effect sizes and reported an overall mean effect size of .72 (SE = .07, p < .05) [243]. Its subgroup analysis found that student-initiated formative feedback had the largest effect (d = 1.16), mixed feedback was also large (d = .83), adult-initiated feedback was moderate (d = .69), and computer-initiated feedback was smaller (d = .42) [243]. Although the extracted text does not fully specify all moderator details, this pattern is strong evidence that student participation and mixed feedback structures may matter.

The cluster-randomized physics intervention by Lichtenberger et al. involved 29 teachers and 604 students at secondary school level. Ten teachers used formative assessment after training with multiple-choice concept questions, monitoring tools, clicker sessions, and reflective lessons; ten teachers used the same concept questions without formative-assessment training; and nine teachers taught traditionally. Students in the formative-assessment group outperformed the other two groups on conceptual understanding immediately after the intervention and 3 months later, while the frequent-testing and traditional-teaching groups did not differ, and better conceptual understanding did not come at the expense of quantitative problem solving (Lichtenberger et al., year not reported in snippet). The extracted snippet does not provide an effect size, but the design and timing make it especially important evidence for the claim that formative assessment works best when teachers are trained to use evidence instructionally.

The junior secondary basic science study by Matilda and Helen reported that formative classroom assessment significantly improved students’ academic performance in the experimental group relative to control, and that the difference favored post-test scores, implying growth after formative-feedback exposure (Matilda & Helen, year not reported in snippet). The extracted snippet does not give a numeric effect size, so the result should be interpreted as positive but not precisely quantifiable from the available record.

### Quasi-Experimental and Observational Evidence

The strongest quasi-experimental pattern in the retrieved set is that formative assessment is more effective when it is embedded in instruction rather than merely added as a measurement routine. In the primary-grade mathematics studies by Gezer et al., formative-assessment data were positively related to summative achievement, with stronger associations in classrooms where average formative performance was lower and, in related analyses, stronger relations in first grade than kindergarten [186]. This is consistent with the broader interpretation that weaker classrooms or earlier grades may benefit more from actionable feedback and instructional adaptation.

In the state-wide mathematics support program, formative assessment was linked to positive outcomes overall, but the excerpted evidence also suggests that higher pretest students may sometimes benefit especially, while other studies show stronger gains for low-achieving students (van den Ham & Heinze, 2022). This ambiguity matters: the literature does not yet support a single subgroup story. Some results favor lower achievers, others suggest stronger gains among higher achievers or better-prepared students, and many subgroup estimates are indirect or underpowered.

Observational studies in higher education and language learning likewise support the plausibility of benefits but do not establish causality. For example, studies of online quizzes, self-assessment, and peer assessment repeatedly report positive perceptions, stronger self-regulation, and greater engagement, but these are usually survey or descriptive findings rather than achievement experiments [250][219][184][245]. The evidence therefore corroborates the causal literature at the level of mechanism but not at the level of strong effect estimation.

### Population, Age, and Subject Variation

The evidence is much richer for K–12 than for postsecondary education. Within K–12, reading, mathematics, and science dominate. Reading has the clearest pooled estimate, mathematics has several quasi-experimental and correlational studies linking formative-assessment data to summative performance, and science has a smaller but still meaningful causal base, including the cluster-randomized physics study and the junior secondary basic-science study [185][186](Lichtenberger et al., year not reported in snippet)(Matilda & Helen, year not reported in snippet). The subject patterns are not identical: reading seems especially responsive to differentiated instruction and teacher-student co-assessment, while mathematics and science often rely more on problem-solving feedback, concept questions, and teacher reteaching [185][186][289].

Age differences are suggested but not fully resolved. In the reading meta-analysis, grade level did not produce a significant difference in effect size, even though primary-level effects were numerically largest and tertiary-level effects smallest in the pooled summaries [185]. In the feedback meta-analysis for text learning, university students showed a significant positive feedback effect while elementary and secondary students did not, suggesting that “feedback” effects are not identical to “formative assessment” effects and may vary by developmental stage and task type [348]. In primary mathematics, lower-performing classrooms and earlier grades often appear more responsive, but the evidence is still largely correlational [186][217].

Evidence for English learners and students with disabilities is notably indirect relative to the brief. The retrieved literature contains many studies on progress monitoring, diagnostic assessment, and reading interventions for ELs and students with disabilities, but few formative-assessment trials that cleanly isolate subgroup effects [321][320](Mancilla-Martinez, 2020)(Lai et al., year not reported in snippet). The strongest defensible conclusion is that subgroup-sensitive, linguistically accessible assessment is important, but the retrieved evidence does not yet identify a precise causal advantage of formative assessment for ELs or students with disabilities relative to other learners.

## Synthesis and Implications

The cross-cutting evidence supports a narrow but important conclusion: formative assessment is most effective when it is part of an instructional improvement cycle, not a standalone assessment add-on. The strongest gains appear when teachers elicit evidence, provide specific feedback, and then change pacing, grouping, or task design in response [185][243][238](Lichtenberger et al., year not reported in snippet). In practical terms, this means that schools should not measure success by the frequency of quizzes or the presence of digital tools alone. The key implementation question is whether assessment data are usable, timely, and acted on.

For practitioners, the most actionable implications are: use short-cycle assessment with explicit learning targets; pair it with descriptive feedback; ensure students participate through self- or peer-assessment when appropriate; and invest in teacher professional development so that evidence leads to reteaching or differentiation [193][185][221]. In K–12 reading, the evidence most strongly favors formative assessment embedded in differentiated instruction and teacher-student collaboration [185]. In mathematics and science, the same logic applies, but the operative forms tend to be concept questions, worked examples, formative homework, and feedback on problem solving [186](Lichtenberger et al., year not reported in snippet)[289]. In higher education, the implication is less about a proven effect size and more about designing feedback-rich, aligned assessment systems that support self-regulation and engagement while avoiding overreliance on end-of-term testing [245][246].

## Limitations and Research Gaps

The evidence base has several serious limitations. First, the strongest effect estimates come from K–12 reading and a smaller set of K–12 mathematics and science studies; postsecondary evidence is much thinner and often non-causal [245][246]. Second, retention and transfer are rarely measured directly, so claims about longer-term learning remain mostly inferential rather than demonstrated. Third, many studies bundle formative assessment with differentiation, coaching, or professional development, making it difficult to isolate which component is causally responsible for gains [185][243][238].

Equity evidence is also incomplete. There are promising but indirect indications that lower-achieving students may benefit more in some contexts, yet other studies suggest stronger effects for higher-pretest students, and subgroup analyses for English learners, disability status, and intersections of EL/disability are sparse or indirect [186][315][321][320]. The field also lacks clean comparisons against alternative comparators such as retrieval practice, low-stakes quizzing, adaptive tutoring, and feedback-only interventions. Finally, implementation fidelity remains underreported relative to outcomes; without clearer fidelity data, it is difficult to know when null findings reflect weak theory, poor delivery, or an inappropriate comparator [193][245].

## Body of Evidence Maturity: LIMITED
Justification: The evidence base is substantial for K–12, especially in reading and mathematics, and includes meta-analyses plus a few strong experimental studies. However, certainty remains low to very low in several syntheses, higher-education evidence is much less causal, and subgroup, retention, and transfer evidence is still too thin for strong generalization across ages, subjects, and learner populations.
