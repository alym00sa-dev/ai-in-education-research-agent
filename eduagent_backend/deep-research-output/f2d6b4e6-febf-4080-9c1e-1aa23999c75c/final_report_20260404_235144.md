## Executive Summary

The direct evidence base on teacher use of generative AI for instructional coaching or lesson-plan feedback is promising but still weak for the core policy question. Across the source pool, the most relevant studies show that generative AI can help teachers and preservice teachers generate drafts, surface revisions, and in some cases increase the speed or uptake of feedback, but there is still no robust body of head-to-head K-12 evidence showing that genAI coaching or lesson-plan feedback matches or exceeds established human supports such as instructional coaches, mentors, peers, or rubric-guided review. The literature is notably stronger on adjacent use cases—teacher planning support, automated feedback, tutor support, and preservice-teacher lesson design—than on authentic school-based coaching cycles for in-service K-12 teachers. Accordingly, the overall conclusion is that genAI is best supported as an assistive planning and feedback tool requiring human oversight, not as a validated substitute for instructional coaching in schools [3] [2] [31] [7] [5] [15].

The strongest experimental evidence in the pool comes mostly from adjacent interventions rather than the exact focal intervention. In a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=4,136 sessions; 782 tutors at launch), Tutor CoPilot provided real-time AI guidance to K-12 tutors and improved proximal math learning by 4 percentage points; effects were larger for lower-performing tutors, whose students gained 9 percentage points, and tutor behavior shifted strongly toward stronger pedagogical strategies, with about 2 standard deviations on a log-odds scale for some strategy categories [4]. In an RCT (n=26 prospective physics teachers; 103 tasks), ChatGPT-supported task development produced tasks comparable in correctness and difficulty to textbook-supported task creation, but textbook-supported work was better on clarity and contextualization, with reported effects d=0.38 and d=1.04 favoring textbook on clarity and context [14]. In an RCT (n=273; uptake model n=161), pre-service teachers receiving identical feedback content labeled as AI, expert, or peer showed that perceived source affected judgments of fairness, usefulness, and acceptance, but not actual behavioral uptake; LLM feedback had the highest relative uptake at 52% versus 45% for expert and 42% for peer feedback, with uptake-model R²=.078 and a feedback-quality coefficient b=1.27; implemented suggestions differed with η²=.11 [16]. In an RCT (n=436), teacher AI training increased reported work-related AI use, including lesson/material creation and assessment support, with effects d=1.07, d=1.32, and d=0.67, and increased perceived preparedness to integrate AI with effects d=0.49 and d=1.65 [10]. Two meta-analyses also provide indirect causal context: a meta-analysis of AI-enabled personalized STEM education in schools found pooled positive effects, but it is about student-facing AI rather than teacher coaching [1]; a meta-analysis of ChatGPT on student learning also found overall positive effects on student outcomes, again indirect for the present question [8].

Quasi-experimental and observational evidence partially corroborates the idea that AI feedback can improve revision processes, but it does not resolve the central comparison with human coaching. A Quasi-Experimental Design (QED) study on AI-assisted writing feedback in secondary EFL reported better writing performance than traditional teacher feedback alone, suggesting that AI-generated formative feedback can be actionable in classroom revision settings, but the intervention targets students rather than teachers and therefore only indirectly informs lesson-plan feedback workflows [12]. A QED study of AI-supported lesson planning in vocational teacher education is highly relevant because it tests an analytical AI platform for feedback on lesson plans over time, but the population is graduate vocational teacher education rather than in-service K-12 teachers, so its relevance is partial rather than direct [22]. Mixed-methods studies in K-12 and preservice contexts consistently report that teachers use genAI to brainstorm objectives, differentiate tasks, draft lesson sequences, generate rubrics, and revise plans, while also reporting hallucinations, shallow alignment, and the need for verification against standards and curriculum materials [26] [39] [34] [42] [41] [44] [21].

Against the tiered research questions, Tier 1 is answered partially: the literature clearly defines the intervention as use of large language models or chatbots for text generation, revision, critique, rubric production, and dialogic planning support, and the most studied outcomes are perceived usefulness, planning efficiency, artifact quality, and feedback uptake rather than downstream teaching or student learning [3] [2] [31] [9]. Tier 2 is answered only partially: studies use heterogeneous counterfactuals including textbook support, human teacher feedback, peer feedback, perceived-source manipulations, training versus no training, and usual tool use, but there is almost no authentic K-12 comparison against instructional coaches or school-based mentoring structures [14] [16] [10] [26]. Tier 3 is answered moderately well by descriptive evidence: effective implementation repeatedly appears to depend on specific prompting, rubric or standards alignment, human review, and iterative rather than one-shot use [3] [7] [11] [18] [23]. Tier 4 remains unresolved for the focal question: there is not enough direct comparative evidence to conclude that genAI-assisted coaching improves lesson-plan quality, planning efficiency, or instructional judgment more than human coaching or other school-based supports for K-12 teachers [31] [6] [32].

Overall confidence is moderate for claims that genAI can support drafting, revision, and workflow efficiency, but low for claims that it improves instructional coaching outcomes relative to established human supports in K-12 schools. The single most important caveat is that the strongest evidence is indirect: much of it comes from tutor-support systems, student-feedback settings, higher education, or preservice teacher samples rather than practicing K-12 teachers engaged in real lesson-planning and coaching cycles [4] [2] [22] [26] [39].

## Defining the intervention and the evidence base

The focal intervention is best defined narrowly as teacher-facing use of generative AI, usually large language models or chatbot interfaces, to generate feedback on lesson plans, suggest revisions, simulate coaching dialogue, produce rubrics, or help a teacher reflect on instructional decisions before teaching. Reviews of teacher-facing LLM use consistently describe these capabilities as planning support, task automation, content generation, and feedback assistance rather than fully autonomous pedagogical judgment [3] [31] [9]. State-of-the-field and systematic reviews likewise frame ChatGPT and similar systems as tools for ideation, drafting, adaptation, and formative feedback, but emphasize that the evidence is early and heterogeneous [7] [5] [11].

The most relevant outcomes in this literature are not uniform. Reviews of automated feedback and LLM planning support show that studies most often examine timeliness, personalization, revision support, artifact quality, user satisfaction, and perceived workload rather than classroom enactment or student learning [2] [3]. Broader teacher-AI reviews similarly report that the literature is dominated by feasibility, perceptions, and use cases, with relatively few rigorous outcome studies centered on teachers’ instructional improvement [31] [33]. This mismatch matters for the present brief because improved drafting efficiency or positive perceptions do not establish improved coaching quality, better pedagogical reasoning, or safer instructional decisions.

The studied populations are also uneven. Preservice teachers and higher-education instructor contexts are heavily represented in lesson-planning and feedback studies [26] [39] [9]. K-12 in-service teachers appear in some mixed-methods and perception work, including rubric generation and lesson-plan customization in low-resource schools, but rigorous comparative studies for practicing K-12 teachers remain scarce [21] [34]. Reviews of adoption factors in K-12 show that institutional support, policy clarity, and teacher confidence influence uptake, but these studies are about adoption rather than effectiveness of coaching or feedback [6].

## What non-AI baselines and counterfactuals look like

Without genAI, lesson-plan feedback and instructional coaching are typically provided by human mentors, supervisors, peers, or self-review with planning templates and rubrics. The broader teacher professional development literature does not address genAI directly, but it shows that effective support structures are often sustained, practice-connected, and socially mediated, rather than one-off feedback exchanges [29]. That matters because many genAI studies compare against much weaker counterfactuals, such as no extra support, textbook support, or general tool use, instead of full coaching models.

The actual counterfactuals in the relevant genAI studies are heterogeneous. In the physics lesson-design RCT, the comparison was textbook-supported task creation rather than mentor or coach feedback [14]. In the pre-service feedback-source experiment, the feedback content was held constant while only the labeled source changed among AI, expert, and peer, making the counterfactual one of attribution and trust rather than substantive differences in feedback quality [16]. In the teacher AI training RCT, the counterfactual was not coaching support but a comparison condition without the same structured training, so the causal claim concerns readiness and reported AI use rather than lesson-plan quality [10]. Mixed-methods lesson-planning studies often compare teacher experiences with and without chatbot assistance, but not against school-based human coaching systems [26] [39] [42].

Because the field lacks strong K-12 head-to-head designs, adjacent literatures are often used as indirect baselines. Reviews of automated feedback in higher education show that genAI is often compared with instructor feedback, peer feedback, or rubric-guided feedback for writing and assignments, suggesting plausible school analogues, but these are not direct studies of teacher lesson planning [2]. Reviews of chatbots, AI in mathematics education, and general AI in teaching similarly show that comparison conditions range from usual instruction to non-generative digital tools, again offering contextual rather than direct baseline evidence for teacher coaching questions [37] [18] [23].

## Experimental evidence most relevant to teacher coaching and lesson-plan feedback

A Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=26 prospective physics teachers; 103 tasks) tested whether ChatGPT 3.5 could support prospective physics teachers in developing assessment tasks compared with textbook-supported task creation [14]. The intervention was ChatGPT-assisted task development, and the comparison condition was conventional textbook support. The study found no significant difference in correctness on the produced tasks and reported that ChatGPT-generated tasks were similarly difficult, but textbook-supported task creation produced stronger clarity and context, with d=0.38 and d=1.04 favoring the textbook condition on those dimensions [14]. This is one of the clearest causal studies tied to teacher planning artifacts, but its sample is small, its population is preservice rather than in-service K-12 teachers, and its outcome is assessment-task quality rather than lesson-plan feedback.

A Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=273 for perception analyses; n=161 for uptake model) examined whether the perceived source of feedback affected pre-service teachers’ judgments and uptake when feedback content was held constant [16]. Participants evaluated feedback on a mathematics learning goal under AI, expert, or peer labels. Perceived source strongly shaped fairness, usefulness, and acceptance judgments, but not actual behavioral uptake [16]. LLM feedback had the highest relative uptake at 52%, compared with 45% for expert feedback and 42% for peer feedback; the uptake model reported R²=.078, the feedback quality coefficient was b=1.27, and implemented suggestions differed with η²=.11 [16]. This study is highly relevant to trust and uptake, but it does not compare naturally generated AI feedback with naturally generated human coaching in authentic K-12 settings.

A Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=436) evaluated structured AI professional development for teachers rather than AI coaching itself [10]. The intervention improved reported AI use for work-related tasks, including lesson/material creation and assessment support, with d=1.07, d=1.32, and d=0.67 [10]. It also increased willingness and perceived preparedness to integrate AI, with d=0.49 and d=1.65, while shifting attitudes toward a more critical but also more optimistic stance regarding AI in schools, with d=0.42, d=0.25, and d=0.50 [10]. This is useful evidence on implementation readiness because it shows teachers can be trained to use AI more actively and critically, but it is not direct evidence that AI feedback improves planning or coaching quality.

A Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=4,136 sessions; 782 tutors at launch) tested Tutor CoPilot, a human-AI system that gives real-time guidance to tutors during live K-12 math tutoring [4]. The intervention improved proximal math learning outcomes by 4 percentage points and produced larger gains for lower-performing tutors, whose students improved by 9 percentage points [4]. The system also changed tutor instructional behavior, making treatment tutors about 2 standard deviations more likely to use stronger strategy categories on a log-odds scale [4]. Although tutoring is not the same as teacher lesson-plan coaching, this is the strongest experimental evidence in the pool that generative AI can augment novice educators’ real-time pedagogical moves rather than merely automate drafting. It therefore provides indirect but important evidence that AI can function as an assistive pedagogical support for less experienced educators.

Another Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=165 students) evaluated LearnLM tutoring in UK secondary classrooms [20]. Supervised LearnLM performed at least as well as human tutoring on immediate math learning and was better on next-unit transfer by 5.5 percentage points, with a 95% CI of [-1.4%, +12.4%] [20]. Tutors qualitatively reported that the system made tutoring more fluid and efficient when supporting multiple students [20]. This study is also adjacent rather than direct, but it reinforces that supervised AI can augment instructional support workflows when human adults remain in the loop.

Two meta-analyses provide broader but indirect context. A meta-analysis of AI-enabled personalized STEM education in schools reported pooled positive effects of AI-supported personalized learning in K-12, though the summary does not provide the exact pooled effect statistic in the source pool and the intervention is student-facing rather than teacher coaching [1]. A meta-analysis on ChatGPT and student learning performance likewise reports overall positive effects on learning outcomes and perceptions under some conditions, but it is not about teachers receiving lesson-plan feedback [8]. These syntheses cannot be used as direct evidence for teacher coaching efficacy, but they indicate that generative and AI-enabled educational supports can produce measurable benefits under some designs.

## Quasi-experimental evidence and adjacent intervention studies

A Quasi-Experimental Design (QED) study investigated AI-assisted writing feedback for secondary EFL students and reported improved writing performance relative to traditional teacher feedback alone [12]. Because the source pool does not report the exact sample size or effect statistic in the summary, those figures are not reported here. The study is not about teachers or lesson planning, but it does offer relevant process evidence: AI-generated feedback can sometimes outperform a standard human-only feedback workflow on revision-oriented tasks. This supports the plausibility, though not the proof, that AI feedback may be useful when feedback demands exceed human capacity.

A Quasi-Experimental Design (QED) study examined AI-supported lesson planning in vocational teacher education through a longitudinal intervention using an analytical AI platform [22]. This is one of the closest studies to the focal intervention because it addresses whether AI feedback can improve lesson planning over time. However, the source summary in the pool does not provide the exact sample size or effect statistic, so these are not reported. Its relevance is still limited by population: graduate vocational teacher education is not the same as in-service K-12 teachers receiving school-based coaching.

Other QED evidence in adjacent learning domains suggests that generative AI effects vary by task and may involve trade-offs. A QED study on AI code generators for novice programming learners raised concerns that immediate task support may come with over-reliance risks [24]. A QED study on generative-AI-supported programming education examined programming output, transfer, self-efficacy, and process measures in undergraduates, again showing that genAI can affect process and performance but in a context far removed from teacher coaching [35]. These studies matter because they complicate simple efficiency narratives: support tools may improve immediate output while also changing underlying cognition and dependence patterns.

## Mixed-methods and observational evidence on teacher planning workflows

Mixed-methods studies provide the richest direct picture of how teachers are actually using genAI for planning. A mixed-methods thesis on K-12 practicum-experienced teaching graduate students found that AI chatbots such as ChatGPT and Magic School were used to generate lesson ideas, draft activities, differentiate content, and accelerate planning, but users also reported concerns about accuracy, overgeneralization, and ethical use [26]. Because the study is descriptive rather than causal, it can support claims about workflows and perceptions, not effectiveness.

A mixed-methods study on pre-service elementary science teachers’ lesson planning found that ChatGPT was used to support science lesson design, but teachers still needed to evaluate pedagogical appropriateness and likely classroom fit [39]. This aligns with another mixed-methods study arguing that LLM-supported teaching simulation may help teachers anticipate misconceptions and refine plans before instruction [41]. Both studies suggest a mechanism of value through rehearsal, anticipation, and iterative refinement, though neither provides strong comparative outcome evidence.

A mixed-methods study of teacher-AI collaboration in low-resource schools, Shiksha Copilot, is especially important for implementation context [34]. It examines how teachers use an AI system to curate and customize lesson plans in multilingual, under-resourced settings. The study suggests that genAI can support adaptation and curation where planning resources are limited, but also underscores the need for teacher review and contextual editing [34]. This provides evidence that low-resource contexts may especially value planning support, though it does not establish comparative effectiveness versus human coaching.

Other mixed-methods studies converge on similar patterns. Tailoring Education with GenAI reports that educators can customize lesson plans more efficiently and perceive the tool as useful, but the evidence is primarily workflow- and perception-based [42]. AI as a Teaching Partner documents classroom co-design with secondary teachers and emphasizes augmentation rather than replacement, highlighting the interaction design challenge of making AI suggestions usable within existing teacher routines [43]. A comparative mixed-methods study on educator preferences between genAI and human-created lesson plans examines how educators choose between outputs and what dimensions matter in those choices, indicating that plan acceptance depends on qualities beyond mere completeness, such as pedagogical fit and teacher control [44]. A mixed-methods study on AI-generated rubric interfaces similarly shows that teachers value efficiency gains in rubric creation but want to preserve control over criteria and feedback interpretation [21].

Observational and review evidence also suggests that teachers’ use of AI is shaped by institutional and psychological factors. Reviews of teacher adoption in K-12 identify perceived usefulness, ease of use, infrastructure, training, and ethical concerns as major determinants of uptake [6]. Broader reviews on AI for teachers argue that teacher roles shift toward orchestration, verification, and pedagogical judgment rather than disappearing [31]. Reviews of AI in higher education likewise find that educators are often underrepresented in AI research and that many tools are evaluated without sufficient attention to instructional practice [33].

## Mechanisms and implementation features

Across the reviews and mixed-methods studies, several implementation features recur. First, prompt specificity matters. Systematic reviews of ChatGPT in education and teacher-facing LLM uses repeatedly note that outputs are more useful when teachers provide detailed prompts tied to learning goals, standards, student characteristics, or constraints [5] [3]. Second, rubric or standards grounding matters. Reviews of automated feedback and mathematics education applications emphasize that unguided outputs tend to be generic, whereas grounded prompts or structured interfaces increase alignment and actionability [2] [18] [23]. Third, human-in-the-loop verification is consistently recommended because hallucinations, incorrect pedagogical claims, and weak contextual fit remain common concerns [7] [11] [32].

The proposed mechanisms for improvement are also consistent across sources. LLMs may reduce planning time by automating first drafts and routine text production [3] [9]. They may increase feedback frequency because teachers can iterate without waiting for scheduled coaching or mentor review [2]. They may broaden idea generation by offering alternative activities, explanations, and differentiation options [26] [42]. They may support reflective practice by acting as a “reflective partner” that prompts reconsideration of goals, sequencing, or deep learning opportunities [15]. Yet these mechanisms remain largely inferential because very few studies isolate them experimentally.

Some implementation evidence also points to heterogeneity by expertise. Tutor CoPilot found larger gains for lower-performing tutors, suggesting AI guidance may be most useful where baseline pedagogical expertise is weaker [4]. The field experiment in Kenya, though outside education, similarly found heterogeneous effects of GPT-4 assistance: the overall treatment effect on standardized business performance was 0.04 sd, but effects were -0.08 sd for low performers and 0.16 sd for high performers, with heterogeneity of 0.23 sd [28]. This non-educational result cannot be generalized directly to teachers, but it is a caution that genAI support may not benefit all users equally and may sometimes widen performance gaps depending on how the tool interacts with prior expertise.

## What is known about effectiveness on lesson quality, feedback quality, and planning efficiency

Direct evidence on lesson-plan quality remains thin. The best causal analogue from preservice teacher task design shows that ChatGPT can produce acceptable work but not clearly superior work; textbook support performed better on clarity and context even when correctness and difficulty were similar [14]. The feedback-source experiment suggests that AI-labeled feedback can be taken up at least as much as human-labeled feedback, but that result concerns uptake of fixed feedback content, not comparative quality of naturally produced AI feedback [16]. The vocational teacher education QED is directly relevant in concept, but the available summary does not report the quantitative outcome needed to judge magnitude [22].

Evidence on planning efficiency is somewhat stronger than evidence on instructional quality. Reviews of LLM-based task automation consistently report time-saving as one of the most common teacher-facing affordances [3]. Reviews of university educators’ ChatGPT use similarly emphasize faster drafting of materials and feedback [9]. Mixed-methods teacher-planning studies repeatedly report that teachers perceive lesson drafting and adaptation as faster with AI [26] [42] [34]. However, the literature does not yet establish whether saved time remains saved after verification, editing, and correction costs are included.

Evidence on feedback usefulness and specificity is mixed. Automated feedback reviews in higher education suggest genAI can produce timely, personalized comments, but feedback quality depends heavily on prompt design and domain constraints [2]. The pre-service teacher perception experiment found that perceptions of usefulness are sensitive to whether feedback is believed to come from AI, expert, or peer sources [16]. Mixed-methods studies on lesson planning indicate that AI feedback can be useful for brainstorming or structure but may remain too generic, insufficiently contextualized, or weakly aligned to local curriculum unless teachers actively steer it [39] [41] [44].

Critically, there is almost no direct evidence on downstream instructional practice or student outcomes from teacher-facing genAI lesson-plan feedback. The tutor-support RCTs show that AI can improve educator actions and student outcomes in tutoring contexts [4] [20]. But lesson-planning studies usually stop at plan quality, revisions, preferences, or perceptions [26] [39] [34]. This is the largest evidentiary gap for school decision-making.

## Variation across populations, settings, and subjects

Preservice teachers are overrepresented. Physics task development, science lesson planning, and feedback-source studies all use teacher candidates rather than practicing school teachers [14] [39] [16]. These studies are useful because they isolate planning tasks, but they do not capture the constraints of in-service practice such as pacing guides, school accountability, classroom management, district curriculum, or collaborative planning routines.

In-service K-12 evidence exists but is mostly descriptive. The K-12 lesson-planning thesis, Shiksha Copilot, and classroom codesign work with secondary teachers all provide authentic practice context, including diverse student needs and low-resource conditions [26] [34] [43]. Yet these studies do not provide rigorous comparative estimates against human coaching, peer collaboration, or usual planning routines. Evidence for elementary settings appears in pre-service science planning and some broader reviews, but middle and high school coaching-specific evidence remains sparse [39] [3].

Subject coverage is uneven. Science, mathematics, writing, and programming dominate the pool [14] [18] [12] [24]. Reviews of EFL writing and critical thinking show substantial interest in AI-supported feedback and revision in language education, but these findings transfer only indirectly to lesson-plan feedback for teachers [27] [30]. There is comparatively little direct evidence for humanities, social studies, arts, special education, or culturally sustaining pedagogy.

Low-resource and multilingual contexts are visible but underdeveloped analytically. Shiksha Copilot is one of the few studies that centers under-resourced schools and customization needs [34]. Reviews of AI ethics in primary and secondary education and K-12 adoption factors imply that equity, access, and context are major concerns, but analytic subgroup evidence remains limited [32] [6].

## Risks, limitations, and safety concerns in the intervention itself

Safety and instructionally sound use are recurrent concerns across nearly all reviews. State-of-the-field syntheses emphasize hallucinations, bias, inaccuracies, opacity, privacy, and overreliance as central risks of ChatGPT-like tools in education [7] [11]. Reviews specifically on teacher-facing AI stress that systems can produce plausible but pedagogically weak or contextually inappropriate recommendations, requiring professional judgment rather than passive acceptance [31] [15].

Ethics reviews focused on primary and secondary education consolidate concerns around data governance, fairness, accountability, and the risk that AI systems reproduce inequities or encode biased assumptions into pedagogical advice [32]. Reviews of mathematics and EFL applications also note the danger of superficial correctness: outputs may appear polished while lacking conceptual depth, disciplinary fit, or adequate explanation [18] [27]. For teacher coaching and lesson-plan feedback, this matters because poor advice may not be obviously wrong to novice users.

Overreliance is another unresolved risk. Adjacent QED evidence on AI code generators suggests that tools can improve immediate task completion while potentially weakening independent problem solving [24]. Reviews of self-regulated learning and critical thinking similarly raise the possibility that generative AI can support planning and reflection when used critically, but may also reduce independent cognitive effort when used passively [17] [30]. The current literature does not yet show whether repeated use of AI feedback helps teachers internalize stronger planning habits or instead creates dependence on external generation.

## Synthesis and Implications

The clearest synthesis is that teacher-facing genAI currently has stronger evidence as a workflow support than as a validated instructional coach. Across reviews and mixed-methods studies, teachers use genAI to draft, adapt, and critique plans; generate objectives, questions, and rubrics; and iterate more quickly than in manual workflows [3] [26] [34]. Across the few experiments, AI can support educator performance in adjacent settings such as tutoring and task design, and AI-labeled feedback can be taken up at rates comparable to or higher than human-labeled feedback in preservice contexts [4] [14] [16]. But the literature does not yet demonstrate that genAI lesson-plan feedback improves instructional quality more than existing human feedback structures in K-12 schools.

For practice, the most defensible implication is augmentation rather than substitution. Schools considering these tools should treat them as first-pass drafting and reflective supports, not autonomous coaching systems [31] [43]. High-quality implementation is likely to require explicit standards or rubric grounding, careful prompting, and teacher or coach verification before use in classrooms [3] [2] [7]. Professional development appears important because structured training can increase teachers’ use and preparedness while also strengthening critical awareness of risks [10]. Low-resource contexts may particularly benefit from planning support where human coaching capacity is thin, but those settings may also face higher stakes from inaccurate or culturally mismatched outputs [34] [32].

For researchers and system leaders, the main practical implication is that procurement or scaling decisions should not assume equivalence to instructional coaching. Existing evidence supports experimentation with guardrailed, human-supervised use for drafting, brainstorming, rubric generation, and iterative revision, especially where feedback bottlenecks are severe [21] [42]. It does not support replacing coaches, mentors, or collaborative planning structures. The most useful near-term models are likely hybrid ones in which AI generates suggestions that teachers or coaches then filter, contextualize, and justify.

## Limitations and Research Gaps

The central limitation is lack of direct causal evidence for the focal intervention in authentic K-12 settings. There is no strong set of RCTs or QEDs comparing genAI-assisted lesson-plan feedback with human instructional coaches, mentor feedback, peer review, rubric-based self-review, or usual planning routines for in-service K-12 teachers [3] [31]. Much of the most relevant evidence comes from preservice teachers, higher education, tutoring, or student-facing feedback studies [2] [14] [16] [4].

Methodological weaknesses also include small samples, short intervention windows, and descriptive designs. The preservice teacher RCT on physics task development had only 26 participants [14]. Many lesson-planning studies are mixed-methods or qualitative, limiting causal inference [26] [39] [41]. Reviews of the field repeatedly note the novelty of the evidence base and the overrepresentation of exploratory studies published soon after ChatGPT’s release [5] [7].

Population coverage is narrow. In-service teachers, experienced teachers, instructional coaches, special educators, and district-level support staff are underrepresented [6] [31]. Grade-band evidence is uneven, and subject-area coverage is concentrated in science, mathematics, EFL writing, and programming [18] [27] [23]. Equity-relevant populations such as teachers in multilingual, rural, or low-resource schools appear in only a few studies, usually without subgroup effect estimates [34] [32].

Outcome measurement is also limited. Most studies measure perceptions, artifact quality, or self-reported efficiency rather than enacted teaching, teacher learning over time, or student outcomes [3] [9]. Safety evidence is fragmented: hallucinations, bias, and generic advice are widely discussed, but there is little systematic evidence on their frequency, severity, or detection rates in teacher coaching workflows [7] [11]. Future research should prioritize head-to-head K-12 trials with authentic comparators, separate measurement of lesson quality and feedback quality, verified planning-time costs including checking and correction, and downstream measures of classroom enactment and student learning.
