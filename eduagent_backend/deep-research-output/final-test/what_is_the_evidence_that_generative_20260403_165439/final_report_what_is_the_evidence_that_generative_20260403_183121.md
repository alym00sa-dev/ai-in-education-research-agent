## Executive Summary

The direct evidence that generative AI improves K–12 reading and literacy outcomes is promising in a few narrow use cases but not yet strong enough to support a broad claim of effectiveness across literacy domains. The clearest K–12 evidence is concentrated in secondary writing-feedback and vocabulary settings, most often in EFL/ESL contexts rather than mainstream English/language arts classrooms. Across those studies, generative AI is usually implemented as a chatbot or feedback assistant that provides immediate corrective feedback, vocabulary practice, or homework support, and it is typically compared with business-as-usual instruction, paper-based practice, or teacher feedback rather than with strong non-AI digital writing tools or peer review systems. By contrast, direct K–12 evidence on reading comprehension, summarization, argumentation from sources, disciplinary literacy, and broader literacy achievement is sparse, and evidence on foundational reading skills is effectively absent. Broader reviews confirm that the school-based generative-AI evidence base is expanding rapidly but remains methodologically immature and unevenly distributed across grades, subjects, and populations [34] [8] [44] [6].

The strongest experimental K–12 finding in the pool is an RCT of GPT-4 as an English homework tutor in secondary school. In that study, GPT-4-supported homework tutoring for high-school English learning produced an overall learning gain of d=0.251 on an externally measured 24-item pre/post test, with heterogeneity by year level: d=0.603 in 3rd-year classes and d=-0.004 in 5th-year classes (RCT, n=75) [4]. The same study found higher homework interestingness (d=0.593), perceived resource availability (d=0.586), usefulness (d=0.356), and comprehensiveness (d=0.281) for the GPT-4 condition [4]. The broadest synthesis directly on ChatGPT across education reports positive average effects on learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking, but it is not specific to K–12 literacy and therefore only partially answers the focal brief (meta-analysis) [6]. Likewise, broader systematic reviews indicate that generative AI and AI tutoring systems can support personalization, feedback, and engagement, but they do not isolate K–12 reading/literacy effects [15] [34] [7].

The strongest direct K–12 quasi-experimental evidence also clusters around writing and vocabulary. A high-school EFL quasi-experiment found that ChatGPT-based mobile feedback reduced ESL writing errors more than traditional teacher feedback, with a reported partial eta squared of .860 for the main effect of error category, though no direct standardized between-group effect size was reported (QED, n=132) [33]. Another high-school quasi-experiment reported that a ChatGPT-based vocabulary application outperformed a traditional paper-based condition on a 54-item vocabulary post-test among Saudi secondary students, though the exact standardized effect was not reported (QED, n=57) [47]. A newer secondary study on AI-assisted writing feedback versus traditional teacher feedback is highly relevant but under-extracted in the source pool, so it cannot bear strong quantitative weight here [27]. These positive findings are tempered by adjacent causal evidence showing that generative AI can improve revision performance while also encouraging heavier reliance on the tool and fewer metacognitive processes in postsecondary writing tasks (RCT, n=117) [24], and by pilot evidence that AI reliance can reduce reading comprehension accuracy when participants outsource processing to the model (RCT, n=32) [21].

Across the tiered research questions, Tier 1 is only partially answered: the intervention is definable as student- or teacher-facing large-language-model tools used for text generation, tutoring, feedback, revision, vocabulary support, or reading-response assistance, but the literature often blurs generative AI with broader AI, tutoring systems, and automated feedback [34] [44] [7]. Tier 2 is partially answered: the most common counterfactuals are business-as-usual literacy instruction, teacher feedback, paper-based vocabulary study, and conventional homework rather than strong head-to-head comparisons with peer review, automated writing evaluation, or human tutoring [4] [33] [47] [8]. Tier 3 is partially answered: implementation appears to matter substantially, with effects varying by degree of teacher structuring, prompt design, autonomy, and learner prior attainment, but literacy-specific classroom-process evidence is still thin [4] [14] [28] [36]. Tier 4 is only partially answered: there is some evidence of positive effects on narrow literacy outcomes, especially feedback-supported writing and vocabulary, but too little rigorous K–12 evidence on reading comprehension, broader writing quality, transfer, or subgroup differences to conclude that generative AI broadly improves K–12 literacy achievement.

Overall confidence is moderate for the narrow claim that generative AI can improve some secondary literacy-related outcomes, especially vocabulary learning, writing error reduction, and structured homework support, and low for the broader claim that it improves K–12 reading and literacy achievement in general. The single most important caveat is that the direct K–12 evidence base is small, heavily concentrated in secondary EFL/ESL contexts, and dominated by short-term studies with proximal outcomes rather than independent, durable, classroom-representative literacy measures [6] [34] [44].

## Defining the intervention and the current scope of the field

In this literature, “generative AI” most often refers to large-language-model systems that generate or transform text in response to prompts, including chatbots such as ChatGPT and GPT-4, writing-feedback assistants, question-answering tutors, vocabulary-practice tools, and systems that generate explanations or model responses [34] [20] [44]. Reviews also note that the boundary between generative AI and broader AI-in-education remains unstable, with many papers blending LLMs with older categories such as intelligent tutoring systems, adaptive systems, automated feedback, and conversational agents [7] [34] [8]. For this brief, the most relevant interventions are student- or teacher-facing text-generating tools used to support literacy tasks such as writing feedback, revision, vocabulary practice, summarization, reading-response activities, and homework tutoring [4] [33] [47].

The school-based literature is concentrated in secondary grades, especially high school, and in language-learning settings rather than general education ELA. The available direct K–12 studies in the source pool focus mainly on high-school English learning, EFL/ESL writing, and vocabulary [4] [33] [47] [27]. Reviews of generative AI in education similarly report strong concentration in higher education and comparatively sparse K–12 evidence, especially for younger students [34] [44] [7] [37]. Elementary and middle-school evidence exists more often in adjacent AI-literacy, writing-support, or mixed-method studies than in rigorous literacy-outcome trials [28] [36] [35].

The most frequently studied outcomes are not broad literacy achievement measures but narrower, proximal outcomes. In K–12 studies these include multiple-choice English-learning test scores, vocabulary post-tests, error reduction in second-language writing, and self-reported engagement or attitudes [4] [33] [47]. Across the wider educational literature, common outcomes also include writing quality, revision behavior, learning perception, and higher-order thinking, but these are often measured in postsecondary contexts and cannot be assumed to generalize directly to K–12 classrooms [6] [24] [9]. Foundational reading outcomes such as decoding, phonics, oral reading fluency, and early reading comprehension are largely missing from the direct generative-AI evidence in this pool. That absence is especially notable because strong non-AI literacy interventions in elementary grades often target exactly those outcomes or integrate reading-to-write in structured ways [1].

## What studies use as the counterfactual

The counterfactuals in the direct K–12 generative-AI literacy studies are usually weak-to-moderate comparators rather than the strongest plausible alternatives. In the high-school GPT-4 homework-tutor RCT, the comparison was ordinary homework without the GPT-4 tutor, not teacher conferencing, human tutoring, or a non-generative tutoring system [4]. In the high-school ChatGPT mobile-feedback quasi-experiment, the comparator was traditional teacher feedback on ESL writing [33]. In the Saudi vocabulary study, the comparator was a traditional paper-based learning condition [47]. These are relevant baselines, but they do not answer whether generative AI outperforms strong peer review, automated writing evaluation, rule-based grammar tools, or intensive human tutoring.

This comparator weakness matters because K–12 literacy outcomes are commonly developed through established non-AI pedagogies such as writing workshop, explicit strategy instruction, teacher-led feedback, peer discussion, and genre-based writing instruction. For example, a non-AI QED on college-admission essay instruction for underserved high-school students found that explicit genre-based instruction improved essay performance relative to business-as-usual, with R²=0.46 for essay outcomes and R²=0.42 for writing self-efficacy [18]. In elementary grades, a non-AI RCT of SRSD Plus for grades 1–2 found very large gains in writing quality (g=1.02), planning (g=1.40), structural elements (g=0.29), use of source text (g=0.48), number of words (g=0.82 to 1.39), and transition words (g=1.33 to 2.46) relative to business-as-usual writers workshop (RCT, n=93 randomized; posttest n=90) [1]. These studies are not generative AI studies, but they provide an important benchmark: existing human-led literacy instruction can produce large effects on writing-related outcomes, so GenAI should be judged against more than low-intensity business-as-usual conditions.

Broader reviews of AI for teachers and tutoring systems reinforce the need for careful comparator choice. Teacher-focused AI reviews indicate that many studies prioritize efficiency and automation but under-specify pedagogical comparison conditions [8]. Reviews of tutoring systems describe long-standing human-versus-machine and adaptive-versus-static comparison problems, including the fact that many AI systems appear beneficial mainly when compared to limited support rather than to well-implemented instruction [15] [16]. This is directly relevant to generative AI for literacy: a chatbot that beats paper worksheets does not necessarily beat expert teacher conferencing or structured peer response.

## Direct experimental and quasi-experimental evidence in K–12 literacy

### High-school English-learning homework tutoring

Design: Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT). The study “GPT-4 as a Homework Tutor can Improve Student Engagement and Learning Outcomes” tested GPT-4 as an interactive tutor for high-school English homework in a real school setting (n=75 total; control n=39, treatment n=36) [4]. The intervention used normal homework materials seeded into GPT-4 so students could receive individualized support during homework rather than only static assignments [4]. The primary outcome was an externally measured 24-item multiple-choice pre/post test of English homework content, focused especially on grammar-syntax material [4]. The reported result was an overall effect of d=0.251, with strong heterogeneity by class level: d=0.603 in 3rd-year classes and d=-0.004 in 5th-year classes [4]. On engagement-related measures, the GPT-4 group rated the homework as more interesting (d=0.593), better resourced (d=0.586), more useful (d=0.356), and more comprehensive (d=0.281) [4]. This is the strongest direct causal K–12 evidence in the pool, but it is a homework-tutoring study in English learning rather than a classroom trial of broad reading or writing instruction.

### High-school ESL writing feedback on mobile

Design: Quasi-Experimental Design (QED). “The Impact of ChatGPT on English Language Learners’ Writing Skills: An Assessment of AI Feedback on Mobile” compared ChatGPT mobile feedback with traditional teacher feedback in an Indian senior secondary public school (n=132; ChatGPT group n=66) [33]. The intervention centered on AI-generated feedback for correcting common ESL writing errors, delivered through mobile access [33]. The reported finding is that students receiving ChatGPT feedback showed significantly greater reduction in writing errors than those receiving traditional teacher feedback, with partial eta squared=.860 reported for the main effect of error category, although no direct between-group standardized effect size was reported [33]. The study also reported positive student attitudes toward using the tool for English grammar learning [33]. This supports a narrow claim that generative AI can assist surface-level writing correction in secondary EFL/ESL contexts, but it does not establish broader improvements in writing quality, argumentation, or independent transfer.

### High-school EFL vocabulary learning

Design: Quasi-Experimental Design (QED). “The Impact of Using a ChatGPT-based Application to Enhance Saudi Students’ EFL Vocabulary Learning” compared a ChatGPT-based vocabulary application with a traditional paper-based condition among Saudi high-school students (n=57) [47]. The intervention targeted vocabulary acquisition through a chatbot-like application, and the outcome was a 54-item vocabulary multiple-choice post-test [47]. The study found that the ChatGPT-based app outperformed the traditional paper-based condition, but the exact effect size was not reported in the source pool [47]. Students in the intervention group also reported positive attitudes toward the app [47]. This is useful evidence that GenAI may support vocabulary learning in secondary EFL contexts, but the lack of an extractable effect size and the paper-based comparator limit precision.

### Secondary AI-assisted writing feedback versus teacher feedback

Design: Quasi-Experimental Design (QED). “The role of AI assisted writing feedback in developing secondary students writing skills” is directly relevant because it compares AI-assisted writing feedback with traditional teacher feedback among secondary EFL students [27]. However, the source pool does not provide extractable sample details, outcome statistics, or exact effect sizes. It can therefore only be used as evidence that this line of comparative research exists, not as firm quantitative support for effectiveness claims [27].

## What is missing in direct K–12 literacy evidence

The strongest gap is reading. No study in the available pool provides rigorous K–12 causal evidence that generative AI improves reading comprehension in ordinary classroom ELA instruction relative to business-as-usual, peer discussion, teacher-led comprehension instruction, or established digital reading supports. The existing K–12 generative-AI studies instead focus on English-learning homework, vocabulary, and writing correction [4] [33] [47]. Reviews of the broader GenAI-in-education landscape acknowledge rapid growth but do not identify a mature K–12 reading-comprehension evidence base [34] [6] [44].

There is also little direct evidence on broader writing quality as distinct from grammar correction. The mobile ChatGPT study emphasizes error reduction [33], and the Saudi study targets vocabulary [47]. The under-extracted 2025 secondary writing-feedback study may address broader writing skill, but the available summary is insufficient for exact reporting [27]. This matters because high-quality literacy instruction aims not just at correctness but also idea development, organization, evidence use, genre control, and revision depth. Non-AI writing studies show that such outcomes can be improved substantially through explicit instruction and self-regulated strategy development [1] [18].

Foundational reading skills are effectively absent. None of the direct generative-AI K–12 studies in the pool tests effects on phonemic awareness, phonics, decoding, or oral reading fluency. That is a critical limitation for any claim about K–12 literacy overall, because early literacy is foundational and developmentally distinct from secondary writing support. The absence of such evidence is even more conspicuous when contrasted with the strong tradition of early-literacy RCTs in non-AI instruction [1].

## Mechanisms and implementation: how generative AI is being used

The dominant implementation models in the K–12 studies are student-facing support during a specific task rather than full curricular replacement. GPT-4 was used as an interactive homework tutor built from standard homework materials, reducing teacher setup after initial configuration [4]. ChatGPT mobile feedback was used as a corrective-feedback engine for ESL writing [33]. The Saudi vocabulary tool was used as a targeted practice application for word learning [47]. These are relatively bounded use cases: feedback, tutoring, and vocabulary support.

Across broader reviews, the main hypothesized mechanisms are increased feedback frequency, personalization, on-demand explanation, motivational support, and reduced delay between student effort and instructional response [34] [9] [15]. Teacher-oriented reviews also suggest that AI’s perceived promise lies in scalability and time-saving, especially where teacher capacity is limited [8]. In the homework-tutor RCT, the teacher reportedly benefited from reduced preparation burden after setup, illustrating a possible operational advantage [4]. But these same mechanisms can shift from scaffolding to substitution if students simply offload the work of reading, generating, or revising text to the system.

Implementation details outside literacy reinforce that effects depend on how support is structured. In two high-school RCTs on AI feedback in physics, effects differed by whether AI hints were compulsory or autonomously requested and by student prior achievement: low-achievers benefited in the compulsory AI-hint condition (d=0.673), while high-achievers benefited in the autonomous AI-help condition (d=0.378) [14]. In a QED with grades 8–9 students, a brief AI-literacy workshop improved students’ regulation of LLM interaction in school science tasks and was associated with higher task performance (r=0.19) [28]. In another high-school QED, an AI chatbot improved situational interest and self-efficacy relative to a static textbook scaffold (d=0.44) [12]. These are not literacy studies, but they support a mechanism claim highly relevant to literacy instruction: effects likely depend on structured use, student self-regulation, and the match between task complexity and AI support.

Mixed-method K–12 studies of LLM classroom use also suggest that classroom orchestration is fragile. A real-time middle-school EFL study reports that classroom constraints such as limited time, mixed proficiency, and teacher orchestration can make LLM writing support break down in practice [36]. A mixed-method paper on “grade-specific teachers” argues that default LLM outputs often fail to match student developmental level, which is especially important for younger learners and equity-sensitive implementation [35]. These studies cannot support effectiveness claims, but they add practical evidence that open-ended student-directed use may be less reliable than tightly bounded, teacher-mediated use.

## Variation by population, subgroup, and setting

The population coverage is narrow. Direct K–12 evidence is mostly secondary and EFL/ESL-oriented [4] [33] [47]. This means the literature speaks more clearly to multilingual or second-language contexts than to mainstream English-medium ELA classrooms. Reviews of EFL/ESL instruction also show strong interest in digital supports and gamified tools, indicating a broader ecosystem in which GenAI is being adopted, but these syntheses are not specific to generative AI nor to literacy outcomes in general education [5].

There is little direct evidence for students with disabilities, struggling readers, or younger children. The K–12 AI-literacy review highlights uneven inclusion and definitional ambiguity across sectors [44]. Teacher-facing AI reviews similarly note that ethical, accessibility, and equity concerns are often discussed more than empirically tested [8]. The absence of direct subgroup trials means the brief’s priority populations remain largely unresolved. Even where heterogeneity is studied, it is usually by prior attainment rather than by disability or language-support designation. The GPT-4 homework-tutor RCT found stronger gains in one year level than another [4], and the high-school physics RCTs found different optimal AI-help structures for lower- versus higher-achieving students [14]. These findings imply that subgroup variation is likely real, but direct literacy evidence is thin.

There is also limited evidence comparing teacher-mediated versus student-directed use. The GPT-4 homework tutor was partly teacher-mediated at the design stage because the teacher seeded the homework materials, but students then interacted with the tutor independently [4]. The mobile-feedback and vocabulary-app studies also appear primarily student-facing within a teacher-organized instructional setting [33] [47]. No direct K–12 literacy study in the pool cleanly randomizes teacher-mediated versus open student-directed GenAI use. That question remains open, though adjacent evidence suggests structured mediation matters [28] [14] [36].

## Adjacent evidence that informs, but does not answer, the K–12 literacy question

Because direct K–12 evidence is thin, adjacent evidence can help identify plausible benefits and risks, but it must be handled cautiously. The most relevant adjacent evidence comes from postsecondary writing and learning experiments involving generative AI feedback, revision, and tutoring.

Design: Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT). “Beware of Metacognitive Laziness” tested ChatGPT, human-expert support, a checklist tool, and no support in a university essay-revision task (n=117) [24]. The ChatGPT group showed greater essay score improvement after revision, with omnibus eta²=0.108, and pairwise 95% CIs indicated advantages over checklist, control, and human-expert conditions [24]. However, process analyses showed heavier reliance on AI and relatively fewer metacognitive processes during revision [24]. For K–12 literacy, this suggests a plausible tradeoff: better immediate revision products may coexist with weaker independent regulation.

Design: Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT). “Experimenting with Generative AI: Does ChatGPT Really Increase Everyone’s Productivity?” studied Thai writing analysis tasks among university students (n=121) [10]. ChatGPT increased average writing-analysis scores by 0.43 points out of 10 and reduced completion time by 1.73 minutes, but stronger econometrics students performed worse on writing tasks with ChatGPT, indicating heterogeneous effects by prior competence [10]. This aligns with the K–12 pattern that benefits may depend on learner profile rather than being uniform.

Design: Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT). “Experimental Evidence on Negative Impact of Generative AI on Scientific Learning Outcomes” found lower comprehension accuracy after reading complex passages when adults used generative AI, with a 25.1% reduction under full AI reliance and a 12% reduction under partial AI reliance relative to manual work (n=32; p=0.002459 and p=0.005081, respectively) [21]. Although not a K–12 study, it directly raises concern for reading-comprehension tasks: if AI use substitutes for active processing, apparent efficiency gains may mask weaker understanding [21].

Design: Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT). “The Effects of Generative AI Agents and Scaffolding on Enhancing Students’ Comprehension of Visual Learning Analytics” found that proactive GenAI agents improved comprehension relative to passive GenAI agents and non-AI storytelling supports in higher education (n=117), showing that fixed support is not equivalent to more interactive scaffolding [3]. This is relevant because literacy interventions may likewise depend on the form of AI support, not just its presence.

Design: Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT). “Directive, Metacognitive or a Blend of Both?” tested different AI feedback types in higher education (n=329) and found effects on revision behavior but not final resource quality; directive feedback increased revision odds relative to metacognitive feedback (OR=2.93, 95% CI [1.25, 6.88]) and hybrid feedback had still higher odds (approximately OR=4.3, 95% CI [1.82, 10.0]) [31]. This suggests feedback design may shape literacy-process behaviors even when final-quality outcomes do not change.

These adjacent studies do not establish K–12 literacy effects, but taken together they imply a coherent mechanism pattern: generative AI may increase feedback volume, speed, and willingness to revise, while also raising risks of cognitive offloading, dependence, and uneven benefits by prior skill [24] [10] [21] [31].

## What broader syntheses say about likely benefits and limits

The most directly relevant broad synthesis is the 2025 meta-analysis on ChatGPT and student outcomes, which concludes that ChatGPT has positive effects on learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking across education, but the source pool does not provide the specific pooled estimates or K–12-literacy moderator results needed for precise reporting here [6]. Because the meta-analysis includes elementary through undergraduate populations, it is highly relevant for general direction but only partially useful for this specific K–12 literacy brief [6].

Other reviews are broader still. A systematic review and bibliometric analysis of generative AI in education finds that the field is expanding rapidly but is still dominated by exploratory work, conceptual debate, and higher-education applications [34]. Reviews of AI in higher education report similar patterns: promise for feedback, personalization, and efficiency, but limited rigorous causal evidence and substantial concerns about ethics, assessment validity, and over-reliance [7] [20] [19] [37]. A systematic review of generative AI for automated feedback finds strong interest in scalable feedback generation, but again mainly in postsecondary settings [9]. Reviews of tutoring systems and AI-enabled personalized learning also indicate that AI can improve learning under some conditions, but they aggregate across subjects and tool types rather than isolating K–12 literacy uses of generative AI [15] [2].

Reviews focused on teachers and AI literacy add a different implication. Teacher-oriented syntheses stress that adoption decisions involve workload, ethics, oversight, and the need for pedagogical framing, not merely outcome gains [8]. AI-literacy reviews argue that students need explicit preparation to question, monitor, and regulate AI outputs, which bears directly on literacy tasks involving source evaluation, summarization, and writing from evidence [44]. Ethics-focused reviews also foreground privacy, digital divide, and sustainability issues, though they do not contribute direct learning-outcome evidence [26]. Together, these reviews support a cautious implementation stance: any literacy benefit likely depends on strong pedagogical orchestration and critical-use norms rather than unrestricted access to text generation.

## Tradeoffs relative to teacher feedback, human support, and non-AI tools

The direct K–12 evidence suggests that generative AI can sometimes match or outperform ordinary comparison conditions on narrow outcomes, but it does not yet show superiority to the strongest human supports. The mobile-feedback QED suggests better writing-error reduction than teacher feedback [33], but the reported statistic does not establish whether gains extended beyond surface correctness. The GPT-4 homework-tutor RCT shows benefits over ordinary homework [4], but it does not compare GPT-4 with human tutoring or teacher conferencing. The vocabulary study compares ChatGPT with paper practice, not with established adaptive vocabulary software or high-quality teacher-led vocabulary instruction [47]. Consequently, the evidence does not justify a broad substitution claim.

The best non-AI comparators in the pool show that strong instruction can yield large literacy effects. SRSD Plus produced large gains in early elementary writing-related outcomes [1], and genre-based essay instruction improved writing and self-efficacy for underserved high-school students [18]. These are important because they establish that pedagogically rich human-led instruction remains a high bar. Reviews of differentiated instruction and other non-AI pedagogies likewise indicate that variation in instructional quality matters greatly for outcomes [11].

There are also construct-validity and authorship tradeoffs. A large observational comparison of human-written and ChatGPT-generated high-school argumentative essays demonstrates that GenAI can produce essay-quality outputs relevant to high-school tasks, which creates challenges for interpreting writing products as evidence of student competence [29]. Assessment-focused reviews similarly note that generative AI complicates the validity of writing assessment, because a better product may reflect external support rather than internalized skill [19]. This tradeoff is central to literacy: improved draft quality under AI support is not the same as improved independent reading or writing ability.

Dependency and over-reliance are another concern. In the postsecondary revision RCT, better revised essays coexisted with fewer metacognitive processes [24]. In a high-school RCT on AI literacy, students still adopted incorrect ChatGPT recommendations 52.1% of the time overall despite an intervention, and the brief intervention did not reduce over-reliance (final n=36; d=-0.25 for over-reliance) [46]. Although that study is in mathematical numeracy rather than literacy, the mechanism generalizes directly to reading and writing tasks where students must evaluate generated explanations, summaries, or wording. The likely literacy tradeoff is therefore scale and immediacy versus independence and source judgment.

## Strength of evidence by outcome family

For writing feedback and error correction, the evidence is the strongest, though still limited. The direct K–12 QED on ChatGPT mobile feedback supports better error reduction than teacher feedback [33], and the under-extracted 2025 secondary writing-feedback study points in the same topical direction [27]. Adjacent postsecondary RCTs on revision also support the plausibility of improved revision products under GenAI-supported feedback [24] [31].

For vocabulary, there is one relevant K–12 QED showing a positive result for a ChatGPT-based app versus paper-based learning [47]. That is encouraging but too thin for a strong general conclusion.

For broader writing quality, argumentation, disciplinary literacy, and source-based writing, the evidence is weak. The available K–12 generative-AI studies do not provide strong experimental evidence on these outcomes, even though non-AI writing interventions demonstrate that they are measurable and improvable [1] [18].

For reading comprehension, the evidence is very weak. No direct K–12 RCT or QED in the pool convincingly tests a generative-AI reading intervention against a strong literacy comparator. The only related causal warning signal comes from adjacent adult evidence showing comprehension losses under AI reliance [21].

For engagement and perceptions, the evidence is more positive but should not be conflated with literacy achievement. The GPT-4 homework-tutor RCT showed better engagement-related ratings [4]. The vocabulary and mobile-feedback studies also reported positive student attitudes [47] [33]. Broader reviews often report enthusiasm and perceived usefulness around chatbots and AI feedback [34] [20]. But perceptions are indirect evidence only.

### Synthesis and Implications

The best-supported conclusion is narrow: generative AI can improve some secondary literacy-related outcomes, especially in structured uses such as English-learning homework support, vocabulary practice, and AI-generated corrective feedback, and these benefits are most visible in EFL/ESL contexts [4] [33] [47]. The evidence does not yet show that generative AI broadly improves K–12 reading comprehension, foundational reading, disciplinary literacy, or overall literacy achievement. It also does not show that GenAI reliably outperforms strong human supports such as expert conferencing, structured peer response, or well-designed strategy instruction [1] [18].

For schools and practitioners, the practical implication is to treat generative AI as a bounded support tool rather than a general literacy solution. The strongest current use cases are feedback acceleration, vocabulary practice, and guided homework tutoring [4] [33] [47]. These uses are most defensible when teachers define the task, constrain the tool’s role, and require students to remain cognitively engaged in revision or explanation. Adjacent evidence suggests that outcomes depend on scaffolding structure, student regulation, and prior knowledge, so implementation should emphasize prompt routines, verification, and reflection rather than open-ended text outsourcing [14] [28] [24].

A second implication is that evaluators should distinguish immediate product improvement from durable literacy learning. Better corrected drafts, more complete homework, or higher engagement do not necessarily mean stronger independent reading or writing skill [19] [24] [29]. Schools considering adoption should therefore prioritize outcome measures that include independent posttests, transfer tasks, and assessments completed without AI assistance.

A third implication is that comparator choice matters for policy. Evidence that GenAI beats paper worksheets or low-feedback conditions is not enough to justify large-scale replacement of teacher-mediated literacy instruction. Given the strong non-AI evidence for explicit writing instruction and strategy-based support, the most appropriate near-term role for generative AI is supplementation where human feedback is scarce, not substitution where high-quality instruction is already present [1] [18] [8].

### Limitations and Research Gaps

The evidence base has five major weaknesses. First, there are too few direct K–12 RCTs on literacy outcomes. Only one strong direct K–12 RCT in the pool targets an English-learning homework context, not broad classroom literacy instruction [4]. Second, the available K–12 studies are concentrated in secondary EFL/ESL settings, limiting generalizability to mainstream English-medium classrooms [33] [47]. Third, outcomes are often proximal and narrow—error counts, vocabulary scores, short-term homework tests, and attitudes—rather than independent standardized literacy measures or follow-up outcomes [4] [33]. Fourth, comparator conditions are often weak; there are few direct comparisons with peer review, automated writing evaluation, strong teacher conferencing, or human tutoring [8] [15]. Fifth, equity-relevant populations are largely missing: students with disabilities, struggling readers, early elementary learners, and varied socioeconomic access groups are underrepresented [44] [26].

There are also notable reporting limitations in the current pool. Several highly relevant studies are under-extracted, with sample sizes, dosage, effect sizes, or outcome details not reported in the provided summaries [27]. Some of the newest studies are preprints, which increases uncertainty about stability and peer-review status [4] [14] [28]. Publication-quality syntheses exist, but most are broad and not specific to K–12 literacy [6] [34] [37].

The highest-priority research gaps are direct K–12 trials on reading comprehension, summarization, argumentation, source-based writing, and disciplinary literacy; elementary-grade studies including foundational reading outcomes; head-to-head comparisons with teacher conferencing, peer review, and non-AI writing tools; subgroup analyses for multilingual learners in mainstream settings, students with disabilities, and struggling readers; and longer-term studies measuring independent performance after AI support is removed [44] [34] [19].

## Body of Evidence Maturity: EMERGING
Justification: The evidence base includes a small number of directly relevant K–12 studies with some positive quasi-experimental and one notable randomized finding, but coverage is narrow, heavily secondary/EFL-focused, and thin for reading comprehension, foundational literacy, and equity-priority populations. Methodological rigor and comparator strength are inconsistent, and much of the surrounding literature is broader, adjacent, or postsecondary rather than directly responsive to the K–12 literacy question.
