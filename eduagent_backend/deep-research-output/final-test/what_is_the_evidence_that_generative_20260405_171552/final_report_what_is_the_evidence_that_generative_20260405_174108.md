## Executive Summary

The direct evidence that generative AI improves core K-12 reading outcomes is currently insufficient, not settled. The available K-12 studies are concentrated in adjacent literacy domains such as L2 writing practice, AI-assisted writing feedback, media/AI literacy, and teacher planning rather than reading comprehension, decoding, fluency, or vocabulary as primary endpoints. Broad syntheses show rapid growth in AI and ChatGPT use in schools, but they also emphasize fragmented designs, uneven outcome measures, and a literature dominated by exploratory or descriptive work rather than robust literacy trials [5] [4] [18] [14]. For the focal brief, that means the strongest conclusion is partial: generative AI appears capable of supporting some literacy-related processes in K-12, but there is not yet a strong causal evidence base showing that it reliably outperforms standard literacy instruction on core reading outcomes.

The strongest experimental findings in or near the target domain are mixed and mostly not direct reading-comprehension trials. In a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=75), an elementary chatbot-based L2 writing practice tool produced higher posttest writing scores than traditional instruction; the experimental group had a posttest mean of 2.11, while the control value was reported in the source summary only as lower, without a full exact statistic beyond significance [7]. In a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=36), a high-school AI-literacy intervention did not significantly reduce over-reliance on incorrect ChatGPT recommendations, with d=-0.25, but it did increase under-reliance on correct recommendations, t(34)=2.35, p=.025, d=0.78, and improved self-reported AI literacy for usage and working mechanism, d=0.76 and d=0.70 [11]. In a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=116), a middle-school AI-literacy workshop improved performance on science tasks with r=.19 and improved interaction regulation, including lower acceptance of underspecified prompts, 51.5% versus 66.7%, p=.044 [17]. These are relevant because literacy instruction using generative AI depends on students’ capacity to question and regulate outputs, but they are indirect evidence for reading instruction, not direct evidence of reading gains. The most directly relevant non-GenAI literacy benchmark in the pool is a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=32) of Headsprout Early Reading for elementary students with specific literacy difficulty, which outperformed treatment-as-usual on word/non-word recognition, d=2.65, sentence reading, d=0.96, and sight-word acquisition, d=1.53 [15]. That study establishes the scale of effects possible for literacy technology, but it should not be treated as evidence for generative AI specifically.

The strongest quasi-experimental and observational findings corroborate a narrower claim: generative AI can assist literacy-related tasks under structured conditions, especially feedback and guided inquiry, but the evidence base is thin and heterogeneous. In a Quasi-Experimental Design (QED, n=56), upper-elementary students using Microsoft Copilot for media-literacy learning achieved significantly higher fake-news detection outcomes than peers using a fact-checking website and Google search; however, the source summary does not report an effect size, and the outcome was media literacy rather than reading comprehension [19]. A Quasi-Experimental Design in high-school EFL settings found AI-assisted writing feedback improved writing more than teacher feedback alone, but the source summary provided here does not report the exact statistic [16]. Systematic reviews of AI in second-language acquisition, ChatGPT in education, AI in special education, and inclusive education repeatedly describe uses such as feedback, conversational practice, scaffolding, simplification, and teacher assistance, yet they also note risks around hallucination, over-reliance, ethics, uneven rigor, and lack of K-12 outcome studies [32] [18] [24] [8].

Across the tiered sub-questions, Tier 1 is answered partially: the literature defines the intervention broadly as student-facing chatbots, tutoring systems, feedback generators, text-generation tools, and teacher-facing planning supports, with writing, feedback, and AI literacy more studied than core reading subskills [18] [20] [23]. Tier 2 is also only partially answered: comparison conditions are usually business-as-usual instruction, teacher feedback, ordinary web search, or traditional lessons, but explicit head-to-head tests against strong literacy comparators such as structured phonics, guided reading, or human tutoring are largely absent in the generative-AI literature [7] [19]. Tier 3 is partially answered: implementation studies and reviews suggest outcomes depend on prompt structure, teacher mediation, curriculum alignment, and safeguards, but this conclusion comes more from descriptive and review evidence than from literacy-specific trials [21] [20] [44]. Tier 4 remains unresolved for the core question: there is too little direct K-12 causal evidence to conclude that generative AI improves reading comprehension, decoding, fluency, or vocabulary relative to standard instruction.

Overall confidence is low to moderate for process-level benefits and low for core K-12 reading-effectiveness claims. The single most important caveat is domain mismatch: much of the strongest available evidence is either outside K-12 literacy, outside reading specifically, or not generative AI at all. As a result, practical decisions should treat generative AI as an experimental support for feedback, practice, planning, and guided discussion rather than as an evidence-established reading intervention [4] [5] [14].

## Research Architecture & Open Questions

### Field Anchors
The citation structure does not show a single dominant pre-2023 foundation for generative AI in K–12 literacy. Instead, later work appears to draw from several adjacent traditions. One anchor is computer-assisted early reading intervention, represented here by Headsprout Early Reading, which established that AI-adjacent or automated instructional systems could be evaluated against business-as-usual literacy support for elementary students with specific literacy difficulty [15]. A second anchor is the pre-generative AI review literature on artificial intelligence in education and on teacher-facing AI use. Reviews of AI applications in higher education and of AI for teachers helped define the main categories of use—feedback, tutoring, planning support, and implementation concerns—that later generative AI studies inherit even when the setting differs [22][23]. A third anchor is the broader shift from conventional AI to large language models and chatbots in school contexts, visible in early post-2022 school-based studies and reviews that operationalize chatbot practice, prompt engineering, and ChatGPT use as educational interventions rather than merely technical tools [7][21][18].

Methodologically, the field is building less on a settled literacy-specific theory than on reusable intervention formats: automated feedback, tutoring systems, chatbot-mediated practice, and teacher support tools. Theoretically, the strongest continuity is with L2 and writing-oriented work, where generative AI is treated as a scaffold for production, revision, and feedback rather than as a reading-comprehension intervention per se [7][16].

### Research Gaps
The clearest gap is that K–12 literacy research on generative AI remains concentrated in writing, feedback, and adjacent literacy domains, with little direct experimental evidence on reading outcomes. The source pool contains school-based work on L2 writing practice [7], secondary writing feedback [16], AI literacy [11][17], and media literacy [19], but no modern RCTs testing generative AI for reading comprehension, decoding, fluency, or vocabulary growth in classroom literacy instruction. This is especially notable for emergent readers, struggling readers, multilingual learners, and students with disabilities, all named as priority populations but only partially represented in the available evidence [1][8][24].

A second gap is comparative design. Multiple studies share L2 or literacy-support logic, yet there are no direct head-to-head comparisons of common generative AI uses such as chatbot tutoring versus AI feedback, or student-facing supports versus teacher planning supports [7][16][23][26]. A third gap concerns teacher-mediated classroom implementation. Reviews identify teacher support and planning as important AI use cases [23][20], but teacher-planning evidence in the pool comes from teacher education rather than K–12 literacy classrooms [26]. Modern extensions into school-based literacy outcomes are largely absent.

### Hypothesis Surfaces
Among elementary multilingual learners receiving literacy instruction, chatbot-based generative AI practice will produce larger gains in standardized reading comprehension and vocabulary outcomes than business-as-usual instruction, tested in a classroom-level randomized controlled trial. This hypothesis follows from the existing L2 writing chatbot evidence [7] and the broader unmet need for reading-focused trials in school literacy.

Among struggling readers in grades 3–5, a generative AI tutoring intervention that provides guided questioning and text summarization support will improve reading comprehension more than a non-generative digital reading program, evaluated through an individual- or classroom-randomized trial using curriculum-aligned comprehension measures. The rationale comes from the older automated reading intervention base [15] and recent tutoring-system reviews showing activity without literacy-specific RCT consolidation [4][9].

Among secondary students, combining an AI literacy lesson with AI-assisted writing feedback will yield stronger independent writing quality and lower uncritical acceptance of incorrect AI suggestions than AI-assisted writing feedback alone, using a randomized controlled design with writing-performance rubrics and measures of over-reliance on erroneous outputs. This hypothesis is directly grounded in the coexistence of writing-feedback studies and AI-literacy interventions that have not yet been integrated in K–12 literacy work [11][16][17].

## Defining the Intervention and the Scope of the Evidence

The intervention in this brief is not a single technology but a family of generative AI uses: chatbots such as ChatGPT or Copilot, large language model tutoring systems, automated feedback generators, text-generation or simplification tools, question-generation systems, summarization aids, and teacher-facing planning supports [18] [20] [10]. Reviews of K-12 AI systems also place generative tools alongside broader intelligent tutoring systems and adaptive supports, but those broader categories include many non-generative systems, so they should not be collapsed into a single GenAI evidence base [4] [9].

Across the source pool, the most studied literacy-adjacent functions are writing practice, AI-generated feedback, conversational practice in second-language settings, media/AI literacy support, and teacher planning rather than direct reading-comprehension instruction [7] [16] [32] [19] [20]. Systematic reviews of ChatGPT and AI in education likewise report uses such as lesson planning, assessment support, tutoring dialogue, and content generation, but they do not establish that these uses improve core reading outcomes in K-12 classrooms [35] [18] [14].

The population coverage is uneven. K-12 reviews include elementary, middle, and high school students, but direct experimental evidence in literacy is concentrated in elementary L2 writing, secondary writing feedback, and middle/high-school AI-literacy tasks rather than in early reading or reading intervention contexts [7] [16] [11] [17]. Reviews focused on inclusive education, special education, and support for multilingual learners indicate substantial conceptual interest in using AI to reduce language barriers and support access, yet they do not provide a strong set of K-12 GenAI impact studies on reading for these groups [8] [24] [44].

## What Outcomes and Populations Are Most Studied

The target skills named in the brief are reading comprehension, vocabulary, fluency, decoding/phonics support, writing about reading, engagement, motivation, completion, and teacher instructional quality or efficiency. In the source pool, writing-related outcomes are more directly represented than reading subskills. The elementary chatbot RCT measured L2 writing performance and student perceptions, not reading comprehension or decoding [7]. The high-school QED on AI-assisted feedback also targets writing performance rather than reading [16]. The elementary Copilot study measures fake-news recognition and technology acceptance, which is literacy-adjacent but not a conventional reading measure [19].

By contrast, decoding, word recognition, sentence reading, and sight-word acquisition are directly measured in the non-GenAI Headsprout RCT for children with specific literacy difficulty [15]. That matters for the research brief because it shows which literacy outcomes can be measured rigorously in school-based trials, but it also highlights the gap: equivalent GenAI trials on decoding or fluency are not present in this source pool. Reviews of AI in second language acquisition and of struggling elementary readers in South Korea show that literacy intervention studies often assess vocabulary, comprehension, and language performance, yet these syntheses are not GenAI-specific K-12 reading meta-analyses [32] [1].

Population coverage is also skewed. Elementary students appear in the chatbot writing RCT and the Copilot media-literacy QED [7] [19]. Middle and high school students appear more often in AI-literacy regulation studies, schoolwide chatbot applications, and general ChatGPT reviews [11] [17] [18]. Students with reading difficulty or disability are most directly represented by non-GenAI or adult-assistive evidence rather than by school-based generative-AI reading trials [15] [34] [24]. Multilingual learners are discussed frequently in conceptual and review work, especially via UDL and language-barrier framing, but causal K-12 GenAI evidence for those subgroups remains sparse [44] [8].

## Counterfactuals and Baseline Conditions

Comparison conditions in the direct K-12 generative-AI studies are usually ordinary instruction or non-AI digital activity rather than strong established literacy interventions. In the elementary chatbot RCT (Randomized Controlled Trial, n=75), the control condition was traditional writing instruction [7]. In the upper-elementary Copilot QED (Quasi-Experimental Design, n=56), the control students used the Taiwan FactCheck Center website and Google search rather than the generative chatbot [19]. In the high-school AI-literacy RCT (Randomized Controlled Trial, n=36), the comparator was a control text rather than business-as-usual literacy instruction [11]. In the middle-school AI-literacy RCT (Randomized Controlled Trial, n=116), students were compared to controls during science tasks rather than literacy lessons [17].

These counterfactuals matter because they limit what can be concluded about classroom reading instruction. The literature does not yet provide many direct tests of generative AI against guided reading, reciprocal teaching, structured phonics, reading specialists, or human tutoring in K-12 literacy settings. Reviews of tutoring systems and ChatGPT in education repeatedly describe comparison against traditional instruction or no-AI conditions, but they also note that rigorous head-to-head comparator design remains limited [4] [9] [18].

The strongest clearly reported literacy counterfactual in the pool comes from the non-GenAI Headsprout study. In a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=32), Headsprout Early Reading was compared with supplementary literacy support typically delivered by a school SENCO, effectively treatment as usual for children with specific literacy difficulty [15]. Children in the program outperformed treatment as usual on word/non-word recognition, d=2.65, sentence reading, d=0.96, and sight-word acquisition, d=1.53 [15]. This provides a useful benchmark for what explicit school-based literacy comparison conditions can look like, but it does not answer the generative-AI question directly.

## Experimental Evidence Most Relevant to K-12 Literacy

A small number of experimental studies bear directly or indirectly on the focal brief, but they differ sharply in relevance.

The study most directly tied to K-12 generative AI and literacy in the source pool is [7]. In this Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=75), elementary students used a chatbot as an L2 writing practice tool, while the control group received traditional writing instruction [7]. The experimental group scored significantly higher on the posttest, with a reported mean of 2.11 for the chatbot group; the source summary does not provide the full control mean or a standardized effect size [7]. Students also reported favorable perceptions of the chatbot, including a mean of 3.54 for helpfulness in reviewing what was learned at school among n=37 respondents [7]. This is direct evidence that a generative conversational tool can support a literacy-related outcome in elementary school, but it is about L2 writing rather than reading comprehension, vocabulary, fluency, or decoding.

The high-school study [11] is not a literacy-instruction trial, but it is consequential for classroom use of generative AI. In this Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=36), students received either an AI-literacy intervention text or a control text before working with ChatGPT [11]. The intervention group showed a lower mean over-reliance rate on incorrect recommendations, M=48.68 versus 55.88, but the effect was not significant and was reported as d=-0.25 [11]. The intervention increased under-reliance on correct recommendations, M=36.84 versus 19.12, t(34)=2.35, p=.025, d=0.78 [11]. Self-reported AI literacy increased for usage, d=0.76, and working mechanism, d=0.70, but not for knowledge of limitations, d=0.19 [11]. For literacy classrooms, this suggests that even when students are trained, regulating generative outputs remains difficult and can shift in both adaptive and maladaptive directions.

The middle-school study [17] likewise addresses regulation rather than reading, but offers experimental evidence on a likely implementation condition for literacy use. In this Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=116), students who received a 2-hour AI-literacy workshop achieved higher final-answer performance on science tasks, with r=.19 [17]. Trained students were less likely to accept underspecified prompts, 51.5% versus 66.7%, p=.044 [17]. However, the workshop did not significantly change students’ GenAI self-report scores, and self-report measures were not correlated with task performance, r=0.01 for GenAI self-report and r=0.04 for metacognitive self-report [17]. Applied to literacy instruction, this indicates that effective use may depend more on observable regulation than on students’ perceptions of their own competence.

Another school-based chatbot RCT in the pool, (A vaccine chatbot intervention, 2025), is not an educational literacy intervention but shows that conversational AI can improve literacy-like outcomes at scale in a school-linked population. In a cluster Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=2,671 parents; 180 classes), the chatbot increased HPV-related literacy, coefficient=0.70, knowledge, coefficient=0.38, and rumor screening, coefficient=0.32, relative to usual care (A vaccine chatbot intervention, 2025). Because the outcome is health literacy among parents rather than student reading, it should only be treated as indirect evidence that structured domain-specific chatbots can deliver information effectively.

The final experimental benchmark is the non-GenAI literacy RCT [15]. In this Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT, n=32), children with specific literacy difficulty using Headsprout Early Reading gained more than the treatment-as-usual group on word/non-word recognition, d=2.65 [15]. The same study reported stronger gains on sentence reading, F(1,30)=14.44, p=0.002, d=0.96, and sight-word acquisition, F(1,30)=8.22, p=0.008, d=1.53 [15]. This is not generative AI, but it is the clearest causal evidence in the pool that digital literacy intervention can substantially improve elementary reading outcomes when designed around a specific reading problem.

## Quasi-Experimental and Mixed Evidence

The most directly relevant quasi-experimental generative-AI literacy study is [19]. In this Quasi-Experimental Design (QED, n=56), upper-elementary students in the experimental group used Microsoft Copilot, while controls used the Taiwan FactCheck Center website and Google search [19]. The experimental group significantly improved fake-news recognition from pretest to posttest and achieved significantly higher learning outcomes than the control group, though the source summary does not report an exact effect size [19]. Perceived cognitive load did not differ significantly between groups for either mental load or mental effort, and students reported favorable acceptance, with mean perceived usefulness of 4.16 and perceived ease of use of 4.23 among the experimental group [19]. This supports the narrower claim that generative AI can assist literacy-adjacent evaluative reading tasks, but not the broader claim that it improves core reading comprehension.

The high-school EFL study [16] uses a Quasi-Experimental Design and asks whether AI-assisted writing feedback improves writing performance more than teacher feedback alone [16]. That study is directly relevant to writing-about-reading and literacy feedback functions, but the source summary supplied here does not include the exact outcome statistic, so the strength of effect cannot be quantified from the available pool [16]. It nonetheless adds evidence that one practical classroom use of generative AI is feedback augmentation rather than replacement of instruction.

Teacher-facing planning evidence is even more indirect. [42] is Mixed-Methods and examines LLM-based teaching simulation for planning rather than student literacy outcomes [42]. [47] is also Mixed-Methods and focused on teacher planning for elementary arts/STEAM rather than reading outcomes [47]. [26] is a QED in vocational teacher education rather than K-12 classrooms [26]. These studies are relevant to the implementer side of the brief because they suggest plausible efficiency and quality gains in planning, but they do not establish that better planning via LLMs translates into better K-12 literacy instruction.

## What Reviews Show About the State of the Field

The highest-level synthesis evidence supports caution. The 2025 meta-analysis on ChatGPT and learning outcomes reports that ChatGPT can improve learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking under some conditions, but it covers populations from elementary through undergraduate and is not limited to literacy outcomes [5]. That makes it important background evidence but not a direct answer to the brief. Similarly, the 2025 systematic review of AI-driven intelligent tutoring systems in K-12 shows that AI tutoring has been evaluated across K-12 education and can affect learning and performance, but this body includes non-generative systems and many non-literacy domains [4].

The 2025 review of ChatGPT in education synthesizes applications, challenges, and impacts across school and higher education and documents uses such as tutoring, writing support, content generation, and teacher assistance [18]. The review is valuable for mapping use cases, but because it spans many subjects and educational levels, it cannot establish K-12 literacy effectiveness on its own [18]. The 2024 review of literature reviews on AI in education similarly concludes that the field is broad, fragmented, and methodologically uneven [14]. That tertiary finding aligns with the direct inspection of the source pool: evidence specific to reading and literacy is much thinner than the overall AI-in-education literature.

Reviews on specialized populations reinforce the same point. [8] discusses support for language barriers and special needs in K-12 settings but is a red-quality review and does not provide a strong causal corpus on literacy outcomes. [24] identifies opportunities and challenges in special education, yet the summary supplied does not indicate a focused set of reading-effectiveness trials. [44] presents teacher perceptions of using ChatGPT for multilingual learners and students with disabilities through a UDL lens, but perception studies cannot support claims of improved student reading outcomes.

The review literature on teachers is especially relevant to implementation. [23] shows that AI is often framed as supporting assessment, feedback, and planning for teachers in K-12 contexts. [20] reviews large language models for reducing workload and supporting instructional planning, including K-12 settings. Both reviews suggest why literacy educators may adopt GenAI even before outcome evidence is mature: the tools promise time savings and personalization. But neither review demonstrates that those efficiencies lead to stronger student reading performance.

## Implementation Models and Proposed Mechanisms

Three implementation models recur across the evidence base. The first is student-facing practice or tutoring, as in chatbot writing practice and LLM dialogue systems [7] [9]. The proposed mechanism is immediate interaction, increased practice opportunities, and individualized feedback. In writing contexts, that appears plausible, but the evidence for analogous reading gains is not yet established [16].

The second model is student-facing support for inquiry, evaluation, and content navigation, as in Copilot-supported fake-news detection or AI-assisted problem solving [19] [17]. Here the mechanism is not basic reading-skill instruction but scaffolding of higher-order literacy practices such as evaluating sources, generating questions, and interacting strategically with information. Reviews of prompt engineering in K-12 STEM indicate that prompt quality is a critical determinant of system usefulness, and that finding likely generalizes to literacy settings where question quality, text framing, and task decomposition matter [21].

The third model is teacher-facing generation and planning. Reviews of LLMs for planning support suggest that teachers use generative AI to draft lesson plans, create materials, generate examples, differentiate texts, and produce feedback more quickly [20] [23]. Mixed-methods studies on lesson planning and simulation further suggest that LLMs may help teachers anticipate misconceptions or generate options during planning [42] [47]. However, the step from teacher efficiency to student literacy gains remains largely untested in K-12.

Implementation conditions repeatedly identified in reviews include teacher mediation, prompt design, age-appropriate safeguards, curriculum alignment, and human verification of outputs [10] [40] [21]. The middle-school and high-school AI-literacy RCTs add a crucial mechanism: students need explicit support in questioning and regulating AI responses [11] [17]. Without that, literacy uses that depend on interpretation, summary, or explanation may be vulnerable to over-reliance and shallow processing.

## Variation by Outcome, Population, and Design

The current evidence suggests outcome variation is large, but this is partly because the outcomes differ so much across studies. Positive results are most visible for writing practice, feedback-supported production, and literacy-adjacent evaluative tasks [7] [16] [19]. Direct evidence for reading comprehension, vocabulary, fluency, and decoding is either absent or indirect in the GenAI K-12 studies represented here. That means apparent outcome differences may reflect what researchers have chosen to study more than true differential effectiveness.

Population variation is also unresolved. Elementary students appear in the chatbot writing RCT and Copilot media-literacy QED, both with favorable findings [7] [19]. Middle and high-school students appear in AI-literacy regulation studies where benefits are mixed and tied to strategic use rather than direct literacy achievement [11] [17]. Evidence on students with disabilities is especially thin in K-12 GenAI classrooms; the most relevant direct reading-related study in the pool is [34], an adult randomized trial for people with self-reported dyslexia, which reported improved reading-related outcomes with GPT-4-annotated text but is not school-based K-12 evidence.

Design variation matters as much as population variation. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews identify broad promise for AI-enabled personalized learning and tutoring, but those syntheses pool many systems that are not generative and many outcomes that are not literacy-specific [2] [4]. Experimental studies provide stronger causal leverage but are few, small, and heterogeneous in task and comparator. Quasi-experimental and mixed-methods studies enrich implementation understanding, yet they do not close the evidence gap on core reading outcomes.

## Risks, Constraints, and Negative Findings

The evidence base contains clear cautionary findings. The high-school AI-literacy RCT showed that brief instruction did not significantly reduce over-reliance on incorrect ChatGPT recommendations, d=-0.25 [11]. The same intervention increased under-reliance on correct recommendations, d=0.78, which the authors interpreted as more skepticism but which also implies possible rejection of valid support [11]. In literacy classrooms, this dual risk matters because students may either trust inaccurate summaries and explanations or dismiss useful scaffolds.

The middle-school AI-literacy RCT also complicates simplistic enthusiasm. Although the workshop improved performance, self-report measures did not change significantly and were unrelated to performance [17]. This suggests that teachers cannot rely on students’ confidence or stated understanding of AI when deciding whether they can use generative tools productively.

Reviews consistently identify further risks: hallucinated content, biased or inaccurate simplification, privacy concerns, equity gaps in access and supervision, and ethical issues in assessment and authorship [10] [40] [18]. Reviews of AI literacy and education reform likewise argue that student and teacher capacity to evaluate outputs is underdeveloped relative to the pace of adoption [39] [30]. For literacy specifically, these risks are not peripheral: hallucinated questions, inaccurate summaries, and over-scaffolded responses strike directly at the validity of reading and writing instruction.

## Evidence for Subgroups

Evidence for struggling readers is stronger for adjacent non-GenAI literacy technology than for generative AI. The Headsprout RCT directly targeted children with specific literacy difficulty and found large gains over treatment as usual across foundational reading measures [15]. The South Korea meta-analysis on English-language intervention for struggling elementary readers is relevant background because it shows the broader intervention literature for struggling readers is mature enough to examine moderators such as outcome type and context, but it is not a GenAI synthesis [1].

For multilingual learners, the evidence is again more suggestive than definitive. The elementary chatbot RCT in L2 writing provides one causal example of benefit in a language-learning context [7]. Reviews of AI in second-language acquisition indicate common uses in conversational practice, feedback, and language support, but they do not isolate K-12 GenAI reading-comprehension effects [32]. Teacher-perception work using a UDL frame suggests educators believe generative AI may help adapt language and materials for multilingual learners, but perception is not outcome evidence [44].

For students with disabilities, the evidence is weakest. Reviews on special education and inclusive education discuss accessibility, personalization, and reduced language barriers [8] [24]. Yet the pool contains no strong K-12 generative-AI RCT on reading outcomes for students with dyslexia, language-based learning disabilities, or other disability groups. The adult dyslexia trial [34] is promising but transfer to K-12 classrooms is uncertain.

## Practical Interpretation for Schools

For schools considering generative AI in literacy instruction, the current evidence supports a narrow and cautious use case. Student-facing generative AI may be most defensible when it is used as a structured support for brainstorming, writing practice, question generation, source evaluation, and guided discussion rather than as a stand-alone reading intervention [7] [19] [16]. Teacher-facing use for drafting materials, differentiating examples, and accelerating planning also appears plausible, though evidence that it improves student literacy outcomes is still indirect [20] [42].

Where schools do use GenAI, the evidence points to several minimum safeguards. Students need explicit instruction in questioning outputs and regulating use, because untrained or lightly trained use can produce over-reliance or unproductive skepticism [11] [17]. Teachers should verify generated texts, questions, and summaries for accuracy and appropriateness, a recommendation repeatedly justified in reviews on ethics and risk [10] [40]. Schools should also avoid assuming that time saved in planning or feedback automatically translates into better literacy teaching, since that causal chain is not yet well established [20] [23].

### Synthesis and Implications

The evidence answers the central brief only in part. Generative AI tools are being used for literacy-related purposes in K-12, but the research base is weighted toward writing practice, AI/media literacy, tutoring prototypes, and teacher planning rather than toward direct tests of reading comprehension, vocabulary, fluency, or decoding [7] [19] [20] [18]. The strongest causal findings in the pool show that chatbot-based writing support can improve elementary L2 writing and that AI-literacy instruction can improve how students regulate interactions with generative tools, though not always cleanly [7] [11] [17]. Those are meaningful findings, but they are not equivalent to evidence that generative AI improves K-12 reading.

The broader review literature suggests that AI’s likely educational value lies in personalization, immediate feedback, scalable practice, and teacher support, but it also makes clear that evidence quality is uneven and that generative AI raises distinctive risks around accuracy, bias, dependency, and assessment integrity [4] [10] [23]. For practitioners, the implication is to treat generative AI as a supplementary tool, not a replacement for explicit literacy instruction, structured intervention, or human feedback. In classrooms, the best-supported near-term uses are likely teacher-supervised writing feedback, guided questioning, source evaluation, and planning support rather than unsupervised reading instruction.

A second synthesis point is that comparator quality remains underdeveloped. Most GenAI studies compare against traditional instruction, a control text, or ordinary web search, while strong literacy comparators such as structured phonics, reading specialists, reciprocal teaching, and human tutoring are rarely tested directly [7] [19]. That means schools should be wary of replacing established literacy interventions with generative AI based on current evidence. The non-GenAI Headsprout RCT shows that digital tools can produce large literacy effects when tightly aligned to a defined reading problem and benchmarked against real treatment as usual [15]. Generative-AI literacy research has not yet reached that level of specificity or rigor.

### Limitations and Research Gaps

The largest limitation is scarcity of direct evidence. There are very few K-12 Randomized Controlled Trials or Quasi-Experimental Designs in which generative AI is used specifically for reading or literacy instruction and compared with credible non-AI alternatives. Much of the corpus is review-based, exploratory, higher-education-focused, mixed-methods, or centered on adjacent outcomes such as writing, science-task regulation, or teacher planning [14] [13] [3]. That weakens direct inference for K-12 classroom reading instruction.

A second limitation is outcome mismatch. Core reading subskills named in the brief, especially decoding/phonics support, oral reading fluency, and vocabulary learning, are scarcely represented in GenAI studies from this pool. Writing-about-reading and broader literacy practices are better represented, while foundational reading remains underexamined [7] [16]. Early elementary and emergent readers are especially undercovered; the clearest elementary reading-effectiveness study is non-generative [15].

A third limitation is subgroup evidence. Multilingual learners, struggling readers, and students with disabilities are frequently discussed in conceptual or review work, but rarely examined in strong K-12 GenAI causal studies with literacy outcomes [8] [44] [24]. Equity dimensions such as socioeconomic access, device availability, and home support are also largely absent from causal analyses.

A fourth limitation is implementation detail. Many studies do not report dosage, fidelity, teacher training, prompt protocols, or how teachers mediated student use. Reviews on prompt engineering, teacher AI use, and planning support indicate these factors likely matter, but literacy-specific evidence is still largely descriptive [21] [23] [20]. Longitudinal durability is another major gap: the literature does not yet show whether any literacy gains persist after AI support is removed or transfer to independent reading and writing.

Finally, the literature still lacks enough direct comparisons against strong alternatives and enough precise reporting. Several relevant studies in the source pool do not provide exact effect sizes in the available summaries, and some of the strongest syntheses are broad AI-in-education reviews rather than focused K-12 literacy meta-analyses [16] [2] [6]. The next research priority should therefore be well-powered K-12 RCTs and QEDs that isolate specific generative-AI literacy functions, specify the counterfactual clearly, report exact reading outcomes, and test subgroup and implementation moderators directly.
