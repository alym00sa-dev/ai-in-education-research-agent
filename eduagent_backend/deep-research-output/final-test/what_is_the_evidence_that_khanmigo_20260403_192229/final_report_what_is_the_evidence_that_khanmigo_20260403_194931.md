Outside science, adjacent tutoring studies suggest what counterfactuals are common in AI-learning research. ## Closest Science-Specific Experimental Analogues

### BEETLE II: Structured Science Tutoring With Dialogue and Simulation

Dzikovska et al. studied BEETLE II, an intelligent tutoring curriculum for basic electricity and electronics that combined simulation with reflective dialogue (Design: Randomized Controlled Trial, n=114 analyzed) [19]. The intervention produced substantial pre- to post-test gains compared with a no-training control, d=1.72 [19]. This is the strongest science-tutoring causal result in the pool. However, the population was undergraduate, not K-12, and the domain was tightly bounded. Crucially, the study could not demonstrate that dynamically generated NLP feedback was superior to non-NLP feedback between the two treatment versions (n=73 analyzed across the two active conditions) [19]. For Khanmigo, that distinction is central: the evidence supports a structured science tutoring environment, but not a conclusion that free-form language generation itself is the active ingredient.

### Personalized Science Text for Middle-School-Age Learners

The science-personalization study tested whether GPT-4 could personalize science texts for diverse middle-school-age learners (Design: Randomized Controlled Trial, n=23) [33]. Students in the experimental group were more likely to select the GPT-4 rewritten science paragraph aligned with their profile, with g=0.715 [33]. This is one of the few K-12 science-adjacent experiments involving an LLM. Yet the reported outcome is selection or preference for personalized text, not science mastery, reasoning, vocabulary acquisition, or inquiry performance. The study therefore supports feasibility of personalization, not effectiveness on the brief’s target outcomes.

### LLM-Powered Tutoring in High-School Physics

The Physics-STAR study examined an LLM-powered tutoring system in physics education (Design: Randomized Controlled Trial, n=12) [34]. The source pool reports that Physics-STAR outperformed both generic GPT-4o tutoring and traditional teacher-led review on physics understanding, especially for information-based problems, and that it shortened completion time on informational items, with mean post-test completion time M=257s for Physics-STAR versus longer times in comparison groups [34]. The study also reports improvements in deep learning, reflective learning, and critical thinking, though exact effect sizes are not provided in the source pool [34]. This is the closest analogue to Khanmigo in secondary science, but the evidence is highly preliminary because the sample is extremely small and the missing exact effect statistics constrain interpretation.

### Science-Linked but Not Science-Outcome Evidence

The SRSD Plus trial for first- and second-grade students used close reading of science texts to support informative writing (Design: Randomized Controlled Trial, n=93; posttest n=90) (Ray, 2023). The intervention produced large effects on writing quality, d=1.02, and planning/discourse knowledge, d=1.40, but the authors did not assess science content mastery directly; the nearest science-related outcome was expository text quality, d=0.48 (Ray, 2023). This study is relevant only as an example of science-text-mediated learning tasks, not as evidence that AI tutoring improves science learning.

Taken together, the science-specific experimental evidence is promising in narrow contexts but insufficient for strong inference about Khanmigo. The strongest positive result is non-K-12 and may owe more to structured tutoring design than to generative dialogue alone [19]. The closest K-12 LLM studies are either too small, focused on preference rather than learning, or lack exact reported effect sizes [33]; [34].

## Experimental Evidence on Mechanisms That May Matter for Khanmigo

### Adaptivity and Sequencing

The 2025 elementary mathematics study tested whether an AI-driven ITS with adaptive sequencing and learner choice improves learning and motivation (Design: Randomized Controlled Trial, n=265) [38]. In this four-arm trial, the ITS improved math learning from pre- to post-test, and the adaptive ZPDES condition outperformed the predefined sequence with η²=0.034 [38]. Choice improved emotional valence, η²=0.044, and showed a motivation trend, η²=0.013 [38]. Although the domain is mathematics rather than science, the result is highly relevant to mechanism: structured adaptivity and limited learner agency can matter, and the magnitude appears modest rather than transformative. For Khanmigo, this implies that any benefit may depend less on mere conversational access and more on how the tool sequences challenge and choices.

A later adaptive-scaffolding study tested worked-example selection based on cognitive engagement and mastery estimates (Design: Randomized Controlled Trial, N=113 enrolled; completers Control 36, BKT 35, DRL 42) [32]. Both adaptive policies significantly improved posttest outcomes with A=.58 and confidence intervals reported for each adaptive condition, and they improved efficiency measures such as time and steps with A=.58 for BKT time and A=.56 for DRL time/steps compared with control [32]. This study is undergraduate and not science-specific in the source summary, but it supports a mechanism that matters for Khanmigo-like systems: adaptivity grounded in engagement or mastery estimates may improve both learning and efficiency more reliably than unconstrained conversation.

### Personalization of Questions and Explanations

The question-personalization study examined whether adapting question phrasing to student proficiency in an ITS improves outcomes (Design: Randomized Controlled Trial; over 400 students, expected n=190, non-expected n=139, control n=141) [43]. Over a two-month A/B test, personalized question phrasing improved learning-related performance and lowered skip rates relative to comparison conditions [43]. Exact effect sizes are not reported in the source pool, but the study indicates that relatively small linguistic changes in prompts can influence both learning and engagement. This is directly relevant to Khanmigo because an LLM tutor’s prompt framing, not only its content, may shape student persistence and understanding.

The personalized XAI study compared an ITS with explanations versus without explanations for learning the AC-3 algorithm (Design: Randomized Controlled Trial, n=41) [47]. Learning differences were small, d=.12, but explanation functionality improved trust, perceived usefulness, and intention to use, ηp²=.28 [47]. This suggests that explainability features may matter more for acceptance and help-seeking than for immediate achievement. For science settings, that implies a possible tradeoff: students may like and trust a system that explains itself, but such trust does not automatically translate into larger learning gains.

### Interface and Engagement Design

The AI-driven interface design study tested whether interface expressiveness and explainability improve engagement in an ITS (Design: Randomized Controlled Trial, n=2,877 in one A/B test and n=19,164 in another) [20]. Adding a radar chart increased conversion from 5.25% to 6.26% and produced up to a 25.13% increase; a second test showed up to a 12.57% increase with a more informative design [20]. These are not learning outcomes, but they matter because Khanmigo’s likely effectiveness in homework or blended learning depends partly on whether students actually use it. The study suggests that feature and interface design can strongly affect uptake independently of the tutoring algorithm.

### Retrieval and Grounding

The KG-RAG study compared knowledge-graph-enhanced retrieval-augmented generation with standard RAG (Design: Randomized Controlled Trial, n=76) [31]. First-year university students using KG-RAG scored higher on a post-interaction assessment, M=6.37 (SD=1.92) versus M=4.71, with d=0.86 [31]. This is not K-12 science, but it is highly relevant to Khanmigo’s likely boundary conditions because grounded retrieval and preservation of conceptual relationships can mitigate one known weakness of free-form LLM tutoring: concept drift or factual inaccuracy. The result supports a hypothesis that science tutoring may work better when the model is constrained by structured knowledge sources.

## Evidence on Risks, Heterogeneity, and Why Open-Ended AI Support May Not Help Everyone

The adult reading-comprehension RCT provides one of the clearest demonstrations of heterogeneous AI effects (Design: Randomized Controlled Trial) [46]. For lower-performing participants, the GPT-based summary tool improved quiz accuracy from 44.9% (SD 15.8) in control to 55.4% (SD 18.7), d=0.45, n=71 [46]. For higher-performing participants, the same tool worsened comprehension from 82.5% (SD 10.5) in control to 66.5% (SD 18.4), d=0.83, n=124 [46]. The SAT/ACT-based subgroup split showed no significant effect for lower performers [46]. Although this is not a K-12 science study, it is directly relevant to Khanmigo expectations: AI supports can produce opposite effects across learner groups, perhaps by reducing desirable struggle or encouraging overreliance among stronger students.

The programming-feedback RCT likewise suggests that AI assistance can help on supported tasks without generalizing cleanly to unsupported tasks (Design: Randomized Controlled Trial, n=93 active submitters) [39]. Students performed better on the 38 tasks where GPT hints were available, but on eight harder tasks where hints were unavailable, the control group initially outperformed the experimental group during the first six attempts [39]. There was no significant time advantage when GPT hints were available across 1,784 successful submissions [39]. This pattern raises a caution relevant to science homework help: students may perform better when assisted, yet transfer or independence may not improve in parallel.

Reviews of generative AI in K-12, LLMs in education, and large language models in learning environments repeatedly point to risks around accuracy, authenticity, assessment, and overreliance [22]; [18]; [29]; [41]. The science-specific risk is especially important because inaccurate explanations can reinforce misconceptions rather than correct them. However, the source pool does not provide a Khanmigo-specific empirical estimate of hallucination or error rates in K-12 science. This gap should materially lower confidence in claims about safe autonomous use.

Teacher-facing reviews add a second layer of caution. The systematic review on AI for teachers finds that teacher roles, data use, and classroom integration are central and often under-specified [17]. Reviews of AI applications in higher education note that educators are often marginal in the research design, even though their mediation shapes pedagogy and ethics [23]; [44]. For Khanmigo in science, this implies that teacher facilitation is likely not optional implementation detail but a core moderator.

## What Baseline Science Support Looks Like Without Khanmigo

The brief asks how science learning is typically supported without Khanmigo. Broader instructional reviews help contextualize the non-AI baseline. The differentiated-instruction review suggests that tailoring instruction in secondary education is complex and variably operationalized, which matters because AI tools are often marketed as scalable differentiation engines [15]. These literatures imply that Khanmigo should be compared not only to “no AI” but also to established structured supports such as concept mapping, guided inquiry, differentiated tasks, and carefully designed science games.

## How the Intervention Is Implemented in Practice: What the Evidence Can and Cannot Say

There is no direct empirical implementation study of Khanmigo in K-12 science in the source pool. Therefore, the report cannot state how science teachers actually deploy Khanmigo for prompts, misconceptions, labs, homework, or formative assessment. No source here reports dosage, assignment design, frequency of use, prompt libraries, or teacher monitoring protocols for Khanmigo science use. This is a major Tier 3 gap.

Adjacent evidence nevertheless suggests several implementation features likely to matter. First, structure matters. BEETLE II paired dialogue with a simulator and a fixed science curriculum rather than relying on unconstrained chat [19]. Physics-STAR appears to have used a prompting structure designed to deepen reasoning on information-rich physics tasks rather than generic open prompting [34]. KG-RAG improved outcomes relative to standard RAG by grounding responses in a knowledge graph, indicating that stronger content constraints can improve instructional quality [31]. Across reviews of tutoring systems and AI-supported personalized learning, effective systems are generally described as tightly integrated with tasks, models of learner knowledge, and pedagogical decisions rather than as free-form assistants [9]; [35]; [6].

Second, teacher mediation likely matters. The review on AI for teachers emphasizes that benefits and challenges depend on how teachers interpret data, supervise use, and incorporate AI into pedagogy [17]. The K-12 generative AI review and LLM survey both suggest that classroom use of generative systems raises issues of monitoring, age-appropriate scaffolding, and curricular alignment [22]; [18]. Science is a domain where explanation accuracy, evidence use, and misconception repair are central, so teacher oversight may be particularly important. Yet no direct Khanmigo-science study in the pool tests teacher-mediated versus student-alone use.

Third, language and task design likely moderate outcomes. Personalized question wording improved learning-related performance and reduced skipping in an ITS [43]. Interface design changed engagement materially [20]. Explanation features changed trust more than learning [47]. Reviews focused on multilingual learners and AI literacy indicate that language load, disciplinary vocabulary, and digital competence can influence whether AI assistance helps or confuses learners [12]; [37]. For science, that points toward careful prompt engineering, bounded tasks, and explicit teacher framing rather than generic chatbot access.

## How Effects Vary Across Populations, Designs, and Conditions

The effect-size picture is uneven because direct Khanmigo evidence is absent and many reviews do not report pooled estimates in the source pool. Still, several patterns emerge.

By design, the largest science-learning effect in the pool comes from a tightly structured RCT in undergraduate electricity and electronics, d=1.72 against a no-training control [19]. More feature-level or personalization-level studies show smaller or more qualified effects: d=.12 for learning with explanations in an ITS [47], η²=0.034 for adaptive versus predefined sequencing in an elementary math ITS [38], and g=0.715 for selecting personalized science text rather than demonstrating learning gains [33]. This suggests a likely pattern: large effects may be more plausible when the intervention is a full curriculum or tutoring environment compared with weak controls, while incremental feature changes within a system tend to produce smaller gains.

By population, the limited evidence suggests heterogeneity rather than uniform benefit. Elementary learners benefited from adaptive ITS sequencing in mathematics [38]. Middle-school-age learners preferred personalized science texts [33]. High-school students in a tiny physics study appeared to benefit from a specialized tutoring structure [34]. Adults showed sharply divergent comprehension effects from GPT summaries depending on prior performance [46]. The reviews of multilingual learners and K-12 generative AI further imply that learner characteristics and context likely condition outcomes, but exact subgroup effects are not available for Khanmigo or for most science applications [12]; [22].

By implementation condition, studies suggest that adaptivity, grounding, and constrained support outperform looser alternatives. KG-RAG beat standard RAG at d=0.86 [31]. Adaptive worked-example selection beat control with A=.58 [32]. Personalized question phrasing improved performance and engagement relative to less aligned conditions [43]. Physics-STAR outperformed generic GPT-4o tutoring and teacher review in a small high-school sample [34]. These findings collectively indicate that specialized pedagogical and technical design may matter more than the mere presence of AI.

By outcome type, the evidence is stronger for engagement and proximal task performance than for durable science learning. Large-scale A/B tests show sizable engagement gains from interface design [20]. Trust and intention-to-use benefits from explanations are clearer than achievement gains [47]. Supported-task performance can improve without clear transfer once support is removed [39]. The source pool provides almost no delayed post-test or retention data for science-relevant K-12 AI tutoring. Retention therefore remains unresolved.

## What the Reviews Say About Science Education Specifically

The 2025 systematic review of generative AI applications in science education is the most directly relevant synthesis to the brief [3]. According to the source summary, it focuses on how GAI affects student learning outcomes in science education, particularly in middle school. Its existence indicates that science-specific GenAI evidence is emerging, but the pool summary does not report pooled effect sizes, sample totals, or strong causal conclusions [3]. This limits its use to contextualization: the field is active enough to review, but not yet mature enough in the provided summary to support definitive quantitative claims about expected effect.

The broader STEM AI review similarly treats AI-STEM as a complex educational system rather than a set of isolated tools [14]. That framing is helpful because it cautions against reducing science learning to chatbot interaction alone; outcomes likely depend on curriculum, teacher practice, task design, and assessment alignment. Reviews of tutoring systems, virtual tutoring with GPT-3, and AI-supported personalized learning also suggest that tutoring efficacy is shaped by affordances such as feedback timing, domain modeling, dialogue quality, and pedagogical orchestration [9]; [7]; [35]. For science, where conceptual dependencies and misconceptions are central, these conditions may be especially consequential.

The review of value-added digital game-based learning in K-12 science offers a parallel lesson from another digital-support literature: what matters is not just the technology category but the value-added mechanism [28]. That logic likely transfers to AI tutoring. A Khanmigo implementation that provides generic answers may differ fundamentally from one that prompts prediction, explanation, evidence use, and reflection. The current source pool, however, does not document which of these mechanisms Khanmigo actually deploys in school science.

## Synthesis and Implications

The evidence does not justify a claim that Khanmigo has been shown to improve K-12 science learning outcomes. No direct study in the provided pool evaluates the focal intervention on the focal population and outcomes. The honest answer to the main brief is therefore that the intervention-specific evidence base is absent.

The adjacent evidence supports a more limited conclusion. AI tutoring can improve learning or engagement under some conditions, especially when the system is structured, domain-constrained, adaptive, and paired with strong pedagogical design [19]; [38]; [32]; [31]. Science-specific analogues suggest potential in personalized reading materials and specialized physics tutoring, but those studies are small or measure indirect outcomes [33]; [34]. Reviews of K-12 ITS, AI-enabled STEM personalization, and science GenAI describe a promising but heterogeneous field in which effects likely vary by comparator, domain, and implementation [1]; [4]; [3].

For schools and practitioners, the strongest practical implication is to avoid assuming that conversational AI alone will raise science achievement. If Khanmigo is used, the adjacent evidence suggests it should be embedded in structured, curriculum-aligned science tasks; bounded to prompting, explanation, and misconception-checking rather than answer substitution; and monitored closely by teachers [17]; [43]; [47]; [31]. Use cases that resemble the stronger analogue studies—guided problem solving, grounded content retrieval, scaffolded reasoning, personalized wording, and explicit instructional sequences—are more defensible than open-ended unsupervised homework help [19]; [34]; [32].

A second implication is assessment discipline. Because some AI tools improve engagement more readily than learning, and because effects may differ by prior achievement, schools should evaluate science use with direct outcome measures rather than usage enthusiasm alone [20]; [46]. Useful local metrics would include curriculum-aligned science quizzes, explanation quality, misconception correction, transfer to unsupported tasks, and subgroup analyses for multilingual learners and lower- versus higher-performing students. Without such measurement, positive adoption signals could mask neutral or even negative academic effects.

### Limitations and Research Gaps

The evidence base is limited first by intervention specificity: there is no direct Khanmigo study on K-12 science outcomes in the source pool. This leaves the primary question unanswered and forces reliance on indirect analogues. The report therefore cannot estimate Khanmigo’s effect size, identify the most responsive science topics, or determine whether benefits differ across elementary, middle, and high school science.

A second limitation is evidence-type mismatch. Much of the adjacent literature is composed of broad systematic reviews whose source-pool summaries do not report pooled effect sizes, or of studies in mathematics, writing, programming, and higher education rather than K-12 science [4]; [35]; [9]; [44]. These sources are useful for mechanisms and implementation hypotheses, but they do not directly establish science learning effects for Khanmigo.

A third limitation is study quality and scale within the most relevant analogue evidence. The high-school physics RCT has n=12 [34]. The middle-school science personalization study has n=23 and measures preference rather than achievement [33]. The strongest science tutoring RCT is undergraduate rather than K-12 [19]. Several reviews explicitly note heterogeneity, ethical concerns, and the need for better-designed studies [1]; [22]; [18].

Key research gaps are clear. First, direct Khanmigo evaluations are needed in K-12 science classrooms, homework contexts, and tutoring/blended-learning settings, using science outcomes aligned to conceptual understanding, scientific reasoning, vocabulary, inquiry, retention, and assignment quality. Second, studies should compare Khanmigo against meaningful counterfactuals: teacher-led review, standard digital homework support, non-AI tutoring, and other AI systems. Third, implementation moderators need direct study, including teacher mediation, dosage, curriculum alignment, prompt restrictions, and whether the system is grounded in vetted science content. Fourth, subgroup analyses are essential for grade band, multilingual learners, students with disabilities, prior achievement, and school resource context. Fifth, risk studies are needed on inaccurate science explanations, misconception reinforcement, overreliance, and transfer after support removal. Until such evidence exists, claims about Khanmigo in K-12 science should remain explicitly provisional and extrapolative rather than evidentially established [17]; [12]; [22]; [18].
