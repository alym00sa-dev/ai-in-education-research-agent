**Query:** What is the impact of ChatGPT on math achievement in middle school students?

**Date/Time:** 2026-04-04 15:02:51

## Executive Summary

The current evidence does not support a strong claim that ChatGPT itself reliably improves middle school mathematics achievement in grades 6–8 relative to standard instruction, teacher-led help, non-AI digital tools, or no-AI conditions. No direct randomized controlled trial or quasi-experimental study in the provided evidence base isolates student-facing ChatGPT for middle school math achievement with curriculum-aligned outcomes and a clean middle-school comparator. The most defensible conclusion is therefore promising but unproven for the focal population and intervention. Broad syntheses of generative AI and AI in mathematics education generally report positive average effects on learning, but they pool heterogeneous populations, subjects, and tools, so they provide only indirect support for middle-school ChatGPT in math [1] [2] [3] [7] [11] [15] [27]. The closest middle-school-relevant causal evidence comes from hybrid human-AI tutoring and AI tutoring systems rather than open ChatGPT use [24] [13] [16].

The strongest experimental findings are adjacent rather than direct. In a meta-analysis of generative AI in education, pooled evidence indicated positive effects on academic achievement, but the source summary does not report the exact pooled statistic in the provided extract, and the sample spans elementary through graduate settings rather than isolating middle-school math [2]. A separate meta-analysis on ChatGPT found positive effects on learning performance overall, but the summary likewise does not provide the exact pooled effect in the excerpt and does not isolate middle-school mathematics [3]. In mathematics specifically, the living meta-analysis on generative AI for learning mathematics concludes that an experimental and quasi-experimental evidence base now exists, but the excerpt does not report a pooled statistic or a middle-school-only estimate [7]. Among individual trials, the most relevant school-age math RCT is Tutor CoPilot: an RCT with n=1,800 K-12 students across 4,136 tutoring sessions found that students whose tutors had AI support were 4 percentage points more likely to pass exit tickets than controls, from 62% to 66% [13]. However, this was tutor-facing human-AI support, not student-facing ChatGPT. In a secondary-school math tutoring RCT in the UK, supervised LearnLM tutoring was at least as effective as human tutoring for immediate learning and produced a 5.5 percentage-point advantage on transfer over human tutors alone, with CI [-1.4%, +12.4%], in n=165 Year 9–10 students [19]. Again, this is high school rather than middle school and a supervised tutoring system rather than open ChatGPT. A much weaker analogue is an adult algebra RCT with n=77 in which ChatGPT-generated hints did not produce statistically significant gains, while human-authored hints did [44]. This cautions against assuming equivalence between LLM output and strong math pedagogy.

The strongest middle-school evidence is quasi-experimental and process-oriented. In a three-site QED of hybrid human-AI tutoring in middle school mathematics, n=585 students across Sites 1–3 showed improved learning-process outcomes relative to software-only conditions: in Site 1, tutoring increased time spent on software with β=0.202, CI [0.057, 0.347]; it also improved a learning-process measure with β=0.2437, CI [0.106, 0.381]; and in Site 3, students completed 0.36 more workspaces per hour, CI [0.02, 0.70] [24]. This is the closest target-population evidence, but it measures engagement and progress indicators more than standardized or curriculum-aligned math achievement. Observational and review evidence further suggests that AI tools in mathematics are most often used for feedback, scaffolding, personalization, tutoring, and problem support, with implementation quality and teacher mediation repeatedly identified as central conditions for benefit [1] [15] [27] [33] [38]. At the same time, adjacent studies raise risks of overreliance, shallow uptake, and heterogeneous effects by learner level; for example, a reading-comprehension RCT found GPT summaries helped lower performers (d=0.45, n=71) but harmed higher performers (d=0.83, n=124) [32], and a high-school RCT found a short AI literacy intervention did not significantly reduce over-reliance on incorrect ChatGPT recommendations in n=36 students [43].

Across the tiered questions, Tier 1 is only partially answered: the literature clearly defines ChatGPT-like use cases in math as tutoring, hinting, explanation, feedback, and problem-generation support, but not with a stable middle-school-specific intervention model [27] [15] [1]. Tier 2 is partially answered: comparison conditions in adjacent studies include software only, static hints, human tutoring, conventional collaboration, and business-as-usual instruction, but direct middle-school ChatGPT-to-standard-instruction comparisons are missing [24] [19] [44] [17]. Tier 3 is partially answered: the best-supported mechanisms are immediate feedback, prompting explanation, teacher or tutor mediation, and structured scaffolding, while open-ended unsupervised use remains under-evidenced [13] [28] [25] [41]. Tier 4 remains unresolved for the core question: there is not enough direct evidence to estimate ChatGPT’s effect on procedural fluency, conceptual understanding, problem solving, or curriculum-aligned test performance for grades 6–8. Overall confidence is moderate for the narrow conclusion that evidence is insufficient and low for any stronger claim of effectiveness. The single most important caveat is the absence of direct middle-school ChatGPT math achievement trials with clear counterfactuals and validated achievement outcomes.

## Defining the intervention and the evidence boundary

The focal intervention is student use of ChatGPT, or a closely related generative AI chatbot, as a learning support for middle school mathematics. Across reviews of mathematics education and K-12 AI, these systems are described as serving roles such as tutor, conversational explainer, hint generator, worked-example provider, feedback source, and problem-solving partner [27] [15] [1] [37]. However, the literature does not yet offer a single stable definition of “ChatGPT use” in middle school math; many studies instead investigate adjacent forms of generative AI, AI-enabled tutoring, or teacher-facing supports rather than open student-facing ChatGPT [7] [11] [16].

The most studied outcomes in broader AI mathematics research include learning performance, problem-solving quality, engagement, feedback quality, personalization, and process indicators, while direct curriculum-aligned middle-school achievement measures are less common in the ChatGPT-specific literature [1] [15] [33] [27]. This matters because the research brief prioritizes procedural fluency, conceptual understanding, problem solving, and assessment performance. Evidence specifically on those outcomes for grades 6–8 with ChatGPT is sparse in the provided pool. Reviews note that AI in mathematics education is rapidly expanding, but much of the evidence is exploratory, descriptive, or heterogeneous in intervention design [1] [15] [33].

The target population here is middle school students, approximately grades 6–8, in classrooms, homework settings, or supplemental tutoring. The source pool does contain some middle-school evidence, but much of it is either mixed K-12, high school, undergraduate, or adult [13] [19] [44] [42]. As a result, direct generalization to middle school math should be treated cautiously. Reviews of AI in K-12 and mathematics also emphasize that population, setting, and implementation differences are substantial, limiting simple transfer of results across grades and subjects [37] [38] [16].

## What broad syntheses say about AI, generative AI, and mathematics learning

The highest-level evidence in the pool comes from systematic reviews and meta-analyses, but these are broad rather than target-specific. The systematic review of AI interventions in mathematics education synthesizes empirical studies from 2020 to 2024 and concludes that AI-based interventions are being used in mathematics classrooms for multiple purposes, including adaptive tutoring, feedback, and support for problem solving, but the summary excerpt does not report a pooled effect size and does not isolate middle-school ChatGPT [1]. The review is still useful for establishing that AI use in math education is no longer confined to legacy ITS and now includes generative systems, though outcome heterogeneity remains high [1].

The meta-analysis of generative AI in education similarly asks whether tools including ChatGPT improve student academic achievement and under what conditions, across elementary through graduate populations [2]. Its relevance is that it synthesizes experimental and quasi-experimental evidence, but the provided summary does not include the pooled statistic, mathematics-specific subgroup effects, or middle-school-only estimates [2]. The meta-analysis of ChatGPT on learning performance, perception, and higher-order thinking is directly about ChatGPT and reports that prior findings were mixed, but again the extract does not provide the pooled effect size or a middle-school-math subgroup estimate [3]. These syntheses support the inference that ChatGPT and generative AI can improve learning in some settings, but they do not settle the focal question.

More domain-specific syntheses point in the same direction but remain indirect. The living meta-analysis on generative AI and mathematics learning was designed precisely because the evidence base changes quickly [7]. It includes experimental and quasi-experimental studies on tools such as ChatGPT, but the provided summary does not report the exact pooled math effect or a grade 6–8 estimate [7]. The meta-analysis on whether GenAI improves mathematics learning outcomes also directly addresses mathematics, yet the excerpt again does not supply a pooled statistic or middle-school-specific breakdown [11]. A broader K-12 AI-enabled personalized STEM meta-analysis suggests that AI-supported personalization in schools can improve outcomes, but it spans STEM more generally rather than middle-school math or ChatGPT specifically [5]. Taken together, the broad synthesis literature is suggestive of positive average effects from AI-supported learning, but not sufficiently specific to conclude that ChatGPT improves middle school math achievement.

Several systematic reviews help interpret likely mechanisms and boundaries. Reviews of current AI practices in mathematics education and of ChatGPT in mathematics education emphasize common affordances: on-demand explanation, adaptive response, multiple solution paths, rapid feedback, and support for self-paced learning [15] [27]. Reviews of tutoring systems and AI-driven ITS in K-12 stress that learning gains are most often associated with structured pedagogical design rather than AI capability alone [14] [16] [8]. Reviews of teacher-facing AI also stress that teachers remain central in selecting tasks, monitoring use, and correcting errors [38]. This is important because the focal question is not whether AI can do math-related dialogue, but whether ChatGPT improves student achievement under school-relevant conditions.

## Direct and adjacent experimental evidence

### Randomized controlled trials

Tutor CoPilot is one of the strongest school-age causal studies in the pool. This was an RCT with n=1,800 K-12 students across 4,136 tutoring sessions in mathematics, where tutors in the treatment condition had access to real-time AI support [13]. Students whose tutors had Tutor CoPilot were 4 percentage points more likely to pass exit tickets than controls, rising from 62% to 66% [13]. The system also changed tutor behavior: classifier analyses over more than 550,000 messages found treatment tutors were more likely to prompt student explanation and ask questions [13]. The study is highly relevant for mechanism and implementation because it shows that AI may improve math outcomes when it augments adult instruction, but it is not evidence about students independently using ChatGPT.

The exploratory UK classrooms study is another RCT relevant to math tutoring with generative AI. This Randomized Controlled Trial included n=165 Year 9–10 students across five UK secondary schools and compared supervised LearnLM tutoring with human tutoring and static pre-written hints [19]. The study found that supervised LearnLM tutoring was at least as effective as human tutoring for immediate math learning and produced a 5.5 percentage-point advantage on transfer relative to human tutors alone, with CI [-1.4%, +12.4%] [19]. Compared with static hints, interactive tutoring yielded much higher second-attempt correctness and misconception resolution: human tutoring achieved 91.2% second-attempt correctness, CI [88.5%, 93.6%], LearnLM achieved 93.0%, CI [90.4%, 95.3%], and static hints achieved 65.4%, CI [63.8%, 66.9%]; for misconception resolution, human tutoring achieved 94.9%, CI [92.6%, 96.8%], LearnLM achieved 95.4%, CI [93.1%, 97.1%], and static hints achieved 86.8%, CI [85.7%, 88.0%] [19]. This is stronger than much of the ChatGPT literature but still not middle school and not open ChatGPT.

The adult algebra hint study gives an important cautionary counterpoint. This Randomized Controlled Trial with n=77 Mechanical Turk participants compared algebra hints generated by ChatGPT with human tutor-generated hints in an adaptive tutoring setting [44]. Both groups showed positive pre-post learning gains, but gains were statistically significant only for the human-hint condition; the summary states that in Elementary Algebra the average gain was 24.63% with p reported for the human condition, while the ChatGPT condition did not achieve significant gains [44]. The study does not provide middle-school evidence, but it directly bears on the quality of math hinting: unstructured ChatGPT-generated hints may not reproduce the effectiveness of human-authored pedagogical support.

The RCT on mathematical modelling preferences is not an achievement study, but it informs role design. In this Randomized Controlled Trial with n=26 undergraduates, the paper did not measure learning gains; students preferred the Tutor role over other AI roles during mathematical modelling, and AI role significantly affected task confidence, perceived helpfulness, and ease of interaction with p<.001 [30]. This cannot support claims about achievement, but it reinforces the plausibility that pedagogical role framing matters when students use AI for mathematics.

The RCT “Beyond the Wow Factor” is outside middle school math but useful for mechanism. In Study 1, n=175 high school and undergraduate learners used either a theory-informed chatbot, a standard ChatGPT chatbot, or teaching-video control [28]. ChatTutor did not significantly outperform ChatGPT immediately post-lesson on conceptual knowledge, though it did outperform the teaching-video condition at some time points, with η²=0.088 and η²=0.070 reported in the summary [28]. ChatTutor also produced higher trust, enjoyment, and behavioral intentions than ChatGPT, with d=0.60, d=0.86, and d=0.57, respectively [28]. The result suggests that pedagogically designed conversational support can outperform generic chatbot interaction on some dimensions, but the evidence is not on middle-school mathematics achievement.

Two additional RCTs are adjacent and help qualify likely risks. In an undergraduate study of pretesting before conversational AI use, n=60 analyzed students who completed a pretest before accessing ChatGPT scored higher on a delayed transfer/final test than students who accessed ChatGPT immediately, with final test d=8.59 [45]. The magnitude is unusually large and should be interpreted cautiously, but the direction suggests that requiring productive effort before AI assistance may improve retention. In a high-school RCT with n=36, a short AI literacy intervention did not significantly reduce over-reliance on incorrect ChatGPT recommendations, with d=-0.25 reported for over-reliance and d=0.78 for another measure in the summary [43]. This indicates that simple guardrails may not be enough to prevent misuse.

### Quasi-experimental evidence most relevant to middle school mathematics

The closest target-population study is the three-study quasi-experimental investigation of hybrid human-AI tutoring in middle school mathematics. This Quasi-Experimental Design study included n=585 total students across three sites: Site 1 n=125, Site 2 n=385, Site 3 n=75 [24]. Across the sites, hybrid human-AI tutoring improved math learning-process outcomes relative to software only. In Site 1, mixed-effects models found that tutoring increased time spent on software, β=0.202, CI [0.057, 0.347] [24]. The study also reports another positive process effect, β=0.2437, CI [0.106, 0.381] [24]. In Site 3, reduced tutor-to-student ratios with dashboard-guided support were associated with 0.36 more workspaces completed per hour, CI [0.02, 0.70] [24]. The study also reports a practical time-on-task change from 24 to 33 minutes per week, though no CI is reported for that descriptive comparison [24]. This is meaningful evidence that AI-supported tutoring can improve engagement and work rate in middle-school math, but it falls short of demonstrating gains on curriculum-aligned achievement tests.

A quasi-experimental undergraduate mathematics study is relevant for outcome decomposition. In the MatGPT study, n=94 undergraduates solving differential equations received MatGPT, MATLAB, or traditional instruction [26]. Using MatGPT improved overall mathematical proficiency with eta^2=0.36 [26]. It also outperformed comparison conditions on procedural fluency, strategic competence, and adaptive reasoning, with eta^2=0.23, 0.26, and 0.33, respectively, while conceptual understanding showed no significant treatment effect in the summary [26]. The relevance here is indirect but useful: if generative AI benefits math learning, those benefits may concentrate in procedural and strategic dimensions rather than conceptual understanding unless the tool is deliberately structured.

The remaining quasi-experimental studies in the pool are outside mathematics or outside the target age range, so they can only inform general implementation questions. For example, AI-supported online classroom management improved achievement and self-directed online learning in middle-school EFL over 8 weeks, with a Time×Group interaction ηp2=0.062 for Unit 2 achievement in n=65 [40]. This cannot be treated as evidence for math achievement, but it does suggest that AI-mediated structure can affect learning behavior in middle school. Similarly, AI-blended learning in undergraduate biology improved attitudes and performance relative to traditional teaching [36], and AI-assisted writing feedback improved secondary writing performance relative to teacher feedback alone [29]. These studies support plausibility for AI-assisted learning but not the focal claim.

## Counterfactuals and what comparison conditions actually look like

One of the main weaknesses in the evidence base is the lack of direct counterfactuals for middle-school ChatGPT in math. In the studies that do exist, comparison conditions vary widely. In Tutor CoPilot, the comparison was tutors without AI assistance during live K-12 math tutoring [13]. In the UK LearnLM study, the comparison conditions were human tutoring and static pre-written hints [19]. In the hybrid middle-school tutoring QED, the comparison was software only rather than tutoring augmented by AI [24]. In the adult algebra hint study, the comparison was human-authored hints within an adaptive tutoring system [44]. These are legitimate counterfactuals, but they are not equivalent to ordinary middle-school classroom instruction or typical homework support.

The broader tutoring-systems literature helps clarify what “business as usual” often means in technology studies. Reviews of ITS and tutoring systems show that comparator conditions commonly include conventional classroom teaching, non-adaptive software, static digital content, worksheet practice, or lower-support digital platforms rather than strong teacher-led individualized instruction [14] [16] [8]. This matters because AI effects can look larger when compared against weak static hints than when compared against expert human tutoring. The UK LearnLM RCT illustrates exactly this pattern: interactive tutoring, whether human or AI-supervised, greatly exceeded static hints on immediate correction and misconception resolution [19].

The conventional non-AI baseline in middle-school mathematics is not empty. Standard instruction typically includes teacher explanation, guided practice, formative assessment, peer discussion, and sometimes digital practice platforms. The RCT on professional development in formative classroom assessment shows that improving teacher assessment skill alone did not reliably increase middle-school math achievement and, in Year 3, adjusted math scores were significantly lower in treatment schools with d≈-0.33, while Years 1 and 2 were near zero at d≈0.03 and d≈-0.01 [35]. This does not argue against teacher-led instruction; rather, it shows that changing one instructional component does not guarantee achievement gains. It also underlines the importance of using meaningful counterfactuals when evaluating ChatGPT.

## Mechanisms: what seems to drive better outcomes

Across the pool, the best-supported mechanisms are scaffolding, dialogue, immediate feedback, and prompting explanation rather than answer delivery. Tutor CoPilot changed tutor practice toward more explanation prompts and questioning, and this coincided with better exit-ticket success [13]. The UK LearnLM tutoring study showed that interactive dialogue outperformed static hints on second-attempt correctness and misconception resolution [19]. These findings are consistent with broader mathematics education theory emphasizing scaffolding and dialogic teaching as mechanisms that support mathematical reasoning, explanation, and sense-making [25].

Reviews of AI in mathematics education also repeatedly identify feedback timing, personalization, and adaptive support as likely pathways to learning gains [1] [15] [33]. The review of AI-driven ITS in K-12 reports that such systems are often evaluated for their ability to provide individualized feedback and track student progress [16]. The review of virtual tutoring systems using GPT-3 likewise frames virtual tutoring benefits in terms of immediate assistance and scalable feedback, while noting limitations relative to face-to-face instruction [21]. None of these reviews proves that ChatGPT improves middle-school math achievement, but they make clear that the most plausible mechanisms are pedagogical, not merely technical.

Design studies further sharpen this point. The “Beyond the Wow Factor” RCT found that a theory-informed chatbot could outperform a standard ChatGPT-based chatbot on some motivational variables and some conceptual outcomes relative to a video condition [28]. The implication is that the design of prompts, constraints, and pedagogical sequencing matters. The mixed-methods study on ChatGPT scaffolding for limit concepts similarly argues that guided inquiry plus ChatGPT-mediated scaffolding can support metacognition, though it does not provide causal evidence for middle-school achievement [39]. The Wayang Outpost/MathSpring study, although older and not about ChatGPT, is relevant because it frames effective math tutoring as addressing cognition, metacognition, and affect together [41]. This aligns with the emerging view that generic chatbot access is less likely to produce strong learning than AI embedded in structured pedagogical routines.

Pretesting evidence reinforces the value of productive struggle before AI help. In the conversational-AI pretesting RCT, students who attempted retrieval before consulting ChatGPT later outperformed those who went straight to the AI on delayed outcomes [45]. While not a math-middle-school study, it supports a mechanism highly relevant to mathematics: AI may support learning better when used after an initial attempt, not before.

## Implementation conditions and usage patterns

The literature strongly suggests that implementation conditions matter, but direct evidence for middle-school ChatGPT math is thin. Reviews of AI in mathematics education and K-12 generative AI consistently describe variation in teacher mediation, learner autonomy, task structure, and guardrails as major determinants of outcomes [15] [37] [38]. The scoping survey of ChatGPT in mathematics education similarly identifies both opportunities and challenges, including risks of inaccuracies and overdependence [27].

The most credible positive evidence in the pool involves teacher- or tutor-mediated deployment rather than unsupervised chatbot use. Tutor CoPilot supported tutors in real time rather than replacing them [13]. The middle-school QED used hybrid human-AI tutoring with dashboard-guided support rather than open ChatGPT access [24]. The LearnLM RCT also involved supervised AI tutoring in classrooms [19]. These studies converge on a practical implementation lesson: AI appears most promising when embedded inside human instructional systems.

Evidence on prompt design and role constraints is indirect but consistent. The mathematical-modelling role study found students preferred a Tutor role over alternatives [30]. The “Beyond the Wow Factor” study found a theory-informed chatbot outperformed standard ChatGPT on several motivational outcomes [28]. Reviews in programming education similarly argue that pedagogical roles and guardrails shape whether LLM tools support deep learning or merely output generation [10] [23]. Although these are outside middle-school math, they support the idea that how ChatGPT is framed and constrained may be as important as whether it is present.

Outside-school access and homework use are central real-world issues, but the evidence base is especially weak there. The source pool does not include a direct middle-school mathematics homework study comparing ChatGPT with no-AI or teacher-led homework support. Reviews identify homework help and supplemental tutoring as common use cases for AI, but they do not provide achievement estimates for middle-school math [15] [21]. This is a major unresolved implementation gap.

## Variation in effects by outcome type and learner characteristics

The available evidence suggests that effects likely vary by outcome type. The undergraduate MatGPT QED found gains in procedural fluency, strategic competence, and adaptive reasoning, but not conceptual understanding [26]. The “Beyond the Wow Factor” RCT found mixed conceptual knowledge findings across time points [28]. The implication is that AI supports may more readily improve execution, strategy, or near-transfer performance than deep conceptual understanding unless activities are specifically designed for that purpose.

There is also reason to expect heterogeneity by prior attainment. The middle-school hybrid tutoring QED explicitly framed low-income students and prior achievement as relevant, though the summary excerpt does not report subgroup effect sizes by prior attainment [24]. The reading-comprehension RCT offers stronger though non-math evidence of differential effects: a GPT-based summary tool improved quiz accuracy for lower-performing participants to 55.4% versus 44.9% in control, d=0.45, n=71, but worsened performance for higher-performing participants to 66.5% versus 82.5% in control, d=0.83, n=124 [32]. This suggests a plausible risk in mathematics as well: learners who need support may benefit from scaffolded AI, while stronger students may become less engaged in deeper processing if AI oversimplifies or short-circuits reasoning.

Direct subgroup evidence for middle-school mathematics is largely absent. The source pool does not provide ChatGPT-specific math effects by English learner status, disability status, gender, race/ethnicity, or special education status for grades 6–8. Reviews repeatedly note equity as an important concern, but they mostly discuss it as a research need rather than presenting resolved evidence [37] [38] [15]. The middle-school hybrid tutoring study is one of the few to foreground low-income contexts, making it especially relevant, but its reported outcomes are process-based rather than subgroup-specific achievement impacts [24].

## Risks, tradeoffs, and possible downside mechanisms

The risk literature is stronger than the direct middle-school achievement literature. The high-school RCT on AI literacy found that a short intervention did not significantly reduce over-reliance on incorrect ChatGPT recommendations [43]. This suggests that simply telling students about AI limitations may not be enough to prevent poor judgments. The adult algebra hint study found that ChatGPT hints did not produce the same significant gains as human-authored hints [44]. Together these findings indicate that incorrect, vague, or pedagogically weak AI output is a real concern in mathematics, where errors can cascade through multistep reasoning.

There is also evidence that AI can improve immediate task performance while raising concerns about deeper learning. The undergraduate RCT on ChatGPT productivity found higher scores on math and data analysis tasks by 1.65 points on a 10-point scale with ChatGPT, in n=121, and faster completion by 0.99 minutes on average [42]. However, 42% did not improve, according to the summary [42]. This pattern is compatible with heterogeneous benefit and possible dependence on task type. The programming-feedback RCT similarly found that students did better on tasks where GPT hints were available, but on harder tasks where hints were removed, the control group outperformed the experimental group during the first six attempts [22]. Although not mathematics, this is directly relevant to the concern about whether AI support builds durable independent problem-solving.

Reviews of ChatGPT and AI in mathematics education consistently note risks of hallucination, shallow copying, reduced productive struggle, and academic integrity concerns [27] [15] [1]. Review evidence on generative AI in K-12 more broadly also identifies uneven access and teacher monitoring as equity and implementation challenges [37]. These concerns are particularly salient in middle school, where self-regulation is still developing and student prompting skill may be limited.

## What can be said about likely impact on middle school math achievement

The empirical literature in the provided pool does not yet justify the claim that ChatGPT improves middle school mathematics achievement relative to standard instruction or other common supports. That conclusion follows not from negative evidence, but from missing direct evidence. No cited study in the pool tests student-facing ChatGPT in grades 6–8 math with outcomes such as procedural fluency, conceptual understanding, word-problem performance, or curriculum-aligned test scores against a clear middle-school comparator.

What can be said is narrower. First, broad review and meta-analytic evidence suggests AI and generative AI often have positive average effects on learning, including mathematics, but those estimates are too heterogeneous to answer the specific brief [2] [3] [7] [11] [1]. Second, the best adjacent causal evidence indicates that AI support can improve math-related outcomes when it is scaffolded, supervised, and integrated with human tutoring or structured pedagogy [13] [19] [24]. Third, direct evidence from algebra hints and over-reliance studies warns that open-ended or weakly constrained ChatGPT use may fail to produce learning gains or may distort learner judgment [44] [43].

The most plausible current inference is therefore conditional: if ChatGPT is to help middle-school math achievement, it is more likely to do so when used as a constrained scaffold for explanation, questioning, and feedback after students attempt problems, ideally under teacher or tutor oversight. That inference is consistent with studies of human-AI tutoring, theory-informed chatbot design, dialogic teaching, and adaptive tutoring systems [13] [24] [28] [25] [41]. But it remains an inference, not a demonstrated middle-school ChatGPT effect.

### Synthesis and Implications

The evidence base supports a restrained synthesis. ChatGPT is best understood not as a proven middle-school mathematics intervention, but as one emerging form of AI-supported instructional assistance within a broader landscape of tutoring systems, conversational supports, and personalized feedback tools [1] [15] [16]. Broad evidence indicates that AI can improve learning, but the strongest school-relevant gains come from structured, interactive, and human-mediated systems rather than generic chatbot access [2] [3] [13] [19]. In middle-school math specifically, the closest available evidence suggests that hybrid human-AI tutoring can increase time on task, participation, and work completion relative to software-only conditions [24]. These process gains may be important precursors to achievement, especially in under-resourced contexts, but they are not substitutes for direct test-score evidence.

For schools and practitioners, the practical implication is to avoid treating open ChatGPT access as an evidence-based replacement for established middle-school math instruction or tutoring. If schools use ChatGPT-like tools, the most evidence-aligned implementation is a constrained support role: prompting explanation, offering hints after student attempts, surfacing misconceptions, and supplementing rather than replacing teacher judgment [13] [19] [25] [38]. Teachers should assume that prompt quality, guardrails, and monitoring matter, because the literature repeatedly links effective AI use to pedagogy and supervision rather than to the model alone [28] [15] [10]. Schools should also be cautious about homework-only or unsupervised use, because the current source base offers almost no direct evidence for those settings and some adjacent evidence indicates risks of over-reliance or reduced independent performance when support is withdrawn [22] [43].

### Limitations and Research Gaps

The largest limitation is directness. The source pool contains no direct RCT or QED that isolates student-facing ChatGPT for middle-school mathematics achievement against standard instruction, no-AI support, teacher-led help, or a well-matched digital comparator. Most evidence is one of four indirect types: broad meta-analyses pooling multiple grades and subjects [2] [3] [7]; AI-in-math reviews that synthesize heterogeneous interventions rather than ChatGPT specifically [1] [15] [33]; middle-school studies of hybrid human-AI tutoring that do not isolate ChatGPT [24]; or non-middle-school ChatGPT studies in high school, undergraduate, or adult settings [19] [26] [44]. Because of this, confidence in any positive claim about the focal intervention must remain limited.

Methodologically, many studies are short-term, use proximal outcomes, or emphasize perceptions and process metrics over validated math achievement. Subgroup evidence is especially thin for the target population: there is little or no direct evidence on variation by prior math attainment, English learner status, disability, gender, or socioeconomic status in middle-school ChatGPT math use [37] [38]. Comparison conditions are also inconsistent, ranging from static hints to software-only controls, which complicates interpretation of relative effectiveness [19] [24] [44]. Publication bias and rapid tool evolution are additional concerns noted implicitly by the need for living reviews and fast-moving syntheses [7].

The most important research gap is a need for direct middle-school mathematics trials of student-facing ChatGPT or closely constrained LLM tutoring. These should compare against business-as-usual instruction, teacher-led support, and established digital tools; report procedural, conceptual, problem-solving, and curriculum-aligned assessment outcomes; include delayed posttests for retention and transfer; and test implementation moderators such as hint-only versus answer-giving use, pretesting before AI access, teacher mediation, and guardrails against hallucinated math explanations. Without that evidence, claims about the impact of ChatGPT on middle school math achievement remain provisional.
