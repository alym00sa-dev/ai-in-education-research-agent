## Executive Summary

The available evidence does not yet show that generative AI-based instructional coaching or automated lesson-plan feedback has been proven to improve K–12 teacher planning quality or instructional practice relative to conventional coaching approaches in authentic school settings. The strongest conclusion is narrower: genAI appears promising as a fast, scalable first-pass support for drafting, revising, and reflecting on instructional plans, but the direct evidence base is still dominated by pre-service teacher studies, higher-education feedback experiments, and mixed-method prototypes rather than rigorous K–12 comparisons against human coaching, peer feedback, or business-as-usual planning supports. Broad reviews of AI in teacher education and K–12 genAI adoption consistently describe growth in teacher-facing uses such as lesson planning, reflection, feedback generation, and professional learning, while also emphasizing fragmented designs, short intervention windows, and unresolved risks around hallucination, pedagogical shallowness, bias, privacy, and overreliance [5] [9] [3] [15] [6] [31] [16]. The brief’s central question is therefore only partially answered: genAI can support teacher planning work, but there is little high-confidence causal evidence that it outperforms standard human coaching on teacher behavior or student outcomes.

The strongest experimental evidence is mostly indirect. A meta-analysis on ChatGPT in education found overall positive effects on student learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking, but this evidence is student-facing rather than teacher-coaching specific [2]. A meta-analysis of AI-enabled personalized STEM education in schools likewise supports positive effects of AI-supported learning environments, but again does not isolate teacher coaching or lesson-plan feedback interventions [1]. Among randomized studies adjacent to the brief, an RCT with first-year university language students (RCT, n=918) found that GPT-generated feedback improved revision outcomes relative to no feedback, with r=0.208, and increased motivation (r=0.29) and engagement (r=0.152) [33]. An RCT on processing ChatGPT-generated feedback in L2 writing (RCT, n=117) found that the individual-processing control group outperformed teacher-, peer-, and teacher-peer-scaffolded groups on immediate task improvement, with pairwise d=0.71 for individual processing versus teacher-scaffolded processing on T3, while teacher-involved collaborative groups showed stronger delayed learning, with d=0.66 for teacher-peer-scaffolded versus individual processing on the new task [18]. For teacher-facing feedback perceptions, an RCT with pre-service teachers (RCT, n=273) found that feedback source labels affected perceived fairness and usefulness, but not actual uptake once objective feedback quality was controlled; reported coefficients included fairness interaction b=1.21, usefulness interaction b=1.76, and uptake predictor b=1.27, R²=.078 [19]. Another RCT on feedback-provider information (RCT, n=169) found higher trustworthiness ratings for LLM-attributed feedback than educator-attributed feedback, beta=0.58 overall, with provider information improving perceptions overall (beta=0.53) and affective reactions (beta=0.79) [37]. The one directly teacher-planning RCT identified, with prospective physics teachers (RCT, n=26), found no significant difference in task correctness between ChatGPT and textbook conditions, but lower scores for ChatGPT-supported tasks on clarity (d=0.38) and context (d=1.04), despite high usability ratings (mean 4.1/5) [22]. Taken together, the experimental record suggests that AI feedback can be usable and influential, but that human mediation and task structure matter, and direct evidence of superiority for teacher lesson planning is absent.

The strongest quasi-experimental and implementation evidence is suggestive but still limited. A quasi-experimental longitudinal intervention in vocational teacher education directly examined AI feedback for lesson planning against standard human feedback, making it among the closest studies to the brief, but the source summary reports no extractable effect size, sample size, or exact outcome statistic, limiting firm inference [27]. Mixed-method lesson-planning studies with pre-service teachers show that teachers use ChatGPT to generate ideas, structure lessons, align materials, and anticipate misconceptions, yet also report inaccuracies, generic advice, and a need for verification and human judgment [42] [39]. In low-resource school contexts, a mixed-method study of Shiksha Copilot describes teacher-AI collaboration for curating and customizing lesson plans under workload and multilingual constraints, indicating practical demand for hybrid planning support, but without causal outcome estimates in the provided summary [29]. Comparative and co-design studies similarly indicate that teachers often value AI as a drafting partner rather than a replacement for expertise, and that preferences for AI- versus human-created lesson plans vary by lesson component and context [43] [40]. Broader adjacent quasi-experimental evidence from AI-assisted writing and programming studies shows that AI feedback can improve output quality or process measures under some conditions, but these populations are students rather than teachers and should not be treated as direct evidence about instructional coaching [14] [35] [24].

Across the tiered sub-questions, Tier 1 is answered partially: the literature defines the intervention broadly as teachers using LLM-based systems such as ChatGPT and related tools for idea generation, plan drafting, automated critique, reflection, and professional learning, but the boundary between “instructional coaching” and “lesson-plan feedback” is often blurred [5] [3] [9]. Tier 2 is answered only partially: studies use counterfactuals such as textbook support, no feedback, teacher feedback, peer scaffolding, and human feedback, but there is little direct K–12 evidence comparing genAI to routine school coaching or rubric-based self-review [22] [33] [18] [27]. Tier 3 is answered partially: implementation themes recur clearly, including iterative prompting, template use, curriculum alignment problems, human-in-the-loop review, and the importance of provider framing and feedback literacy [42] [39] [40] [37]. Tier 4 remains largely unresolved: effect sizes are available for several adjacent RCTs, but there is almost no robust experimental evidence on teacher lesson planning quality, coaching uptake, self-efficacy, or downstream student learning in real K–12 coaching systems [22] [19] [38].

Overall confidence is low to moderate. Confidence is higher that genAI can provide rapid, scalable, and often acceptable feedback-like support; lower that it improves teacher planning better than human coaching; and very low that it improves student learning through teacher coaching pathways. The single most important caveat is construct mismatch: much of the available literature evaluates student-facing AI feedback, pre-service artifact production, or perceptions of AI-generated advice rather than actual teacher behavior change after receiving genAI coaching in schools [6] [4] [5] [9].

## Research Architecture & Open Questions

### Field Anchors

The citation structure points to two pre-2023 works as the clearest foundations for the present literature on teachers’ use of generative AI for coaching-like support and lesson-plan feedback. The first, “Characterization of Models for Identifying Physical and Cognitive” (2020), appears to function as a methodological anchor for later education-facing reviews and design papers, especially those concerned with how AI systems are characterized, interpreted, or positioned for instructional use [17], [21], [27]. In practice, later work seems to depend on it less for teacher coaching specifically than for a broader framework for thinking about model capabilities and educational applicability. The second anchor, “Evaluating Large Language Models Trained on Code” (2021), appears to establish an evaluation logic for large language model performance that is then taken up in work on code generation and broader generative AI surveys [25], [30]. For the teacher-feedback topic, its importance is indirect but still consequential: it underwrites expectations that model outputs can be assessed systematically and compared across tasks before being embedded in educational support workflows.

More immediate domain-specific grounding comes from adjacent literatures rather than from a mature teacher-coaching lineage. Peer feedback and observation research provides a non-AI baseline for instructional improvement [22]. Reviews of AI in higher education, ChatGPT in education, K–12 use, and teacher professional development establish the surrounding conceptual and implementation landscape into which teacher-facing generative AI feedback studies now fit [17], [15], [16], [20], [21], [9].

### Research Gaps

The clearest gap is evidentiary rather than conceptual. The network identifies an active subfield with substantial momentum but no randomized controlled trial evidence. That matters directly for teacher coaching and lesson-plan feedback, where many recent papers are reviews, mappings, or exploratory studies rather than causal tests of effectiveness [4], [5], [6], [9], [15], [20]. There is some emerging work on AI-supported lesson planning in vocational teacher education [28], [29] and on pre-service teachers’ perceptions of AI versus expert or peer feedback [19], but modern extensions remain thin for in-service K–12 teachers, especially in school-based coaching contexts. English language learner contexts are also flagged as underrepresented in the hypothesis surface, and current extensions appear more concentrated on student writing and language-learning applications than on teachers as feedback recipients [10], [14], [27]. Outcome coverage is similarly narrow: the literature appears stronger on perceptions, revisions, and use cases than on observed instructional quality, transfer into classroom practice, or sustained professional learning gains.

### Hypothesis Surfaces

One testable hypothesis is that in-service K–12 teachers who receive generative AI lesson-plan feedback will produce higher-quality lesson plans than teachers receiving business-as-usual self-revision alone. The population would be in-service K–12 teachers, the intervention would be structured LLM-based feedback on lesson plans, the primary outcome would be rubric-scored lesson-plan quality, and the appropriate design would be a randomized controlled trial.

A second hypothesis is that pre-service teachers receiving generative AI feedback plus human coaching will outperform those receiving AI-only feedback on both revision quality and later instructional enactment. The population would be pre-service teachers, the intervention would be hybrid AI-plus-coach feedback, the outcomes would be lesson-plan revision quality and observation-based teaching performance, and the design should be a multi-arm randomized trial including AI-only, human-only, and hybrid conditions [19], [22].

A third hypothesis is that K–12 teachers serving English language learners will benefit more from domain-tailored generative AI coaching prompts than from general-purpose prompts. The intervention would be specialized AI feedback for ELL-responsive planning, the outcome would be rubric-based measures of instructional responsiveness and plan quality, and the design should be a comparative randomized trial.

## Defining the intervention and the scope of the evidence

The intervention in this brief covers teacher-facing uses of generative AI for professional support rather than student-facing tutoring. Across reviews of teacher education and K–12 genAI use, teacher-facing applications are typically described as lesson-plan drafting, automated critique of plans or materials, idea generation, reflective dialogue, differentiation suggestions, assessment creation, and professional learning support [5] [9] [3] [15]. However, the literature often collapses distinct functions under broad labels such as “teacher support” or “professional development,” making it difficult to separate automated lesson-plan feedback from fuller instructional coaching relationships [6] [7] [20].

That distinction matters. Lesson-plan feedback usually refers to comments on an instructional artifact, such as alignment, clarity, sequencing, differentiation, or assessment fit. Instructional coaching generally implies a broader improvement process, often iterative and relational, involving diagnosis, feedback, reflection, and revision of practice. The closest teacher-facing genAI studies in the pool tend to address artifact critique or planning support rather than sustained coaching cycles [27] [42] [39]. Reviews of teacher education similarly note that many AI applications help with planning and reflection, but only a smaller subset resembles dialogic coaching or repeated professional guidance [5] [3].

The outcome domain is also heterogeneous. Studies in this area variously assess artifact quality, usability, teacher perceptions, efficiency, self-efficacy, feedback trust, feedback uptake, revision frequency, and in a few adjacent cases student learning or writing performance [4] [37] [19] [33]. This means that evidence on whether AI can produce feedback is much more abundant than evidence on whether teachers change instructional decisions after receiving that feedback. Reviews of educational genAI repeatedly identify this gap between output quality and behavioral or classroom impact [31] [6] [9].

The studied populations are skewed. Pre-service teachers appear frequently in lesson-planning and task-development studies [22] [42] [38]. Teacher-education contexts and higher-education settings are better represented than practicing K–12 instructional coaching systems [5] [17]. K–12-focused reviews show growing school use of genAI, but not a mature causal evidence base centered on in-service teachers receiving coaching-like support in schools [9] [16].

## What the comparison conditions look like

Because the literature is fragmented, baseline conditions vary substantially. In the most direct teacher-planning RCT identified, prospective physics teachers were assigned to ChatGPT support or a traditional textbook-based approach, making textbook support the counterfactual rather than human coaching [22]. In adjacent automated-feedback RCTs, comparison groups include no feedback [33], different modes of scaffolding around the same AI feedback [18], or differing source labels for equivalent feedback [19] [37].

This is notably different from the standard counterfactuals used in teacher improvement systems. Reviews of peer observation and feedback describe conventional supports as human observation, peer review, coaching conversations, principal evaluation, and collaborative reflection, all of which are labor-intensive and socially situated rather than automated [21]. Reviews of teacher professional development for genAI likewise position AI as an add-on to, not a replacement for, human mentoring and ethics-oriented PD [15]. The vocational teacher education study is especially important because it explicitly compares AI feedback with standard human feedback for lesson planning, but the source summary does not provide the sample size or exact comparative statistics, so its contribution is directional rather than definitive [27].

Several broad reviews confirm that teacher-facing genAI studies seldom compare AI against strong school-based alternatives such as district instructional coaching, lesson study, structured peer coaching, or rubric-guided collaborative planning [5] [9] [31]. Instead, many studies compare AI-supported work to weaker baselines such as solo work, no feedback, or noninteractive resources. This makes the existing evidence more informative about whether genAI can offer some support than whether it can outperform established human systems.

## Experimental evidence most relevant to the brief

### Meta-analytic evidence

A meta-analysis of AI-enabled personalized STEM education in schools synthesized evidence that AI-supported personalized STEM education has positive effects in school settings, but it does not isolate teacher-facing coaching or lesson-plan feedback interventions, so it is only indirect evidence for this brief [1]. It is still useful for showing that AI-supported educational interventions can improve outcomes in school contexts, but not for determining whether teachers receiving genAI coaching change planning quality.

A second meta-analysis examined the effect of ChatGPT on students’ learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking across elementary, secondary, and undergraduate populations [2]. This is also indirect. It shows that ChatGPT-based educational uses can produce positive student-level outcomes under some conditions, but it does not answer whether teacher coaching or lesson feedback is the mechanism.

Other reviews are more descriptive than quantitative. A systematic review of empirical research on ChatGPT in education found that the literature was early, mixed, and concentrated in exploratory studies rather than robust evaluations [6]. A systematic review of genAI use in K–12 education similarly maps applications and reported impacts but does not provide pooled causal estimates specific to teacher coaching [9]. Reviews focused on teacher education, language teacher education, and AI integration in teacher education all conclude that teacher-facing genAI use is expanding rapidly while rigorous comparative evidence remains limited [5] [3] [15].

### Randomized evidence on AI feedback and teacher-facing outcomes

The most directly teacher-relevant randomized study in the pool is the prospective physics teacher experiment. In Physics task development of prospective physics teachers using ChatGPT (Randomized Controlled Trial, n=26), participants used either ChatGPT 3.5 or textbooks to develop physics assessment tasks [22]. Task correctness did not differ significantly between conditions. However, tasks developed with ChatGPT scored lower on clarity, with d=0.38, and substantially lower on context, with d=1.04 [22]. Participants rated ChatGPT usability highly at mean 4.1/5, but output quality was rated below neutral at mean 2.76/5 and usefulness at mean 3.24/5 [22]. This is important because it directly tests teacher artifact production and does not show a planning-quality advantage for genAI.

In AI, Expert or Peer? Examining the Impact of Perceived Feedback Source on Pre-Service Teachers Feedback Perception and Uptake (Randomized Controlled Trial, n=273), pre-service teachers received feedback with manipulated source labels, allowing the study to isolate effects of perceived source on perceptions and uptake [19]. Feedback source strongly affected perceptions but not actual uptake once objective feedback quality was controlled; reported statistics included fairness interaction b=1.21, usefulness interaction b=1.76, uptake predictor b=1.27, and R²=.078 [19]. This matters for coaching implementation because source framing may shape teachers’ receptivity even when behavior does not change correspondingly.

In Embracing LLM Feedback: the role of feedback providers and provider information for feedback effectiveness (Randomized Controlled Trial, n=169), participants rated feedback from the LLM more positively on trustworthiness than feedback from the educator, beta=0.58 overall, including expertise beta=0.74, benevolence beta=0.62, and integrity beta=0.39 [37]. Provider information improved feedback message perceptions overall, beta=0.53, and affective reactions, beta=0.79 [37]. Without provider information, the LLM was rated as less fair than the educator, d=0.53, but this gap fell to d=0.09 when provider information was given [37]. Exploratory analyses also showed that higher feedback literacy was associated with better feedback message perceptions, R²=0.05, but lower perceived trustworthiness, R²=0.04 [37]. Again, this is not evidence that lesson plans improve, but it does show that implementation details affect how AI feedback is received.

### Randomized evidence adjacent to the brief

In Enhancing university level English proficiency with generative AI: Empirical insights into automated feedback and learning outcomes (Randomized Controlled Trial, n=918), first-year university language students who received GPT-generated feedback improved more from original to revised essays than the no-feedback control group, with effect r=0.208 [33]. They also reported higher motivation, r=0.29, and engagement, r=0.152; positive emotion differences were not statistically significant, r=0.135 [33]. This study shows that genAI-generated feedback can affect revision quality and engagement at scale, but because recipients were students rather than teachers, it is only indirect support for teacher-facing planning feedback.

In Comparing individual vs. collaborative processing of ChatGPT-generated feedback: Effects on L2 writing task improvement and learning (Randomized Controlled Trial, n=117), students were assigned to individual processing or teacher-, peer-, or teacher-peer-scaffolded processing of ChatGPT-generated feedback [18]. On task improvement from draft to final product, the individual-processing control group outperformed scaffolded groups, with pairwise d=0.71 for individual processing versus teacher-scaffolded processing on T3 [18]. On learning progression across tasks including a delayed new task, teacher-involved collaborative groups performed better, with pairwise d=0.66 for teacher-peer-scaffolded versus individual processing on the new task [18]. This is relevant because it suggests that AI feedback may support immediate revision differently from longer-term learning, and that human mediation can either slow immediate gains or deepen later transfer.

In Mindset matters: Fostering teachers’ responsible AI use through professional development (Randomized Controlled Trial, n=57 preservice teachers), both groups showed reduced AI anxiety over time, but only the tools-only group had a statistically significant pre-post decrease; the summary reports η2p=.05 [38]. Overall competence self-efficacy did not differ significantly by time or group, although the tools-only group showed a significant pre-post increase and the summary reports a motivation-related predictor η2p=.11 [38]. This is relevant to teacher self-efficacy and professional learning, but it does not test lesson-plan feedback effectiveness directly.

## Quasi-experimental and mixed-method evidence closest to teacher planning

The most directly relevant quasi-experimental study is How AI feedback supports lesson planning in vocational teacher education: a longitudinal intervention study using an analytical AI platform (Quasi-Experimental Design) [27]. The study explicitly investigates whether an analytical AI feedback platform can support lesson planning in vocational teacher education and whether its effects differ from standard human feedback [27]. That direct comparator makes it highly relevant to the brief. However, the source summary does not provide sample size, effect size, or exact outcome statistic, so the report can only conclude that this is one of the few head-to-head evaluations in the field, not what the magnitude or direction of impact was.

Several mixed-method studies provide richer implementation detail. Using ChatGPT for Science Learning: A Study on Pre-service Teachers' Lesson Planning (Mixed-Methods) examines how pre-service elementary science teachers incorporated ChatGPT into lesson planning and how they perceived its usefulness and risks [42]. The study is valuable for showing actual teacher workflow use: pre-service teachers used ChatGPT in planning, but also surfaced concerns about accuracy and suitability, implying that verification and pedagogical judgment remained necessary [42].

Exploring the potential of LLM to enhance teaching plans through teaching simulation (Mixed-Methods) focuses on using LLMs to improve teaching plans before instruction by helping teachers anticipate misconceptions, learning difficulties, and possible instructional adjustments [39]. This is one of the clearest studies on AI as pre-instruction planning support rather than as student tutor. The summary indicates a planning-support focus, but does not provide numeric effects, so the study supports mechanism and feasibility claims rather than effectiveness claims [39].

Shiksha Copilot: Teacher-AI Collaboration for Curating and Customizing Lesson Plans in Low-Resource Schools (Mixed-Methods) addresses lesson-plan customization under heavy workload, multilingual instruction, and limited pedagogical support in low-resource schools [29]. This is important for equity and implementation because it places genAI in a context where conventional coaching resources may be scarce. Yet the source summary does not provide outcome statistics, so the evidence is best treated as design and feasibility evidence.

AI as a Teaching Partner: Early Lessons from Classroom Codesign with Secondary Teachers (Mixed-Methods) examines how secondary teachers used a generative AI platform designed as a teaching partner and how their judgments shaped adoption [40]. This supports the view that teacher uptake depends not only on output quality but on fit with planning routines and teacher agency. Connecting Feedback to Choice: Understanding Educator Preferences in GenAI vs. Human-Created Lesson Plans in K-12 Education – A Comparative Analysis (Mixed-Methods) further shows that educators compare AI-generated and human-created lesson plans component by component rather than making a simple all-or-nothing judgment [43]. These studies inform Tier 2 and Tier 3 by clarifying likely comparison logics and workflow realities, but they are not impact evaluations.

## What implementation looks like in practice

Across the literature, teachers most often use genAI in planning as a first-draft generator, brainstorming partner, reviser, or critique engine rather than as an autonomous coach. Reviews of K–12 genAI use, AI tool applications, and teacher-education integration all describe teachers using tools such as ChatGPT to draft lesson components, create examples, generate questions, differentiate materials, align tasks to standards, and reflect on possible improvements [9] [16] [5]. Language teacher education reviews similarly show ChatGPT being used for reflection, material creation, and pedagogical support rather than only for direct student interaction [3].

Several sources suggest that iterative prompting and contextualization are central implementation features. Mixed-method lesson-planning studies indicate that teachers refine outputs through repeated prompting, clarification, and adaptation to subject and student context [42] [39]. Co-design work with secondary teachers similarly portrays value emerging when the AI is embedded in collaborative planning workflows rather than used as a one-shot generator [40]. Reviews of pedagogical conversational agents and tutoring systems reinforce this implementation logic: better educational outcomes tend to depend on structured interaction design, clear pedagogical roles, and scaffolding rather than raw model capability alone [34] [13].

Guardrails and grounding matter because generic output is a recurrent limitation. Reviews of ChatGPT in education and genAI-enhanced education repeatedly cite hallucination, weak contextual judgment, shallow pedagogical reasoning, and misalignment with curriculum or local standards as central risks [6] [20] [7]. The South Africa scoping review argues that genAI may help address teacher preparation constraints, but also implies the need for contextual adaptation in under-resourced settings where imported or generic content may not fit local realities [8]. The PearlAI framework similarly emphasizes contextual and equity-oriented design rather than generic deployment [30].

Provider framing and feedback literacy also shape implementation. The feedback-perception RCTs show that who the feedback is said to come from, and what information accompanies it, changes teacher perceptions of fairness, expertise, usefulness, and trustworthiness [19] [37]. This suggests that successful school implementation would require explicit norms for disclosure, explanation, and human oversight rather than simply making AI feedback available.

## Mechanisms: why genAI might help, and why it might fail

The most plausible positive mechanism in this literature is feedback scale and speed. Reviews of automated feedback in higher education and programming education consistently argue that AI can deliver rapid, individualized comments at a volume that human providers often cannot sustain [4] [25]. Applied to teacher planning, this implies potential gains in time efficiency, more revision opportunities, and lower access barriers to formative critique, especially where human coaching is scarce [27] [29].

A second mechanism is cognitive prompting for reflection. Teacher-focused studies describe genAI as helping users surface alternatives, anticipate misconceptions, or structure a planning conversation that might otherwise not occur [39] [42]. Reviews of AI integration in teacher education suggest that this reflective function may be especially useful for novice teachers who need examples, scaffolds, and fast feedback on draft ideas [5] [15].

A third mechanism is increased revision activity. Although not teacher-facing, the AI-assisted feedback study with postgraduate EFL learners showed positive correlations between use of generative AI features and revision counts, with r=0.398 to 0.421, and higher AI use increased explained variance in post-test writing scores by ΔR2=0.113 in hierarchical regression [10]. This is indirect but consistent with the idea that AI may increase productive revision behavior by lowering the cost of feedback cycles.

The main failure mechanisms are equally clear. Systematic reviews warn that AI feedback can be superficially plausible but pedagogically weak, inaccurate, or overconfident [6] [20] [36]. The physics teacher RCT provides direct evidence of this risk: ChatGPT-assisted task development did not improve correctness and produced lower clarity and contextual quality than textbook support [22]. Reviews of overreliance on AI dialogue systems and of critical thinking in EFL education further caution that frequent dependence on AI outputs may weaken independent judgment or reduce deeper cognitive engagement if not carefully scaffolded [36] [11].

## Effects on lesson quality, pedagogical decisions, efficiency, self-efficacy, and uptake

### Lesson quality and pedagogical decision-making

Direct evidence on lesson-plan quality is scarce. The vocational teacher education QED directly targets lesson planning, but no effect size is reported in the source summary [27]. The prospective physics teacher RCT found no correctness advantage and worse performance on clarity and context in the ChatGPT condition, which cuts against strong claims that genAI improves planning quality by default [22]. Mixed-method lesson-planning studies nonetheless suggest that genAI can help generate workable drafts and expose alternatives for revision [42] [39]. The most defensible synthesis is therefore conditional: genAI may support planning processes, but direct evidence of improved pedagogical quality over conventional supports is weak and mixed.

### Efficiency and workflow support

Efficiency is more often claimed than measured. Reviews of AI tools in K–12 education, automated feedback systems, and pedagogical conversational agents repeatedly describe speed, scalability, and workload reduction as key advantages [16] [4] [34]. Mixed-method studies with teachers in authentic or quasi-authentic planning settings also foreground workload relief and rapid drafting as reasons teachers value AI tools [29] [40]. However, the source pool contains little exact comparative time-on-task data for teacher lesson planning, so claims about efficiency should remain cautious.

### Teacher self-efficacy and professional confidence

Evidence on self-efficacy is thin and not strongly positive. In the preservice teacher PD RCT, overall competence self-efficacy did not differ significantly by time or group, though the tools-only group showed a significant pre-post increase and a reported η2p=.11 on a motivation-related predictor [38]. Reviews of teacher education and professional development suggest that genAI can increase perceived readiness or confidence when paired with structured PD, but these claims are largely descriptive rather than causal [5] [15].

### Feedback uptake and receptivity

Feedback uptake is one of the better-measured mechanisms, but still mostly through pre-service or higher-education studies. The pre-service teacher RCT found that perceptions of source changed fairness and usefulness judgments but not actual uptake once feedback quality was controlled [19]. The provider-information RCT similarly found that explanatory framing improved perceptions and affective reactions to feedback [37]. These findings suggest that uptake may depend less on whether feedback is AI-generated per se than on the quality of the message and the framing conditions under which it is introduced.

### Downstream student learning

Direct evidence on downstream student learning from teacher-facing genAI coaching is essentially absent in the source pool. Meta-analyses show positive student outcomes from AI-enabled or ChatGPT-supported learning interventions [1] [2], but those interventions are not teacher coaching studies. Reviews of teacher-facing genAI repeatedly identify downstream classroom and student outcomes as under-measured [5] [9] [31].

## Variation across populations, settings, and conditions

Variation by teacher experience is plausible but not well established. Reviews suggest that novice and pre-service teachers may benefit more from genAI’s scaffolding, examples, and structuring support because they have less prior repertoire to draw on [5] [3]. At the same time, lower expertise may increase vulnerability to accepting inaccurate or generic suggestions uncritically [6] [36]. The current evidence does not provide strong subgroup estimates comparing novice and experienced teachers directly.

Subject matter also likely matters. Language education and EFL contexts are overrepresented in both primary studies and reviews [3] [11] [26]. Science and mathematics appear in lesson-planning, assessment-development, and AI-use reviews, but direct coaching evidence remains sparse [42] [22] [12]. Vocational education has one relevant QED on lesson planning [27]. There is little direct evidence for social studies, arts, physical education, or special education in the present pool.

Context and resourcing likely shape value. The low-resource school study suggests a stronger practical case for AI support where human coaching and planning infrastructure are constrained [29]. The South Africa scoping review similarly frames genAI as potentially compensatory in contexts with teacher preparation gaps, though without rigorous outcome testing [8]. Yet low-resource settings may also face larger risks around contextual mismatch, limited verification capacity, or governance gaps [30].

Human oversight appears to be a key moderator. The collaborative-processing RCT suggests that human scaffolding can change whether AI feedback helps immediate performance or longer-term learning [18]. Co-design and mixed-method teacher studies likewise portray AI as most useful when embedded in human review, adaptation, and local curriculum judgment [40] [39]. The overall pattern does not support stand-alone deployment as a replacement for expert coaching.

## Risks, tradeoffs, and reasons for caution

The risk literature is broader than the impact literature. Systematic reviews on ChatGPT in education, overreliance on AI dialogue systems, and broad genAI-in-education syntheses repeatedly identify hallucinations, factual inaccuracy, bias, privacy concerns, opaque data use, and shallow or homogenized outputs as major threats [6] [36] [7] [20]. Teacher-facing reviews add ethical and professional concerns, including reduced autonomy, deskilling, and uncertainty about responsible use in planning and assessment [15] [5].

Some of these risks are directly visible in the most relevant intervention studies. The physics teacher RCT found worse contextual quality for ChatGPT-supported task development [22]. Mixed-method planning studies report that outputs often need correction and adaptation before classroom use [42] [39]. Comparative preference studies indicate that teachers do not uniformly prefer AI-created plans over human-created ones, implying that usefulness is component-specific and conditional [43].

The literature also warns against conflating acceptability with effectiveness. Teachers or learners may trust, like, or quickly use AI feedback even when objective performance gains are limited. The RCTs on source attribution and provider information show that perceptions can move independently from uptake or learning [19] [37]. This is especially important for school procurement and implementation decisions.

### Synthesis and Implications

The evidence supports a restrained synthesis. Generative AI can plausibly function as a scalable first-pass support for teacher lesson planning and feedback, especially by accelerating drafting, increasing access to critique, and prompting revision or reflection [9] [4] [5]. But the evidence does not yet establish that genAI-based instructional coaching or automated lesson-plan feedback improves teacher planning quality more than conventional human coaching or peer feedback in real K–12 settings. The best direct experimental evidence on teacher planning is mixed to negative, with no correctness advantage and worse clarity and context in one RCT of prospective teachers [22]. The strongest positive causal evidence comes from adjacent feedback domains, not school-based teacher coaching [33] [18].

For practice, the most defensible implication is to use genAI as augmentation, not substitution. Schools and teacher educators should treat AI-generated lesson feedback as a draftable input that can widen idea sets and reduce turnaround time, while maintaining human review for contextual fit, curriculum alignment, and pedagogical judgment [40] [42] [39]. Implementation should include clear disclosure, prompt templates tied to local standards, guidance on verification, and structures for discussing AI suggestions with mentors or peers [37] [15]. Where coaching capacity is limited, especially in under-resourced settings, genAI may have practical value as an access-expanding support, but current evidence does not justify replacing human instructional coaches [29] [8].

The research priorities are also clear. Future studies should compare genAI feedback directly with human coaching, peer review, rubric-only self-review, and business-as-usual planning in authentic schools; measure teacher revision behavior, instructional enactment, and student outcomes; and test how results vary by teacher experience, subject, and level of human oversight [5] [9] [31]. Hybrid models in which AI produces draft critiques that are then moderated by coaches appear especially promising, but they remain under-tested.

### Limitations and Research Gaps

The main limitation is the thinness of direct evidence. There are very few Randomized Controlled Trial or Quasi-Experimental Design studies directly focused on teachers receiving genAI lesson-plan feedback or coaching, and even fewer in K–12 schools. Much of the cited evidence is indirect, coming from student feedback studies, pre-service task-development experiments, or descriptive teacher-use research [6] [5] [9].

Methodological weaknesses are common. Many studies are single-site, short-duration, mixed-method, or exploratory, often without strong counterfactuals or reported effect sizes [42] [39] [40]. Even one of the most relevant comparative studies, the vocational teacher education QED, lacks extractable sample and outcome statistics in the source summary [27]. Publication timing is another constraint: because the field is very recent, reviews often synthesize early empirical work with heterogeneous tools, prompts, and outcomes [31] [6].

Population coverage is uneven. Pre-service teachers, language education, and higher education are overrepresented, while experienced in-service K–12 teachers, elementary contexts, special education, multilingual learners, rural schools, and instructional coaches themselves are underrepresented [3] [5] [9]. Student learning outcomes linked specifically to teacher-facing genAI coaching are largely absent [31].

Finally, several key policy and implementation questions remain unresolved: how to audit AI-generated advice, how to protect privacy in planning workflows, how to align outputs to local curriculum, how to prevent overreliance, and how to divide labor productively between AI tools and human coaches [15] [20] [36] [16]. Until those issues are addressed with stronger field-based comparative studies, conclusions about effectiveness should remain cautious.
