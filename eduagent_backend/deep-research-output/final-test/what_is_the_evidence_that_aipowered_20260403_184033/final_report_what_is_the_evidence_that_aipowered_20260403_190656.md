## Executive Summary

The evidence on AI-powered tools for scientific reasoning and inquiry in K-12 education is best characterized as promising but still indirect and incomplete. Direct evidence for the focal question—whether AI tools improve K-12 students’ scientific reasoning, inquiry skills, evidence evaluation, hypothesis generation, experimental design, data interpretation, explanation construction, and metacognition relative to standard or alternative science instruction—is thin. The strongest direct K-12 science evidence in the source pool comes from studies of AI-related tutoring, personalized science text adaptation, and simulation-supported inquiry, but few studies directly compare AI-powered science tools against clearly specified non-AI alternatives on validated scientific-reasoning outcomes. Broader syntheses of intelligent tutoring systems and AI-enabled personalized STEM education indicate that AI-supported instructional systems can improve K-12 learning overall, including in STEM, but these reviews are not specific enough to establish a mature causal case for K-12 scientific inquiry outcomes in science classrooms ([1]; [2]; [4]; [9]). The most defensible conclusion is therefore conditional: structured, scaffolded, and teacher-mediated AI systems are more plausible supports for science reasoning than unguided generic generative AI, but the field has not yet produced enough direct comparative K-12 science trials to answer the brief fully.

The strongest experimental evidence is mixed in relevance. A meta-analysis of K-12 ITS in the U.S. reports overall positive effects of intelligent tutoring systems, while explicitly emphasizing heterogeneity by design, learner, setting, and outcome; the summary provided does not report a pooled effect size in the source pool, so the direction is positive but the exact statistic is not available here (meta-analysis) ([1]). A second meta-analysis of AI-enabled personalized STEM education in schools also reports positive overall impacts with moderation by context and implementation, but again the exact pooled effect is not reported in the source pool summary (meta-analysis) ([2]). For individual trials, the most directly science-relevant randomized study in the pool is the GPT-4 science-text personalization trial: in a small RCT with middle-school learners (n=23), students in the experimental condition were more likely to choose GPT-4-rewritten science paragraphs that matched their inferred preferences, with g=0.715, but they could not reliably identify their own true learner profile, with h=0.615 and p=.405; this speaks to personalization validity more than to science learning gains (RCT, n=23) ([19]). A high-school inquiry-environment RCT with animated pedagogical agents found substantial pre-post gains in kinematics knowledge across all conditions, d=1.10, but no significant condition effect for the agent manipulation, indicating that the inquiry environment itself mattered more than the added agent support in this study (RCT, n=61) ((Kim, 2015)). A middle-school PhET study, labeled in the source pool as an RCT though described in the summary as a one-group pretest-posttest design, reported science process skill N-gain=0.71 and knowledge N-gain=0.74 for 30 grade-8 students, but without a clearly specified non-AI comparison group this cannot establish comparative effectiveness of AI versus standard instruction (RCT per source pool, n=30) ([35]). Outside science but still informative about AI-enabled instruction design, the Lumilo classroom trial found higher learning gains for students in the Glasses + Analytics condition in middle school math and showed that AI changed teacher attention allocation in ways consistent with better decision-making (RCT, n=286 analyzed) ([5]). Tutor CoPilot found that students whose tutors had AI support were 4 percentage points more likely to pass exit tickets across 4,136 K-12 tutoring sessions, while also increasing deeper instructional strategies by about 2 SD on the log-odds scale (RCT, n=1,800 students) ([11]). These adjacent trials strengthen the case for AI as a scaffold for human instruction, but they do not directly establish science inquiry effects.

The strongest quasi-experimental and observational evidence both corroborates and complicates the experimental picture. A high-school STEM quasi-experiment found that generative AI without literacy support underperformed compared with control and search-engine conditions in physics-related coursework, and survey differences in engagement and enjoyment were small and non-significant, suggesting that unguided GenAI can be ineffective or harmful in secondary STEM learning (QED, n=171; survey n=60) ([49]). By contrast, a high-school simulation study found large post-test differences favoring the simulation condition on attitudes toward scientific inquiry, partial η2=.748, with similarly large effects for enjoyment and career interest, but this was a computer-simulation intervention rather than a clearly AI-powered one (QED, n=90) ([44]). Broad evidence on computer-based scaffolding in STEM problem-based learning and scaffolding in STEM more generally supports the mechanism that structured prompts, adaptive feedback, and support for ill-structured problem solving can improve learning, which is highly relevant to scientific inquiry design even when the tools are not always labeled AI ((Belland, 2017); (Belland, 2018)). Reviews of educational chatbots, tutoring systems, and LLMs similarly indicate that learning benefits are most plausible when systems provide timely feedback, conversational prompting, or personalization within bounded instructional tasks, but they also flag inaccuracy, ethics, and overreliance risks ([37]; [46]; [24]; [31]).

Across the tiered sub-questions, the evidence answers Tier 1 partially, Tier 2 partially, Tier 3 moderately, and Tier 4 only partially. Tier 1 is partially answered because the literature reasonably identifies scientific reasoning and inquiry as including science process skills, evidence-based explanation, higher-order thinking, and problem-centered reasoning, but operationalizations vary widely and often collapse reasoning with broader science achievement or attitudes ([18]; [12]; [40]). Tier 2 is only partially answered because comparison conditions are usually business-as-usual instruction, traditional teaching, non-AI digital tools, or search engines, but the source pool contains little direct characterization of baseline K-12 science inquiry instruction or typical pre-intervention performance without AI ([49]; (Kim, 2015); [35]). Tier 3 is better answered: implementation evidence consistently points to teacher mediation, curriculum alignment, bounded tasks, scaffolding, and safeguards against blind AI reliance as important design conditions ([5]; [11]; [9]; [37]). Tier 4 remains unresolved in the narrow sense of science inquiry because direct K-12 science comparisons are too few, subgroup evidence is sparse, and long-term transfer is largely unstudied, though adjacent research suggests structured AI scaffolds may be beneficial while unguided GenAI may not be ([1]; [2]; [13]; [49]).

Overall confidence is moderate for the narrow claim that AI can support science learning processes when embedded in structured scaffolding and human-guided instruction, but low for the stronger claim that AI-powered tools reliably improve K-12 scientific reasoning and inquiry relative to standard or alternative science instruction. The single most important caveat is domain mismatch: much of the strongest causal evidence comes from adjacent areas such as math tutoring, writing feedback, general ITS, or higher education, not from direct K-12 science inquiry trials with validated scientific-reasoning outcomes ([11]; [5]; [9]; [2]).

# Research Architecture & Open Questions

## Field Anchors

The contemporary research on AI-powered tools for K-12 science reasoning rests on foundational work establishing how intelligent systems can augment rather than replace teacher practice. The 2017 study "Intelligent tutors as teachers' aides: exploring teacher needs for" established that effective educational AI must be designed with explicit attention to teacher constraints, workflows, and decision-making—a principle that subsequent work has neither abandoned nor thoroughly operationalized in science-specific contexts. The TA Framework (2020) extended this by proposing systematic real-time teaching augmentation for K-12 environments, introducing the conceptual apparatus for orchestrating multiple actors (teacher, student, system) in classroom time. Three 2019 studies on classroom orchestration and teacher-student complementarity further solidified the notion that AI tools succeed only when aligned with existing pedagogical roles and designed through participatory methods that include educators. These anchors collectively establish that technical capability alone is insufficient; integration requires understanding teacher needs, classroom ecology, and the division of cognitive labor between human and machine guidance.

## Intellectual Lineage

The current run of 60 papers extends these foundations along two generational lines. The first generation (2018–2021) translated orchestration and co-design principles into prototype systems—moving from theory to proof-of-concept implementations in science classrooms, though primarily in small-scale, facilitator-intensive settings. The second generation (2022–present) has begun scaling these prototypes and exploring specific AI modalities: generative language models, adaptive prompt engineering, and simulation-based feedback. However, the lineage shows a critical narrowing: while the foundational work emphasized teacher needs and classroom ecology across diverse student populations, recent implementations cluster heavily around well-resourced schools and advanced learners, with minimal systematic investigation of how these tools perform for students with low prior science achievement, English language learners, or students in under-resourced settings. The 60 new papers appear to deepen technical sophistication (better prompting, finer-grained feedback) but have not yet circled back to re-examine teacher integration or equity outcomes that the foundational work foregrounded.

## Research Gaps

The citation structure reveals three critical absences. First, randomized controlled trials in science inquiry settings remain scarce; the field's evidence base relies heavily on qualitative case studies and small quasi-experimental designs, leaving causal claims about student reasoning gains unverified at scale. Second, underrepresented populations—English language learners, students with disabilities, and students in rural or lower-income districts—appear in anchor citations (teacher support studies) but are nearly absent from recent AI-tool implementations. Third, longitudinal outcomes beyond immediate engagement are unexplored; no papers in the run appear to track whether AI-supported inquiry translates to sustained scientific reasoning gains, transfer to novel problems, or shifts in science identity over semesters or years.

## Hypothesis Surfaces

**Hypothesis 1**: Among sixth to eighth grade ELL students engaged in inquiry-based physical science units, AI tutoring systems providing real-time, language-scaffolded feedback on hypothesis formation will increase the frequency and syntactic complexity of scientific explanations compared to standard curriculum, measured via coded transcripts in a cluster-randomized trial across 12 classrooms over one semester.

**Hypothesis 2**: Intelligent tutoring systems designed through participatory co-design with teachers serving high-poverty schools will be adopted and used with fidelity at rates 40% higher than those designed without teacher input, measured via classroom observation and log data in a matched-pair quasi-experiment.

**Hypothesis 3**: Adaptive AI prompts calibrated to individual prior achievement levels will reduce the achievement gap in post-assessment reasoning scores between high- and low-prior-achievement students, tested via item response theory analysis in a within-subjects design across three science domains.

## Defining the intervention and outcomes

The focal intervention spans several distinct tool families: intelligent tutoring systems, conversational tutors, adaptive feedback systems, AI-supported simulations, generative AI assistants, and teacher-facing analytics or orchestration tools ([1]; [4]; [9]; [46]). The research base does not treat these as interchangeable. ITS and natural-language tutors are typically structured around bounded pedagogical models, domain representations, hinting, and dialogue management, whereas newer LLM-based tools are more open-ended, generative, and variable in reliability ([42]; [9]; [46]; [24]). That distinction matters because most of the stronger positive evidence comes from structured systems, not generic chatbot use ([1]; [11]).

Scientific reasoning and inquiry are also operationalized inconsistently across studies. In the most directly relevant K-12 science studies in the pool, outcomes include science process skills, kinematics knowledge in an inquiry environment, attitude toward scientific inquiry, and preference-aligned comprehension materials rather than a consistent battery of hypothesis generation, control-of-variables reasoning, evidence evaluation, experimental design, or explanation construction ([35]; (Kim, 2015); [44]; [19]). Broader reviews of higher-order thinking in problem- and project-based learning confirm that studies often blend higher-order thinking, critical thinking, and critical-analytic thinking into overlapping constructs, which complicates synthesis for the narrower target of scientific reasoning ([18]). Reviews of eye-tracking in science education likewise show that science-learning studies often infer reasoning from attention, processing, or task performance rather than directly measuring inquiry subskills ([12]).

The populations most represented in the available science-relevant evidence are middle and high school students, especially in physics or general STEM contexts ((Kim, 2015); [35]; [44]; [49]). Elementary science is notably underrepresented in direct studies of AI-supported inquiry in this source pool. Reviews of K-12 ITS and AI-enabled STEM personalization cover elementary through high school, but the summaries provided do not specify science-specific subgroup effects by grade band ([1]; [2]). Evidence on diverse learners, language background, disability status, and prior science achievement is especially sparse for the focal domain.

## What non-AI baseline instruction and comparison conditions look like

The source pool provides only partial evidence on how scientific reasoning and inquiry are typically developed without AI. General STEM and inquiry reviews indicate that non-AI approaches include teacher-led inquiry, problem-based learning, project-based learning, scaffolding through prompts and feedback, simulations, lab work, and authentic problem solving ((Belland, 2017); (Belland, 2018); [18]; [40]). These approaches are relevant because most AI science tools are layered on top of them rather than replacing them. The baseline problem AI is meant to address is usually not the absence of inquiry pedagogy, but the difficulty of sustaining individualized support, timely feedback, and metacognitive prompting during complex inquiry tasks ((Belland, 2017); [9]; [4]).

In direct studies, comparison conditions are usually standard instruction, conventional teaching, search engines, or non-AI digital resources rather than human tutoring or carefully matched non-AI inquiry supports. In the high-school STEM quasi-experiment, the GenAI condition was compared against both a control and a search-engine condition; the GenAI-without-literacy-support group underperformed, making this one of the clearest cautionary comparisons in the source pool (QED, n=171) ([49]). In the animated pedagogical agent study, students were assigned across three conditions within an inquiry learning environment, but all learned substantially and the condition effect on kinematics knowledge was not reported as significant, indicating that the common inquiry platform may have driven most of the gain (RCT, n=61) ((Kim, 2015)). In the science-text personalization trial, the comparator was a non-personalized text condition rather than standard classroom science teaching (RCT, n=23) ([19]).

The lack of explicit baseline-performance reporting is a major limitation. Some studies provide pretest means, such as the PhET science process skill study, where mean scores increased from 29 to 80 and knowledge from 24 to 80, but because the design details are unclear and the comparator is not clearly specified, these data describe learning trajectory rather than typical performance under business-as-usual instruction ([35]). The broader literature in the pool identifies common gaps in existing instruction—insufficient personalization, limited teacher time for feedback, and uneven support for higher-order reasoning—but does not quantify baseline K-12 science inquiry performance robustly ((Belland, 2017); [37]; [31]).

## Direct K-12 science evidence

### Randomized and quasi-experimental studies in science-related settings

The middle-school GPT-4 science personalization study is one of the few directly science-themed AI trials in the pool. This study tested whether GPT-4 could personalize science texts for diverse middle-school-age learners. In a small randomized controlled trial with n=23 students, the experimental group was more likely to choose GPT-4 rewritten science paragraphs that matched their inferred preferences, with effect g=0.715 (RCT, n=23) ([19]). However, the profile-evaluation task found that 14 of 23 students selected the true profile, which was not significantly different from chance, p=.405, with effect h=0.615 (RCT, n=23) ([19]). The study therefore provides evidence that an LLM can generate differentiated science text variants aligned with inferred profiles, but not evidence that this improves scientific reasoning, comprehension, or inquiry performance.

The animated pedagogical agent study examined a science inquiry environment in high school. In this randomized controlled trial with n=61 high-school students, all students showed significant gains on the kinematics knowledge test from pretest to posttest, with d=1.10 overall, indicating that the inquiry environment itself supported learning (RCT, n=61) ((Kim, 2015)). The study also found an overall increase in self-efficacy over time, d=0.51, but the source pool summary states that the condition effect on academic outcomes was not reported as significant (RCT, n=61) ((Kim, 2015)). This is important for the present brief: adding an AI-like or agent-based layer to inquiry did not clearly outperform other versions of the same science learning environment.

The PhET science-process-skills study focused on grade-8 science learning about light and optical instruments. The source pool labels it as an RCT, but the summary describes it as a one-group pretest-posttest study. In the reported sample of n=30, science process skills increased from a mean of 29 to 80, with N-gain=0.71, and knowledge increased from 24 to 80, with N-gain=0.74 (RCT per source pool, n=30) ([35]). Because the comparison condition is not specified in the summary, this study cannot be used as strong comparative evidence for AI or AI-like simulation support relative to standard instruction, but it does indicate that simulation-supported inquiry can coincide with substantial gains in science process outcomes.

The high-school simulation-attitudes study compared a simulation-based physics condition with a control condition. In this quasi-experimental design with n=90, the intervention improved attitudes toward scientific inquiry, with post-test scores favoring the experimental group and partial η2=.748 (QED, n=90) ([44]). It also improved enjoyment of science lessons, partial η2=.785, and career interest, partial η2=.797 (QED, n=90) ([44]). This study is useful for showing that simulation-rich science learning can strengthen inquiry-related dispositions, but it is not clearly an AI intervention and it focuses on attitudes more than reasoning performance.

The high-school generative AI STEM study is the clearest direct warning sign. This quasi-experimental study included n=171 students across AP and on-level physics. The study found that generative AI without literacy support underperformed compared with control and search-engine conditions in high-school STEM learning (QED, n=171) ([49]). Survey evidence for understanding, engagement, and enjoyment was weak and non-significant, with MANOVA p=0.115 and overlapping 95% confidence intervals across groups (QED/survey, n=60) ([49]). The paper’s practical implication was not to ban AI but to pair it with AI literacy supports and clearer policies ([49]). For the present brief, that is a direct indicator that unguided GenAI use is not a reliable route to better scientific reasoning or science learning.

### What these science studies do and do not show

Taken together, the direct science studies do not establish a strong comparative case that AI-powered tools improve K-12 scientific reasoning and inquiry beyond existing instruction. One study shows personalization capability rather than learning impact ([19]). One shows an inquiry environment with overall learning gains but no clearly superior agent condition ((Kim, 2015)). One shows positive simulation-linked process-skill gains without a specified comparator ([35]). One shows favorable attitudes toward inquiry with simulations, not clearly AI ([44]). One shows that generative AI can underperform alternatives when used without scaffolding ([49]). This pattern supports a narrow interpretation: the strongest direct evidence is more consistent with “design and implementation matter enormously” than with “AI itself improves inquiry.”

## Broader experimental and meta-analytic evidence adjacent to the focal question

### Meta-analyses and systematic reviews

The most relevant broad causal synthesis is the 2025 meta-analysis of K-12 intelligent tutoring systems in the U.S. This meta-analysis directly asks whether ITS benefit K-12 students and whether effects vary by design, student characteristics, instructional features, outcome measures, and setting (meta-analysis) ([1]). The source pool summary indicates positive overall effects and heterogeneity, but it does not provide the pooled effect size, number of studies, or science-specific moderator statistics. This makes it strong for plausibility but limited for the exact research brief.

The 2025 meta-analysis of AI-enabled personalized STEM education in schools is also highly relevant because it synthesizes quantitative evidence on AI-supported personalized learning in K-12 STEM (meta-analysis) ([2]). Again, the source pool summary does not report an exact pooled effect, science-specific breakdown, or inquiry-specific moderator, so it cannot support more precise claims than that overall effects were positive and context-dependent.

The 2018 Bayesian meta-analysis of computer-based scaffolding in problem-based STEM learning is not AI-specific, but it is central for mechanism inference. It concludes that computer-based scaffolding can improve student learning in PBL STEM environments (meta-analysis) ((Belland, 2018)). The 2017 synthesis of instructional scaffolding in STEM likewise argues that authentic problem-centered instruction is effective when paired with appropriate support structures (meta-analysis) ((Belland, 2017)). For scientific inquiry, these reviews support the idea that AI’s likely value lies in scalable scaffolding—prompts, hints, feedback, decomposition of complex tasks—rather than in open-ended answer generation.

Systematic reviews of tutoring systems and AutoTutor further support this interpretation. The tutoring-systems review describes a trajectory from computer-assisted instruction to modern AI-driven ITS and considers learning and engagement benefits alongside ethics and implementation challenges ([4]). The AutoTutor review synthesizes 17 years of natural-language tutoring research and emphasizes deep reasoning questions, collaborative dialogue, and adaptive feedback as core tutoring mechanisms ([9]). Although much of this evidence is not K-12 science-specific, these are among the most directly relevant AI mechanism literatures for explanation construction and evidence-based reasoning.

Reviews of educational chatbots and LLMs provide a more cautionary complement. The educational chatbots review shows wide variation in design, deployment, and evaluation across 36 studies and does not indicate a settled effectiveness profile ([37]). The survey of LLMs for education organizes applications across student support, teacher support, and adaptive learning but is technology-centric and not outcome-specific ([46]). The umbrella review of ChatGPT and AI chatbots in education similarly synthesizes a rapidly expanding but methodologically uneven literature ([24]). These reviews collectively suggest opportunity, but not robust direct evidence for K-12 science inquiry.

### Adjacent randomized and quasi-experimental studies

The Lumilo study is important because it tests teacher-facing AI in authentic K-12 classrooms. In a randomized classroom field study with 343 middle-school students, 286 analyzed, across 18 classrooms, 8 teachers, and 4 schools or districts, students in the Glasses + Analytics condition showed greater learning gains than comparison conditions, while teachers redistributed attention toward students needing help (RCT, n=286 analyzed) ([5]). The source pool summary does not report the exact academic effect size in the excerpt provided, so it cannot be stated here. This study is outside science, but it is highly informative for inquiry-oriented classrooms where teacher orchestration and timely support are often bottlenecks.

Tutor CoPilot provides another strong adjacent model of human-AI complementarity. In a randomized controlled trial with 1,800 K-12 students and 4,136 tutoring sessions, students whose tutors had access to Tutor CoPilot were 4 percentage points more likely to pass exit tickets (RCT, n=1,800) ([11]). The intervention also changed tutor behavior: treatment tutors were more likely to use strategies associated with deeper understanding, with effects of about 2 SD on the log-odds scale for key high-quality strategies, based on 550,000+ messages (RCT, n=4,136 sessions) ([11]). No statistically significant effects were found on perceived tutor care, confidence in learning math, or session ratings (RCT, survey n=1,931–1,952) ([11]). For the present brief, this is valuable evidence that AI may improve instructional process quality more reliably than broad affective outcomes.

Evidence from ChatGPT-related meta-analysis is similarly adjacent rather than direct. The 2025 meta-analysis on ChatGPT reports effects on learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking across elementary through undergraduate settings, but the source pool summary does not provide pooled statistics in the excerpt available here (meta-analysis) ([8]). This supports the possibility that LLMs can affect higher-order outcomes, yet the heterogeneity across tasks and domains means it cannot be mapped directly onto K-12 science inquiry.

Quasi-experimental studies outside science also reinforce the need for caution about dependence and transfer. The study of AI code generators for novice learners found that such tools can alter immediate task performance but raise concerns about overreliance and reduced independent understanding (QED) ([13]). The undergraduate programming QED on generative AI similarly examined programming output quality, transfer, self-efficacy, and process rather than simply final answers, highlighting that AI can change how students learn, not only what they score (QED) ([25]). These studies are not science studies, but they are directly relevant to scientific inquiry because inquiry requires independent planning, interpretation, and explanation, all of which may be vulnerable to overhelping.

## Mechanisms: how AI is supposed to support scientific reasoning and inquiry

Across the evidence base, the dominant mechanism is scaffolding. The scaffolding reviews show that learners in complex STEM tasks benefit when support helps them interpret problems, plan strategies, monitor progress, and connect evidence to explanations ((Belland, 2017); (Belland, 2018)). AutoTutor’s long-running research program operationalizes this through conversational moves: asking deep reasoning questions, eliciting student explanations, supplying hints, and maintaining dialogue until learners articulate better answers ([9]). These mechanisms map closely to the target science outcomes of evidence evaluation, explanation construction, and metacognition, even though many underlying studies are not K-12 science trials.

Tutoring-system reviews similarly emphasize adaptivity, dialogue, immediate feedback, and learner modeling as the defining features of AI-powered support ([4]; [42]). In the science-text personalization trial, the intended mechanism was differential access to explanatory science text tuned to learner preferences ([19]). In Tutor CoPilot and Lumilo, the mechanism was not direct student-AI interaction but improvement of human instructional decisions through AI guidance ([11]; [5]). That distinction is especially important for science inquiry, where teachers often need to decide when to let students struggle, when to redirect misconceptions, and how to manage lab-like activity.

Reviews of educational chatbots show that conversation can support question answering, practice, and feedback, but the review also underscores variation in whether systems are scripted, retrieval-based, or more open generative agents ([37]). Reviews of generative AI and critical thinking in EFL report similar uncertainty: LLMs may support reflection and idea generation, but they can also shortcut reasoning if students outsource core thinking steps ([21]). This concern aligns with the negative finding from the high-school STEM GenAI study and the overreliance concerns from programming studies ([49]; [13]).

Simulation and multimodal analytics research suggest a second mechanism family: AI-supported representation and monitoring. Simulation studies in science indicate that interactive representations can improve process skills and inquiry attitudes ([35]; [44]). Reviews of multimodal methods and eye-tracking in science education show growing interest in using fine-grained learner data to infer engagement, cognitive load, and inquiry behavior ([20]; [12]). However, these literatures mostly demonstrate measurement and design possibilities, not direct causal learning gains from AI adaptation.

## Implementation conditions and classroom integration

The most consistent implementation lesson is that AI works better as a structured support inside pedagogically designed activity systems than as a free-standing answer engine. The tutoring and ITS reviews repeatedly frame successful systems as integrated with curriculum, bounded tasks, and explicit learning goals ([1]; [4]; [9]). The high-school GenAI study directly supports this principle by showing weaker outcomes when generative AI was used without literacy support ([49]). The practical implication from that study was to use AI literacy programs and clearer classroom policies rather than unrestricted use.

Teacher role appears central. In Lumilo, AI improved learning by helping teachers better allocate attention in AI-supported instruction, a model of human-AI complementarity rather than automation ([5]). Tutor CoPilot likewise improved tutor practice by supplying real-time expert-like guidance to novice tutors ([11]). Reviews of AI in education also note that educators are often underrepresented in AI research design, despite their central role in implementation fidelity, ethics, and orchestration ([36]; [31]). For K-12 science inquiry, where activities often involve discussion, experimentation, and interpretation of ambiguous evidence, this teacher-mediation pattern is likely highly relevant.

Safeguards against error and overreliance are also recurrent. Reviews of ChatGPT and LLMs in education flag hallucination, bias, opacity, and inappropriate confidence as recurring risks ([24]; [46]; [31]). The RCT on AI-supported tutoring decision-making with adults shows that AI assistance improved accuracy over no-AI baselines but still underperformed AI alone, while explanations increased time per task and shifted both under-reliance and over-reliance depending on whether AI was correct (RCT, n=95) ((AI Knows Best, 2026)). Although not a K-12 science study, this is directly relevant to scientific inquiry because students and teachers using AI must decide when to trust or verify outputs. There is little direct evidence in the source pool on verification behavior in K-12 science, which remains a major gap.

Infrastructure and access are discussed more often than tested. Reviews of tutoring systems, chatbots, and LLMs note implementation challenges around device access, ethics, data privacy, and teacher readiness ([4]; [37]; [46]). However, the source pool contains little direct comparative evidence on dosage, duration, homework versus classroom use, or lab integration for science-specific AI inquiry tools.

## Heterogeneity by population, context, and design

Grade-band heterogeneity is largely unresolved for the focal question. The available direct science evidence clusters in middle and high school ([35]; (Kim, 2015); [19]; [49]). The broad K-12 meta-analyses include elementary through high school, but the source pool summary does not provide science-specific grade-band moderators ([1]; [2]). Elementary science inquiry with AI remains especially under-evidenced.

Prior achievement and learner-needs moderation are also thin. The brief asks about prior science achievement, language background, and special education status, but the source pool contains almost no direct science-specific moderator evidence on these variables. Work on assistive technology in inclusive classrooms suggests that technology-supported instruction can improve teachers’ inclusive practice, g=0.32, and that teachers observed greater learner responsiveness and confidence, but this is not a science inquiry study and does not isolate AI-powered supports (QED, n=220) ([39]). The science-text personalization study was explicitly motivated by diverse middle-school-age learners, but it did not report subgroup effects by language status, disability, or prior science achievement ([19]).

Design heterogeneity appears more consequential than learner heterogeneity in the current evidence. Structured tutoring systems, scaffolded dialogue, and teacher-facing AI supports show the strongest positive signals ([1]; [9]; [11]; [5]). Unguided or weakly guided generative AI shows the clearest negative signal in high-school STEM ([49]). Reviews of GenAI in programming, writing, and critical thinking echo this pattern: benefits are more likely when AI is constrained to feedback, revision, or bounded support tasks than when students rely on it to produce answers wholesale ([13]; [14]; [21]).

## What adjacent evidence suggests about likely impacts on scientific reasoning

Because direct science evidence is limited, adjacent research can only inform, not settle, the focal question. The most relevant adjacent evidence indicates that AI is likely to help scientific reasoning when it performs three functions: eliciting explanation, supplying adaptive feedback, and helping teachers target support. AutoTutor’s natural-language tutoring model is built precisely around explanation-centered dialogue ([9]). Tutor CoPilot shows that AI can increase tutor use of probing, explanation-oriented strategies at scale ([11]). Lumilo shows that AI can sharpen teacher noticing and attention allocation in complex classrooms ([5]). Scaffolding meta-analyses suggest these forms of support are especially valuable in ill-structured STEM tasks, which closely resemble scientific inquiry ((Belland, 2017); (Belland, 2018)).

At the same time, adjacent evidence also warns that AI may weaken durable inquiry skills if it bypasses core reasoning. The code-generator study highlights concerns that learners may complete tasks with less independent understanding ([13]). The adult RCT on tutoring decision-making shows that explanation features can increase both accuracy and misplaced reliance, depending on AI correctness ((AI Knows Best, 2026)). Reviews of LLMs in education repeatedly foreground hallucination and trust calibration as unresolved challenges ([46]; [24]). For science inquiry, where evaluating evidence and justifying claims are central, this means that any benefit from AI is conditional on students being asked to inspect, critique, and verify outputs rather than merely accept them.

The broader problem-based and project-based learning literature also matters here. Systematic review evidence indicates that higher-order, critical, and critical-analytic thinking in PBL and PjBL are shaped by how tasks are structured and assessed, not just by the medium of support ([18]). This implies that AI effects are unlikely to be uniform across science tasks. AI may be more helpful for prompting explanations, organizing evidence, or personalizing reading support than for authentic experimental design or hypothesis generation unless the tool is purpose-built for those subskills. The source pool does not contain enough direct studies to differentiate these subskills empirically.

### Synthesis and Implications

The evidence supports a restrained synthesis. First, AI-powered tools are not a single intervention class. Structured ITS, conversational tutors, teacher-facing orchestration tools, adaptive scaffolds, and open generative assistants differ substantially in mechanism and risk profile ([1]; [9]; [46]). Second, the strongest positive signals come from systems that scaffold reasoning within bounded pedagogical designs or that help humans provide better support, not from unguided student use of general-purpose GenAI ([11]; [5]; [49]). Third, the direct science evidence is too sparse and heterogeneous to conclude that AI reliably improves K-12 scientific reasoning and inquiry over standard instruction or non-AI alternatives.

For schools and practitioners, the practical implication is to treat AI as a scaffold for inquiry rather than as a substitute for inquiry. Tools are more plausibly useful when they provide prompts, hints, feedback on explanations, personalized reading support, or teacher-facing analytics tied to specific science tasks ((Belland, 2017); (Belland, 2018); [9]). Adoption should prioritize curriculum-aligned, structured uses over open-ended chatbot dependence, especially for younger learners and complex reasoning tasks ([37]; [46]). Where schools experiment with generative AI in science, they should pair it with AI literacy instruction, explicit verification norms, and teacher oversight, because the clearest direct K-12 STEM evidence shows that unsupported use can underperform conventional alternatives ([49]).

The strongest near-term opportunity may lie in human-AI complementarity. Teacher-facing and tutor-facing systems show that AI can improve the quality, timing, and distribution of instructional support without removing the educator from the loop ([5]; [11]). In science inquiry settings, this could mean helping teachers identify which students need support with evidence interpretation, which groups are stuck on variable control, or where misconceptions are emerging in real time. That use case is more strongly supported by current evidence than fully autonomous AI guidance for scientific reasoning.

### Limitations and Research Gaps

The central limitation is the scarcity of direct, high-rigor K-12 science studies. Very few RCTs or QEDs in the source pool directly test AI-powered tools for scientific reasoning or inquiry against clearly specified non-AI comparators in authentic science classrooms. Many relevant sources are broad reviews, adjacent-domain studies, or studies of science learning outcomes that do not isolate AI effects ([1]; [2]; [4]). Several experimental studies are small, short-term, or focused on proximal outcomes such as preference matching, motivation, or attitudes rather than validated inquiry subskills ([19]; (Kim, 2015)).

Comparator quality is also weak. Many studies compare AI tools against business-as-usual or loosely defined controls rather than teacher-led inquiry, non-AI digital scaffolds, or human feedback matched in intensity. Baseline reporting is limited, so the field cannot yet say much about what ordinary science instruction produces on the same measures or which gaps AI uniquely fills ([35]; [49]). Longitudinal and delayed post-test evidence is largely absent, so durable transfer of scientific reasoning after tool removal remains unresolved.

Equity evidence is especially underdeveloped. The source pool provides little direct evidence on students with disabilities, multilingual learners, low prior science achievement, or under-resourced schools in AI-supported science inquiry. Reviews frequently mention ethics, bias, and access, but few empirical studies test these as moderators ([24]; [46]; [31]). Elementary grades are underrepresented, and science domains beyond physics are sparse.

Finally, the field lacks mechanism-isolation studies. Many papers claim benefits from personalization, prompting, adaptive feedback, or dialogue, but few experimentally disentangle which of these features drives outcomes or when they backfire through overhelping or overreliance ((Belland, 2017); (Belland, 2018); [13]; (AI Knows Best, 2026)). Future research needs head-to-head K-12 science trials that compare AI scaffolds with equivalent non-AI scaffolds, report specific inquiry subskills, test transfer and delayed retention, and examine moderation by grade band, prior achievement, language background, disability status, and school context.

## Body of Evidence Maturity: EMERGING
Justification: The evidence base includes relevant meta-analyses and a small number of direct K-12 science trials, but the direct causal literature for AI-powered support of scientific reasoning and inquiry is still sparse, heterogeneous, and often indirect. Rigor is stronger in adjacent tutoring and scaffolding research than in the focal science domain, and equity, subgroup, comparator, and long-term outcome coverage remain weak.
