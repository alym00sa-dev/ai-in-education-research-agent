## Executive Summary

The direct evidence base on ChatGPT for middle school mathematics achievement is currently insufficient to conclude that ChatGPT improves grades 6–8 math achievement relative to standard instruction, teacher-led support, worksheets, or other common comparators. No source in the pool reports a middle-school randomized trial of ChatGPT itself against standard classroom instruction on validated math achievement outcomes. The strongest available evidence is therefore indirect: broad meta-analyses of generative AI and AI-supported mathematics learning, systematic reviews of AI in mathematics education, and adjacent school-based studies of AI tutoring or hybrid tutoring systems rather than ChatGPT specifically. Taken together, these sources suggest that AI-supported math learning often produces positive but heterogeneous effects, with outcomes depending heavily on implementation, comparator, learner characteristics, and whether the tool provides scaffolded tutoring versus answer delivery [5] [1] [3] [4] [6]. For the focal question of ChatGPT in middle school math, the evidence answers the brief only partially: it supports cautious plausibility, not a firm causal claim.

The strongest experimental findings in the source pool come from broader or adjacent interventions. A meta-analysis on generative AI and mathematics reports overall positive but heterogeneous effects across mathematics-learning studies, though the summary provided does not report a middle-school-ChatGPT-specific pooled estimate [5]. A meta-analysis of ChatGPT’s influence on learning achievement likewise synthesizes multi-age studies and indicates variation by learner age, outcome type, instructional approach, and design, but the source summary does not provide a math-specific middle-school statistic [9]. Another meta-analysis of ChatGPT in education reports effects on learning performance, perceptions, and higher-order thinking, again without a middle-school-math-specific pooled estimate in the summary [10]. The most relevant school-based causal analogue is an exploratory RCT in UK secondary mathematics: supervised LearnLM tutoring with Year 9–10 students (RCT, n=165) improved immediate remediation and misconception resolution relative to static hints, and students receiving LearnLM were 5.5 percentage points more likely than those with human tutors to answer the next unit’s first question correctly, although the confidence interval crossed zero, 95% CI [-0.014, +0.124] [34]. Static hints yielded 56.2% correct on the next unit’s first question, versus 66.2% for LearnLM and 60.7% for human tutors in the same study [34]. These findings suggest that interactive generative-AI tutoring may support transfer better than static hints under supervised conditions, but they are not direct evidence about ChatGPT or middle school.

The strongest quasi-experimental and observational evidence is similarly adjacent rather than definitive. A three-study middle-school QED on hybrid human-AI tutoring found improved process indicators rather than standardized achievement: Site 1 reported increased time spent in math software, β=0.202, 95% CI [0.057, 0.347], Site 2 reported more lessons passed, β=0.244, 95% CI [0.106, 0.381], and Site 3 reported +0.36 workspaces per hour, 95% CI [0.02, 0.70] [47]. These results indicate better engagement and learning-process intensity in middle school settings, but they do not isolate ChatGPT and do not provide validated math achievement effects. Broad reviews of AI in mathematics education, intelligent learning systems, tutoring systems, and digital technology also converge on the point that tutoring-like personalization, adaptive feedback, and teacher mediation are more consistently associated with benefit than unguided technology use [12] [15] [24] [2]. At the same time, reviews of ChatGPT and generative AI in education repeatedly flag risks of inaccuracy, shallow reasoning support, overreliance, and integrity concerns, with very limited direct measurement of these risks in middle-school math settings [16] [36] [52].

Across the tiered sub-questions, Tier 1 is answered partially: the literature defines the focal intervention broadly as conversational generative-AI support for explanations, hints, feedback, tutoring, and problem generation, but most studies do not isolate ChatGPT in middle school mathematics specifically [16] [12] [23]. Tier 2 is answered moderately well: comparator conditions in adjacent studies commonly include standard instruction, static hints, software-only support, earlier cohorts, worksheets, or no-added-support baselines [34] [47] [2]. Tier 3 is answered partially: implementation evidence consistently points to the importance of scaffolding, prompt quality, feedback design, teacher oversight, and hybrid orchestration, but direct middle-school ChatGPT implementation studies are sparse [41] [1] [35]. Tier 4 remains unresolved for the focal question: there is still no direct, high-quality evidence that ChatGPT itself improves middle school mathematics achievement versus standard alternatives.

Overall confidence is low for the focal causal claim and moderate for the narrower claim that well-scaffolded AI tutoring systems can improve some math-learning outcomes under supervised conditions. The single most important caveat is external validity: most positive evidence comes from broader AI tutoring, generative-AI, or secondary/higher-education studies rather than ChatGPT-specific trials with middle school mathematics students [5] [9] [34].

## Defining the intervention and the scope of the evidence

The intervention in this brief is ChatGPT used as an educational support tool for middle school mathematics learning: conversational help with explanations, hints, worked steps, feedback, problem solving, practice, or tutoring-like support. The difficulty is that the current literature rarely isolates that intervention cleanly. Reviews of ChatGPT in mathematics education describe uses including generating explanations, solving problems, offering personalized guidance, creating tasks, and supporting feedback, but they also note that many studies are descriptive, conceptual, or conducted outside middle school settings [16]. A broader review of AI in mathematics education similarly reports that studies often bundle multiple AI functions together, making it difficult to identify effects attributable specifically to ChatGPT as opposed to other generative or adaptive systems [12].

This definitional ambiguity matters because “ChatGPT in math education” may refer to very different instructional models. In some studies and reviews, the tool is used directly by students for conversational tutoring; in others, it is used by teachers to generate materials, hints, or feedback; in still others, it is treated as an example of generative AI more broadly rather than a distinct intervention [3] [36] [52]. Reviews of virtual tutoring systems and GPT-based tutoring likewise describe a continuum from structured tutoring systems with limited response space to open-ended large-language-model chatbots, with different implications for reliability and control [23] [24].

The outcome domain is similarly heterogeneous. In mathematics education reviews, “achievement” can include correct answers on immediate posttests, procedural performance on item sets, conceptual understanding, misconception repair, retention, transfer, quality of explanations, or engagement proxies such as time on task and lessons completed [3] [29]. The focal brief prioritizes math achievement, problem-solving accuracy, conceptual understanding, procedural fluency, and transfer; however, many available middle-school-adjacent studies report only process outcomes or perceptions, not validated math achievement gains [47] [16].

Middle school populations are included in many reviews, but usually as one subgroup within broader K-12 or multi-level syntheses. Reviews of AI-driven ITS in K-12, digital learning, intelligent learning systems, and AI-enabled personalized STEM education all include middle school populations, yet their conclusions are typically pooled across grade spans rather than grade 6–8 specifically [1] [2] [15] [4]. As a result, the literature is better at answering whether AI-supported learning can help school-aged learners in mathematics than whether ChatGPT helps middle-schoolers in particular.

## What the broader evidence says about AI, generative AI, and mathematics learning

The broadest evidence comes from meta-analyses and systematic reviews rather than direct ChatGPT trials. A living meta-analysis on generative AI and mathematics learning concludes that generative-AI-based interventions can improve mathematics learning, but that effects are heterogeneous and shaped by contextual and design factors; the summary supplied does not report a middle-school-only or ChatGPT-only pooled effect [5]. A separate meta-analysis on whether GenAI improves mathematics learning outcomes also synthesizes 2023–2025 mathematics studies across school and undergraduate populations, indicating that the field has enough empirical work for pooled analysis but not yet enough specificity in the provided summary to support a middle-school-ChatGPT estimate [8].

A broader meta-analysis of generative AI in education reports that learning effects depend on conditions of use and are not uniformly positive [6]. A meta-analysis of ChatGPT’s influence on learning achievement likewise states that effects vary by learner age, outcome type, instructional approach, and research design [9]. Another ChatGPT meta-analysis extends this pattern to learning performance, learning perception, and higher-order thinking, again indicating variation rather than a simple average benefit [10]. Because the available summaries do not provide mathematics-by-middle-school effect sizes, these studies support only a cautious inference: average effects in the broader literature do not automatically transport to middle school math.

Reviews focused on AI in mathematics education are somewhat more proximal. A systematic literature review of AI interventions in mathematics education finds a range of AI-based supports in mathematics classrooms and examines their impact on learning outcomes, but the summary indicates breadth rather than a narrow focus on ChatGPT or middle school alone [3]. A systematic review of current practices and future directions in AI in mathematics education emphasizes practical use cases and market-ready tools, suggesting that the field has moved beyond proof-of-concept systems, but it does not establish ChatGPT-specific causal effects for middle school mathematics achievement [12]. A review of recent developments in digital technology in mathematics education similarly maps technology trends rather than estimating focal treatment effects [29].

The more general K-12 AI tutoring and digital-learning syntheses are useful for identifying plausible mechanisms and baseline expectations. A systematic review of AI-driven ITS in K-12 asks what effects ITSs have on learning and performance and what designs are used to evaluate them [1]. An evidence review on digital technology in schools concludes that the central question is not whether technology helps in general, but which kinds of technology use work, under what conditions, and in which subjects [2]. A meta-analysis of AI-enabled personalized STEM education also frames effectiveness as contingent on implementation conditions rather than inherent to the technology itself [4]. These sources are relevant because ChatGPT in middle school mathematics would normally be deployed as a personalized digital support, not as a stand-alone curricular replacement.

## What counts as the baseline or counterfactual

The source pool provides better information on common comparators than on ChatGPT itself. In broader digital-learning and tutoring research, typical counterfactuals include standard teacher-led instruction, worksheet or textbook practice, no-added-support control conditions, static digital hints, software-only environments, earlier cohorts before an intervention, or alternative tutoring conditions [2] [1] [24].

The clearest mathematics-specific example is the UK classroom RCT of LearnLM. In that study, the comparator conditions were static pre-written hints and human tutors. In Year 9–10 mathematics (RCT, n=165), interactive tutoring improved immediate remediation and misconception resolution relative to static hints [34]. For transfer, students using static hints answered 56.2% of the next unit’s first questions correctly, compared with 66.2% for LearnLM and 60.7% for human tutors [34]. This makes the counterfactual concrete: a generative-AI tutor was not tested against “nothing,” but against both low-cost hinting and human support.

A middle-school example comes from the three-study hybrid human-AI tutoring QED. There, the counterfactuals were software-only tutoring or pre-treatment periods before tutoring was added. Across the three sites, the intervention improved process metrics relative to those baselines: more time spent, more lessons passed, and greater workspace productivity [47]. This study is informative for likely real-world comparisons in middle school, where schools may choose between software-only remediation and a hybrid human-plus-AI model. However, because the study reports process indicators rather than standardized posttest effects, it cannot answer the brief’s central achievement question.

Outside generative AI, related studies clarify common instructional baselines in mathematics learning. A QED on more formative assessment in tertiary mathematics compared a cohort receiving more formative supports against an earlier cohort with more summative assessment, reporting a composite mathematics performance effect of 0.475 and a Task 7 effect of 0.70 on structured reasoning [46]. An RCT with fourth graders on explanatory adaptive feedback compared explanatory feedback, corrective feedback, and active control conditions in a fractions game [33]. A small RCT in algebra compared videogame-style points feedback against feedback without points in middle and high school students, reporting higher mixture-problem test scores for the points condition, though no exact effect size is given in the summary [27]. These are not ChatGPT studies, but they show the kinds of non-AI comparisons that matter: feedback type, motivational framing, and formative support all affect mathematics outcomes and may confound apparent AI effects if not controlled.

## Direct evidence on ChatGPT for middle school mathematics achievement

Direct evidence remains the central weakness of the literature. No study in the source pool is a randomized controlled trial of ChatGPT used by middle school students for mathematics achievement, compared with standard instruction or another clear comparator, with validated procedural, conceptual, or transfer outcomes. Reviews that specifically discuss ChatGPT in mathematics education acknowledge a rapidly expanding literature but are dominated by conceptual papers, higher-education studies, teacher-oriented analyses, and exploratory use cases rather than robust middle-school achievement experiments [16] [52].

The available ChatGPT-focused systematic reviews are therefore more useful for mapping use cases and concerns than for estimating impact. The SWOT-oriented review of ChatGPT in teaching and learning emphasizes strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats, implying that the literature is still centered on affordances and risks rather than stable outcome estimates [36]. A scoping meta-review of AI use in educational contexts likewise suggests that AI-in-education evidence is expanding faster than high-quality synthesis can stabilize, particularly for specific tools and populations [30].

Because of this gap, the evidence cannot fully answer whether ChatGPT improves middle school mathematics achievement relative to standard instruction. The most defensible conclusion is narrower: the literature has not yet produced high-quality direct evidence on the focal intervention-population-outcome combination. That is a substantive finding, not merely an absence of search success, because multiple broad reviews of AI in mathematics and ChatGPT in education still do not surface such trials in their summaries [3] [12] [16].

## Adjacent causal evidence from mathematics tutoring and generative-AI support

### Secondary mathematics RCT evidence

The strongest adjacent causal evidence is the exploratory RCT of LearnLM in UK classrooms. This study evaluated a generative-AI tutor in secondary mathematics under supervised real-classroom conditions (RCT, n=165 Year 9–10 students across five schools) [34]. The intervention was interactive tutoring, compared with static pre-written hints and human tutors. On immediate mistake remediation, human tutors led to 91.2% correct on the second attempt versus 81.7% for LearnLM and 65.7% for static hints [34]. On transfer, both interactive tutoring conditions outperformed static hints: static hints produced 56.2% correct on the next unit’s first question, LearnLM 66.2%, and human tutors 60.7% [34]. The study also reported that students who received supervised LearnLM tutoring were 5.5 percentage points more likely than those with human tutors to answer the next unit’s first question correctly, but with 95% CI [-0.014, +0.124], so this specific advantage over human tutoring was not statistically secure from the summary provided [34].

This RCT matters for the present brief because it isolates an LLM-based tutoring model in mathematics and includes meaningful comparators. It suggests that interactive conversational support can outperform static hints on misconception repair and near transfer, especially when supervised. Yet the external-validity boundary is important: Year 9–10 is older than middle school in the brief, LearnLM is not ChatGPT, and the conditions included tutor supervision [34].

### Middle-school QED evidence on hybrid human-AI tutoring

A more age-relevant but less causal study is the three-study quasi-experimental investigation of hybrid human-AI tutoring in middle school. This paper examined whether combining human tutoring with AI-based math software improved engagement and early achievement-related indicators in mostly low-income middle-school settings [47]. Site 1 reported a significant increase in time spent in the software, β=0.202, 95% CI [0.057, 0.347], with n=125 [47]. Site 2 reported more lessons passed per week, β=0.244, 95% CI [0.106, 0.381], with n=385 [47]. Site 3 reported +0.36 workspaces per hour, 95% CI [0.02, 0.70], with n=75 [47].

These results show that middle-school learners can engage more intensely with mathematics software when human support is layered onto AI-enabled systems. The study also argues that hybrid tutoring can improve efficiency without the full costs of conventional high-dosage tutoring [47]. But the outcome domain is process-based rather than direct achievement, and the intervention is not ChatGPT. It therefore informs implementation plausibility more than focal efficacy.

### Other RCT analogues on feedback and tutoring features

Other experimental studies in the pool are useful for mechanism inference but not direct generalization. In a randomized study of 288 fourth graders, explanatory adaptive feedback in a fractions game improved near-transfer fraction knowledge from pretest to posttest and retention in both experimental conditions, whereas the active control did not show the same pattern [33]. The study also found mixed motivational effects: trait math motivation decreased in the control and corrective-feedback conditions, while state math self-concept was lower in the experimental game conditions than in active control [33]. This suggests that richer feedback can help mathematics learning without uniformly improving affect.

A small RCT of videogame-style points feedback with middle and high school students (RCT, n=28) found that students receiving points for correct algebra answers scored higher on a 20-item mixture-problems test than students receiving answer feedback alone [27]. Because the summary provides no exact effect size, the finding is suggestive rather than precise. An undergraduate mathematics ITS RCT (n=120) found that multiple-choice practice yielded higher adjusted posttest scores than free-response practice, with adjusted posttest mean difference intervals [8.32, 10.10] versus [6.99, 8.89], while an error-reflection feature did not significantly improve math learning, F(1,115)=0.07, p=0.79 [18]. These studies underscore that design features matter and that seemingly desirable supports like reflection prompts may not produce gains.

## Mechanisms and implementation features that likely shape outcomes

The strongest consistent theme across reviews is that outcomes depend more on instructional design than on AI branding. Reviews of ITS, intelligent learning systems, and tutoring systems converge on the importance of structured feedback, adaptivity, dialogue management, and alignment between support and task demands [1] [15] [24]. Classic review work on AutoTutor similarly emphasizes that natural-language tutoring can be effective when it prompts learners, manages dialogue around misconceptions, and supports explanation rather than simply giving answers [41]. This is directly relevant to ChatGPT: if middle school students use it as an answer engine, expected learning effects may differ from uses that enforce scaffolded dialogue.

Prompting and feedback structure are especially salient. Reviews of ChatGPT in mathematics and education commonly note that output quality and educational usefulness depend on prompt specificity, constraints, and iterative refinement [16] [36]. Broader work on personalized learning terminology also warns that personalization is often loosely defined, making implementation details easy to overlook [39]. In practice, this means that “using ChatGPT” may range from unrestricted asking for final answers to carefully scaffolded Socratic prompting with verification steps. The evidence base rarely compares these variants directly in middle school mathematics.

Teacher mediation appears repeatedly as a likely moderator. The digital-technology evidence review argues that technology works best when integrated into effective teaching, not when substituted for it [2]. Reviews on teacher and student agency make a related point: AI systems can support decision-making, but they can also narrow agency if teachers and students are reduced to following machine suggestions [40]. In the secondary-math LearnLM RCT, the intervention was explicitly supervised, and tutors reported that the tool made support feel more fluid and efficient; 82.4% of tutors selected “supporting multiple students at the same time” as its most useful feature, based on a survey of n=17 tutors [34]. That finding suggests that one practical route for middle school may be teacher- or tutor-mediated orchestration rather than fully independent student use.

Task type also matters. Reviews of intelligent tutoring for word-problem solving indicate that autonomous systems may be more or less effective depending on the structure of the mathematical domain and the extent to which the system can diagnose strategy or misconception patterns [11]. Broader surveys of LLMs for mathematical reasoning emphasize that open-ended mathematical reasoning remains challenging and error-prone for language models, especially on conceptually demanding or multi-step tasks [50] [45]. Those are not student outcome studies, so they cannot prove learning harms, but they do indicate why unguided use in middle school mathematics may be risky.

Dialogue quality may be another mechanism. A scoping review on classroom dialogue and digital technologies suggests that digital tools can support dialogue but also create orchestration challenges [35]. Because mathematics learning often depends on explaining, justifying, and revising reasoning, a chatbot’s value may be greatest when it supports mathematical discourse rather than merely returns solutions. However, the source pool does not contain direct middle-school ChatGPT studies measuring discourse quality in mathematics.

## Variation in outcomes by population, context, and outcome type

### By age and developmental level

Meta-analyses of ChatGPT and generative AI report that effects vary by learner age [9] [6]. This is highly relevant because middle school students are younger and typically less self-regulated than undergraduate populations that dominate many early ChatGPT studies [13] [22]. Without direct middle-school trials, it is not possible to assume that findings from older students will transfer.

A related meta-analysis on AI and learner autonomy focuses on self-regulated and self-directed learning, primarily in higher education, indicating that AI may support regulation-related processes but also raising the issue that benefits depend on learners’ capacity to use such support productively [19]. This matters because younger adolescents may be more vulnerable to overreliance or less able to evaluate incorrect AI explanations.

### By outcome type

Outcome type is a major source of heterogeneity. The secondary LearnLM RCT showed stronger evidence on immediate remediation and near transfer than on superiority to human tutoring [34]. The middle-school hybrid QED showed process and engagement gains, not validated achievement gains [47]. The adaptive-feedback fractions RCT showed learning gains alongside mixed self-concept effects [33]. Reviews of ChatGPT in education similarly distinguish between learning performance, perceptions, and higher-order thinking rather than treating them as interchangeable [10]. Therefore, positive engagement or usability findings should not be read as proof of mathematics achievement improvement.

### By prior achievement and subgroup

Direct subgroup evidence for middle school mathematics is largely absent. No source in the pool reports ChatGPT-specific middle-school effects by grade 6 versus 7 versus 8, by multilingual status, disability, gender, or socioeconomic status. The nearest analogue is an adult RCT on GPT-based reading supports showing differential effects by baseline ability: a summary tool improved lower-performing participants’ quiz accuracy, d=0.45, n=71, but worsened higher-performing participants’ accuracy, d=0.83, n=124, under one subgroup split [44]. This is not a math or middle-school study, so it cannot be generalized directly, but it does show that GPT-based educational tools can help some learners while hindering others.

Another adjacent study is the cluster RCT on AI-supported e-mentoring among socioeconomically disadvantaged high-school students (RCT, n=168). The intervention improved self-regulated learning, with adjusted effects β=0.41 at T1 and β=0.36 at T2, and also improved secondary academic-performance indicators [21]. Again, this is not middle-school math, but it suggests that AI-mediated support may interact with student disadvantage and self-regulation needs.

## Risks, tradeoffs, and reasons for caution

Reviews of ChatGPT in mathematics and education consistently describe risks of inaccuracy, hallucinated or shallow explanations, overreliance, reduced productive struggle, and academic-integrity concerns [16] [36] [52]. These concerns are especially salient in middle school mathematics, where students are still building foundational concepts and may lack the expertise to detect subtle errors. However, the evidence base in the source pool rarely measures these harms directly in grades 6–8 math settings.

Adjacent empirical studies provide reasons the risks are plausible. In programming, an RCT of GPT-generated feedback found that students performed better on tasks where GPT hints were available, but on harder tasks where hints were removed, the control group outperformed the experimental group during the first six attempts, suggesting possible dependence or limited transfer under some conditions [26]. A QED on AI code generators for novice learners similarly raises concerns that immediate task support may not translate into deeper learning [42]. These are not mathematics studies, but they illustrate a mechanism highly relevant to ChatGPT in math: answer-rich support can improve short-run task success while weakening independent performance on harder or later tasks.

Other evidence suggests that more support is not always better. In the undergraduate mathematics ITS RCT, an error-reflection feature did not improve learning, despite its intuitive appeal [18]. In the fractions adaptive-feedback RCT, richer feedback improved learning but lowered state math self-concept relative to active control [33]. These patterns caution against assuming that conversational AI will improve both achievement and motivation simultaneously.

Equity and access remain under-studied. Reviews of inclusive education through technology and AI use in education suggest that access conditions, tool design, and learner diversity matter, but they do not provide focused evidence on whether ChatGPT narrows or widens middle-school mathematics gaps [49] [30]. The field also lacks strong evidence on home versus classroom use, parental mediation, and device/connectivity barriers in this specific context.

## What the evidence implies for middle school mathematics practice now

For schools considering ChatGPT as a support for middle school mathematics, the evidence supports a restrained implementation logic rather than strong efficacy claims. First, ChatGPT should not be treated as a proven replacement for teacher-led math instruction, human tutoring, or established math software in grades 6–8, because direct comparative evidence is not yet available [16] [3]. Second, if used at all, the strongest adjacent evidence favors supervised, tutoring-like, scaffolded use over unguided answer retrieval [34] [41] [2].

Third, implementation should emphasize constrained prompting, verification, and teacher oversight. Reviews of tutoring systems, ITS, and ChatGPT all suggest that support is more educationally defensible when it elicits reasoning, diagnoses errors, and withholds final answers until students engage with the problem [1] [24] [16]. Fourth, schools should evaluate outcomes beyond simple correctness, including transfer, independence after support removal, and possible overreliance. This recommendation follows directly from the mixed adjacent evidence on short-run success versus later independent performance [26] [34].

Finally, the most evidence-aligned near-term use case may be teacher- or tutor-mediated augmentation rather than direct unrestricted student use. The hybrid human-AI middle-school QED and the supervised secondary-math RCT both point in that direction [47] [34].

### Synthesis and Implications

The evidence base supports four main conclusions. First, the direct answer to the focal brief is unresolved: there is not yet sufficient causal evidence that ChatGPT itself improves middle school mathematics achievement relative to standard instruction or other common supports [16] [12]. Second, adjacent evidence from generative-AI tutoring, ITS, and digital mathematics support suggests that AI can improve some mathematics outcomes, especially when it provides interactive, adaptive, and supervised support rather than static hints or unguided access [5] [1] [34]. Third, implementation design is likely decisive: prompt structure, feedback granularity, teacher mediation, and guardrails shape whether the technology supports reasoning or merely supplies answers [41] [2] [36]. Fourth, outcome heterogeneity is real. Some studies show gains in remediation, transfer, engagement, or process measures; others show null effects on desired features or possible dependence risks when support is removed [47] [18] [26].

For practitioners, the implication is not to reject ChatGPT categorically, but to avoid overclaiming. If schools pilot conversational AI in middle school mathematics, the most defensible approach is limited, supervised use for hints, explanation prompts, misconception diagnosis, and checked feedback within a broader teacher-led structure [34] [2]. Evaluation should prioritize validated math outcomes, especially conceptual understanding and transfer, and compare ChatGPT-supported conditions against realistic alternatives such as teacher-created hints, static digital tools, and human tutoring. Until such evidence exists, claims of achievement gains should remain tentative.

### Limitations and Research Gaps

The largest limitation is the absence of direct high-quality studies matching the brief exactly. No RCT or strong QED in the source pool tests ChatGPT specifically with middle school mathematics students against standard instruction on validated achievement outcomes. Much of the literature is broader than the target intervention, broader than the target population, or focused on perceptions, design, or feasibility rather than learning [9] [16] [30].

A second limitation is heterogeneity. Meta-analyses of ChatGPT, generative AI, and AI-enabled learning synthesize diverse subjects, grade levels, outcomes, and comparator conditions, so their pooled conclusions are only partially transportable to middle school mathematics [6] [10] [4]. A third limitation is outcome validity: several relevant middle-school studies report process indicators like time on task, lessons passed, or engagement rather than standardized math learning gains [47]. A fourth limitation is weak subgroup coverage. The literature does not adequately address grade-specific effects within middle school, prior achievement, multilingual learners, students with disabilities or math learning difficulties, gender, or socioeconomic heterogeneity [3] [12].

Research priorities are clear. The field needs ChatGPT-specific middle-school mathematics RCTs and strong QEDs with transparent comparator conditions: standard instruction, teacher-authored hints, worksheets, non-AI digital tools, human tutoring, and no-added-support baselines. These studies should separate procedural fluency, conceptual understanding, retention, and transfer; measure harms such as copying, overreliance, and exposure to incorrect explanations; and report subgroup effects by grade, prior achievement, and learner need. Implementation studies should also compare guided versus unguided use, teacher-mediated versus student-only use, and scaffold-first versus answer-first prompting. Without that evidence, the literature can justify cautious experimentation, but not confident claims that ChatGPT improves middle school mathematics achievement.
